# THEME 7
## industrial-revolution | The Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the second half of the eighteenth century and transformed how goods were made. It started in cotton textiles, where inventions such as James Hargreaves's spinning jenny (1760s), Richard Arkwright's water frame and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule mechanised spinning, and Edmund Cartwright's power loom mechanised weaving. James Watt's improved steam engine, patented in 1769, provided reliable power for factories, mines and, later, railways and steamships. George Stephenson's locomotives helped launch the railway age; the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830.

Historians explain Britain's lead through several factors: abundant coal and iron ore, an agricultural revolution that raised food output and released labour, capital from trade and colonies, a large merchant class, protected property rights, a culture of practical invention, and access to markets and raw materials through its empire.

The social effects were profound. Cities such as Manchester grew rapidly, often with crowded, unsanitary housing. Factory work brought long hours, child labour and harsh discipline, prompting reforms such as the Factory Acts and the rise of trade unions. New ideas responded to these conditions, from liberal economics (Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations*, 1776) to socialism; Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published the *Communist Manifesto* in 1848.

Industrialisation spread to Belgium, France, Germany, the United States and Japan in the nineteenth century, in what is sometimes called a second industrial revolution based on steel, chemicals, electricity and oil. For colonies such as India, it meant markets for manufactured goods and pressure to supply raw materials, contributing to deindustrialisation.

- The Industrial Revolution began in Britain's textile industry in the late eighteenth century.
- Coal, capital, markets, colonies and invention explain Britain's lead.
- It produced urbanisation, new social classes and new ideologies, and reshaped colonial economies.
W: The Industrial Revolution is a foundation topic for world history and helps explain colonialism and modern ideologies.

## american-revolution | The American Revolution

In the mid-eighteenth century, Britain had thirteen colonies along the Atlantic coast of North America. After the costly Seven Years' War (1756–63), Britain tried to raise revenue from the colonies through new taxes, including the Stamp Act of 1765 and the Townshend duties. Colonists objected that they had no representatives in the British Parliament, summed up in the slogan "No taxation without representation". The Boston Tea Party of 1773, in which colonists dumped British tea into Boston harbour, led to punitive laws and escalating conflict.

Fighting began at Lexington and Concord in 1775. On 4 July 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson. It declared that all men are created equal and have unalienable rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and that governments derive their powers from the consent of the governed. With crucial help from France, the colonial army under George Washington won the war; the British surrendered at Yorktown in 1781, and the Treaty of Paris of 1783 recognised American independence.

The Constitution of the United States, drafted in Philadelphia in 1787, created a federal republic with a separation of powers between the legislature, executive and judiciary, and checks and balances among them. The Bill of Rights, the first ten amendments, was added in 1791.

The revolution had limits: slavery continued, women and Native Americans were excluded, and voting was restricted to property-owning men. But its ideas of popular sovereignty, written constitutions and rights influenced the French Revolution and, later, India's Constitution, which borrowed the idea of a written bill of rights, judicial review and an independent judiciary.

- Taxation without representation led to conflict between Britain and its colonies.
- The Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776) asserted natural rights and government by consent.
- The US Constitution (1787) introduced federalism, separation of powers and judicial review.

## french-revolution | The French Revolution

In 1789 France was an absolute monarchy under Louis XVI, deeply in debt partly because of its support for the American Revolution. Society was divided into three estates: the clergy, the nobility and everyone else. The third estate, the vast majority, bore most of the tax burden while the first two enjoyed privileges.

To solve the financial crisis, the king summoned the Estates-General in May 1789. The third estate declared itself the National Assembly and took the Tennis Court Oath not to disband until France had a constitution. On 14 July 1789 crowds stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison in Paris, now celebrated as France's national day. In August, the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed liberty, equality before the law and popular sovereignty.

The revolution grew more radical. France became a republic in 1792, and Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. Under the Jacobins, led by Maximilien Robespierre, the Reign of Terror (1793–94) saw thousands executed as enemies of the revolution. After Robespierre's fall, a more conservative Directory governed until Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in 1799. Napoleon crowned himself emperor in 1804, spread revolutionary legal reforms through the Napoleonic Code, and conquered much of Europe before his final defeat at Waterloo in 1815.

The revolution's slogan, "Liberty, equality, fraternity", spread across the world. It established the idea that sovereignty belongs to the nation, encouraged nationalism and inspired later democratic movements. The words liberty, equality and fraternity appear in the Preamble of India's Constitution.

- France's financial crisis and unequal estates system led to revolution in 1789.
- The storming of the Bastille and the Declaration of the Rights of Man defined the revolution's ideals.
- The revolution turned radical, then gave way to Napoleon, but its ideas spread worldwide.
W: The French Revolution is central to world history questions and to the origins of the Preamble's ideals.

## nationalism-unification-italy-germany | Nationalism and the unification of Italy and Germany

After Napoleon's defeat, the Congress of Vienna (1814–15), led by the Austrian statesman Metternich, tried to restore old monarchies and suppress liberal and nationalist movements. But nationalism, the idea that people sharing a language, culture and history should form their own state, spread across Europe. Revolutions broke out in many countries in 1848, and though most failed, they kept nationalist hopes alive.

Italy was divided into several states, some under Austrian control. Its unification, the Risorgimento, combined three figures. Giuseppe Mazzini, through his Young Italy movement, gave it an idealistic, republican vision. Count Camillo di Cavour, prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia, used diplomacy and alliance with France to defeat Austria. Giuseppe Garibaldi led his volunteer Red Shirts to conquer Sicily and Naples in 1860, then handed them to Piedmont's king. Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed King of Italy in 1861. Venice was added in 1866 and Rome in 1870.

Germany was a loose confederation of 39 states, dominated by Austria and Prussia. Otto von Bismarck, appointed Prussian minister-president in 1862, unified Germany through "blood and iron". Prussia won three short wars: against Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71). In January 1871 the German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, with the Prussian king as Kaiser Wilhelm I.

German unification changed the European balance of power. France's loss of Alsace-Lorraine left lasting bitterness, and rivalry among the great powers grew, contributing to the alliances that led to the First World War.

- The Congress of Vienna tried to suppress nationalism, but the 1848 revolutions kept it alive.
- Mazzini, Cavour and Garibaldi unified Italy by 1861, completed in 1870.
- Bismarck unified Germany through three wars, culminating in the German Empire in 1871.
W: The unification of Italy and Germany is a standard world history question, often compared with Indian nationalism.

## imperialism-scramble-for-africa | Imperialism and the scramble for Africa

Between about 1870 and 1914, European powers expanded their empires dramatically, a period often called the age of new imperialism. Industrial economies needed raw materials and markets, investors sought profitable outlets, and nationalism made colonies symbols of prestige. Ideas of racial superiority and a "civilising mission" provided justification. Technologies such as steamships, the telegraph, quinine against malaria and the machine gun made conquest easier.

Africa saw the most rapid partition. In 1870, Europeans controlled about a tenth of the continent, mostly along the coasts. At the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, convened by Bismarck, European powers set rules for claiming African territory without consulting any African. By 1914, only Ethiopia, which defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remained independent. Borders were drawn with little regard for ethnic, linguistic or economic realities, creating problems that persist. King Leopold II of Belgium ruled the Congo Free State as personal property, and forced labour there caused millions of deaths.

Asia was also divided. Britain controlled India, Burma and Malaya; France took Indochina; the Dutch held Indonesia. China was forced to open treaty ports after the Opium Wars (1839–42 and 1856–60) and divided into spheres of influence. Japan modernised rapidly after the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and became an imperial power itself, defeating China in 1895 and Russia in 1905.

Imperialism left deep legacies: arbitrary borders, economies built around exporting raw materials, and the anti-colonial nationalism that would produce dozens of new nations after 1945.

- Economic interests, nationalism and racist ideology drove new imperialism after 1870.
- The Berlin Conference (1884–85) set rules for dividing Africa; only Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent.
- China was weakened by the Opium Wars, while Japan became an imperial power after 1868.
W: Imperialism links world history to India's own colonial experience and to modern global inequality.

## first-world-war | The First World War

The First World War (1914–18) grew from rivalries among the great powers. Europe was divided into two alliances: the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, and the Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia. Imperial competition, a naval arms race between Britain and Germany, militarism and nationalism, especially in the Balkans, raised tensions. The spark came on 28 June 1914, when Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary was assassinated in Sarajevo by a Bosnian Serb nationalist. Within weeks, the alliance system drew all the powers into war.

On the western front, the war became a stalemate of trench warfare, with battles such as the Somme and Verdun killing hundreds of thousands for little territorial gain. New weapons included machine guns, poison gas, tanks, submarines and aircraft. The Ottoman Empire joined Germany; Italy switched sides to join the Entente. Russia withdrew in 1918 after the Bolshevik revolution. The United States entered the war in 1917, partly because of German submarine warfare, tipping the balance. Germany signed an armistice on 11 November 1918.

About 1.3 million Indian soldiers served, in France, Mesopotamia, East Africa, Gallipoli and Palestine; more than 74,000 died. India Gate in Delhi commemorates them. Indians expected political concessions in return, and disappointment fuelled the national movement.

The Treaty of Versailles (1919) blamed Germany for the war, imposed heavy reparations, reduced its army and took away its colonies. The Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian empires collapsed. The League of Nations was created to keep the peace, but the United States never joined. Many historians see the harsh peace as one cause of the Second World War.

- Alliances, imperialism, militarism and Balkan nationalism led to war in 1914.
- Around 1.3 million Indian soldiers served, and more than 74,000 died.
- The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany and created the League of Nations.

## russian-revolution | The Russian Revolution

In 1917 Russia was an autocracy under Tsar Nicholas II, with a mostly peasant population, a small but growing industrial working class, and a government discredited by defeat in the war with Japan (1904–05) and catastrophic losses in the First World War. Food shortages and inflation added to the anger.

In February 1917 (March by the Western calendar), strikes and mutinies in Petrograd forced the Tsar to abdicate. A Provisional Government took over, but shared power with soviets, councils of workers and soldiers. It kept Russia in the war and delayed land reform, losing support. In October 1917 (November by the Western calendar), the Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized power in Petrograd. Their slogan was "Peace, land and bread".

The Bolsheviks made peace with Germany in the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918), giving up large territories. They nationalised industry and banks and redistributed land. A civil war (1918–21) between the Red Army, organised by Leon Trotsky, and the anti-Bolshevik Whites, supported by foreign powers, ended in Bolshevik victory. The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formed in 1922. Lenin's New Economic Policy (1921) allowed limited private trade to revive the economy.

After Lenin's death in 1924, Joseph Stalin took power. He launched five-year plans for rapid industrialisation from 1928 and forced the collectivisation of agriculture, which caused famine, including in Ukraine. His purges of the 1930s killed or imprisoned millions. The revolution inspired communist and anti-colonial movements worldwide, and India's early planners were influenced by the Soviet model of five-year plans, though India remained a democracy.

- The February 1917 revolution ended the Tsar's rule; the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917.
- Lenin's government withdrew from the war, nationalised industry and won the civil war; the USSR formed in 1922.
- Stalin's five-year plans and collectivisation transformed the economy at a huge human cost.
W: The Russian Revolution is a core world history topic and helps explain India's planning model and the Cold War.

## interwar-depression-fascism | The interwar years: the Great Depression and fascism

The 1920s began with recovery in many countries, but ended in disaster. In October 1929, the New York stock market crashed. Banks failed, credit collapsed and world trade shrank by about two-thirds over the following years. Unemployment in the United States reached about a quarter of the workforce. Countries raised tariffs to protect their industries, which deepened the slump. Prices of agricultural goods collapsed, hurting farmers in colonies such as India.

Governments responded in different ways. In the United States, President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal used public works, social security and banking regulation. The economist John Maynard Keynes argued that governments should increase spending during depressions, ideas that shaped economic policy for decades.

Elsewhere, the crisis strengthened authoritarian movements. Fascism, which glorified the nation and the state, rejected democracy and liberalism, and used violence against opponents, first took power in Italy, where Benito Mussolini became prime minister in 1922. In Germany, the Nazi Party of Adolf Hitler exploited resentment over the Treaty of Versailles, economic misery and fear of communism. Hitler became chancellor in January 1933 and quickly established a dictatorship. The Nazis persecuted Jews through laws such as the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, rearmed Germany in violation of Versailles, and remilitarised the Rhineland in 1936. Japan's militarist government invaded Manchuria in 1931.

The League of Nations failed to stop aggression: it could not prevent Italy's conquest of Ethiopia in 1935–36 or Japan's expansion in China. Britain and France followed a policy of appeasement, culminating in the Munich Agreement of 1938, which allowed Germany to annex the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia.

- The 1929 crash led to the Great Depression, mass unemployment and collapsing trade.
- Fascism rose in Italy (1922) and Nazism in Germany (1933), exploiting economic crisis and resentment.
- The League of Nations and appeasement failed to stop aggression.
W: The Depression and the rise of fascism help explain the Second World War and modern debates on economic policy and democracy.

## second-world-war | The Second World War

The Second World War began on 1 September 1939, when Germany invaded Poland; Britain and France declared war two days later. Germany conquered much of Europe by 1940, including France, using fast-moving "blitzkrieg" tactics. Britain held out under Winston Churchill, winning the air Battle of Britain. In June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union. On 7 December 1941, Japan attacked the US naval base at Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States into the war.

The tide turned in 1942–43. The Soviet victory at Stalingrad (1942–43) was a turning point in Europe, and the US victory at Midway (1942) in the Pacific. Allied forces landed in Normandy on 6 June 1944 (D-Day). Germany surrendered on 8 May 1945. The United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August), and Japan surrendered on 15 August, formally signing on 2 September 1945.

The war killed an estimated 70 to 85 million people, the majority of them civilians. The Nazis murdered about six million Jews in the Holocaust, along with millions of others. Over 2.5 million Indians served in the British Indian Army, the largest volunteer army in history, fighting in North Africa, Italy and South-East Asia; the battles of Imphal and Kohima in 1944 stopped the Japanese advance into India. The Bengal famine of 1943 killed around three million people.

The war's consequences shaped the modern world. The United Nations was founded in 1945. The United States and the Soviet Union emerged as superpowers. European empires were weakened, speeding decolonisation, including India's independence. The Nuremberg and Tokyo trials established the principle of individual responsibility for war crimes.

- The war began with Germany's invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 and ended in 1945.
- Stalingrad and Midway were turning points; atomic bombs were used on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
- The war led to the UN, the superpower rivalry and the end of European empires.
W: The Second World War's course and consequences, and India's part in it, are frequent exam topics.

## cold-war-decolonisation | The Cold War and decolonisation

The Cold War (1947–1991) was a rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union and their allies, fought through ideology, arms races, propaganda and proxy wars rather than direct conflict. The US policy of containment, expressed in the Truman Doctrine (1947) and the Marshall Plan for European recovery, aimed to stop the spread of communism. Military alliances formed: NATO in 1949 and the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Germany was divided, and the Berlin Wall was built in 1961.

The Cold War turned hot in Asia. The Korean War (1950–53) ended in stalemate, and the Vietnam War ended with the fall of Saigon in 1975. The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 brought the world closest to nuclear war. Both superpowers built vast nuclear arsenals, later limited by treaties such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), which India has not signed, viewing it as discriminatory.

Decolonisation happened alongside. India and Pakistan became independent in 1947, Indonesia in 1949, Ghana in 1957 and most of Africa by the 1960s. The year 1960 alone saw seventeen African countries become independent. Some decolonisation was peaceful; some, as in Algeria and Kenya, was violent. Many new states joined the Non-Aligned Movement to avoid choosing sides.

The Cold War ended as the Soviet economy stagnated. Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) loosened control. The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, Germany reunified in 1990, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. India, which had close ties with the Soviet Union, had to rethink its foreign and economic policy, contributing to the 1991 reforms and closer ties with the United States.

- The Cold War was a US-Soviet rivalry from 1947 to 1991, with alliances, arms races and proxy wars.
- Decolonisation created dozens of new states, many of which joined NAM.
- The fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and the Soviet collapse (1991) ended the Cold War and reshaped India's policies.
W: The Cold War and decolonisation are essential context for India's foreign policy and the NAM.
