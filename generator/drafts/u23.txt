# THEME 23
## isro-launch-vehicles | ISRO and its launch vehicles

The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) was founded in 1969 under the leadership of Vikram Sarabhai, who argued that space technology should serve national development: communication, weather forecasting and resource mapping. India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was launched on a Soviet rocket in 1975. The first successful Indian launch, of the SLV-3 carrying Rohini, came in 1980, under a project led by A. P. J. Abdul Kalam. ISRO's main launch centre is the Satish Dhawan Space Centre at Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh; a second spaceport for small rockets is being built at Kulasekarapattinam in Tamil Nadu.

The Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV), first flown successfully in 1994, became ISRO's workhorse. It launched Chandrayaan-1 (2008), the Mars Orbiter Mission (2013) and Aditya-L1 (2023), and in 2017 placed a record 104 satellites in orbit on a single flight. Its reliability was dented by two failures in eight months, both blamed on third-stage problems: PSLV-C61, carrying the EOS-09 earth observation satellite, in May 2025, and PSLV-C62, carrying a DRDO satellite and fifteen others, in January 2026.

The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) uses a cryogenic upper stage, which burns liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen. India developed this engine itself after the United States pressured Russia in the 1990s not to transfer the technology. The heavier Launch Vehicle Mark-3 (LVM3) carried Chandrayaan-2 and Chandrayaan-3 and launches commercial satellite batches, and a human-rated version will carry Gaganyaan. The Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV) is designed for quick, low-cost launches of small satellites; it failed on its first flight in 2022 but succeeded in 2023 and 2024.

ISRO is developing a reusable launch vehicle and a Next Generation Launch Vehicle for heavier payloads. NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), ISRO's commercial arm, sells launch services abroad.

- ISRO was founded in 1969 under Vikram Sarabhai; SLV-3 made the first Indian launch in 1980.
- PSLV is the workhorse but failed in May 2025 and January 2026; GSLV and LVM3 use indigenous cryogenic stages.
- SSLV serves small satellites; reusable and heavier rockets are in development.

## chandrayaan-aditya-missions | Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan and Aditya: India's science missions

India's planetary missions have achieved notable firsts at low cost. Chandrayaan-1, launched in 2008, carried an impactor and international instruments, including NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper, which helped confirm the presence of water molecules on the lunar surface. The Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan), launched in November 2013, entered Mars orbit in September 2014, making India the first Asian country to reach Mars and the first country to do so on its first attempt, at a cost of about Rs 450 crore. Chandrayaan-2 (2019) placed an orbiter around the Moon that is still working, but its Vikram lander crashed during the final descent.

Chandrayaan-3 succeeded where its predecessor failed. Its lander Vikram touched down near the Moon's south pole on 23 August 2023, making India the fourth country to soft-land on the Moon and the first to land near the south polar region. The landing site was named Shiv Shakti point, and 23 August is now observed as National Space Day. The Pragyan rover confirmed the presence of sulphur and other elements in the lunar soil. The south pole interests scientists because permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice, a potential resource for future missions.

Aditya-L1, India's first solar observatory, was launched in September 2023 and placed in a halo orbit around the first Sun-Earth Lagrange point (L1), about 1.5 million km from Earth, in January 2024. From there it observes the Sun continuously, studying the corona, solar wind and flares, which affect satellites and power grids on Earth. AstroSat (2015) was India's first dedicated space astronomy observatory, and XPoSat (2024) studies X-ray polarisation.

Approved future missions include Chandrayaan-4, a lunar sample-return mission; a joint lunar polar mission with Japan (LUPEX, also called Chandrayaan-5); and the Venus Orbiter Mission.

- Chandrayaan-1 helped confirm water on the Moon; Mangalyaan reached Mars on the first attempt in 2014.
- Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023, now National Space Day.
- Aditya-L1 studies the Sun from the L1 point; Chandrayaan-4 will return lunar samples.

## gaganyaan-space-station | Gaganyaan and India's human spaceflight plans

Gaganyaan is India's programme to send astronauts into space on an Indian rocket. Approved in 2018, it plans to carry a crew of up to three to a low Earth orbit of about 400 km for up to three days and bring them back safely to a splashdown in Indian waters. The crew module will be launched on a human-rated version of the LVM3 rocket, with a crew escape system that can pull the capsule away if the rocket fails. ISRO tested the escape system in the TV-D1 flight in October 2023.

Before the crewed flight, ISRO plans uncrewed missions. The first, G1, will carry a humanoid robot called Vyommitra to test life-support and safety systems. G1 has been repeatedly delayed. In 2026 ISRO completed tests such as an air-drop test of the crew module in April and began integrating Vyommitra, and in June 2026 the ISRO chairman said G1 was expected in the last quarter of 2026. The first crewed flight is expected after further uncrewed missions.

Four Indian Air Force test pilots were named as astronaut-designates in February 2024: Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair, Ajit Krishnan, Angad Pratap and Shubhanshu Shukla. In June-July 2025, Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla flew on Axiom Mission 4, a private mission to the International Space Station, becoming the first Indian to visit the ISS and the second Indian in space, 41 years after Rakesh Sharma flew on a Soviet mission in 1984. The experience provides training and data for Gaganyaan.

India's longer-term vision includes the Bharatiya Antariksh Station, a national space station, with its first module targeted for 2028 and the full station by 2035, and an Indian landing on the Moon by 2040. The SpaDeX mission in early 2025 demonstrated docking of two satellites in orbit, a key technology for building a space station, making India the fourth country to achieve docking.

- Gaganyaan will send up to three astronauts to about 400 km orbit; the uncrewed G1 flight is expected in late 2026.
- Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian on the ISS in 2025.
- India plans a space station (first module 2028) and a crewed Moon landing by 2040; SpaDeX demonstrated docking in 2025.
W: Gaganyaan, the space station plan and SpaDeX are high-priority current topics for Prelims and Mains.

## space-sector-reforms | Space sector reforms and private companies

For decades, India's space activities were carried out almost entirely by ISRO, with private companies acting only as suppliers of parts. Reforms since 2020 aim to open the sector to private players, to expand India's small share of the global space economy and free ISRO to focus on research and advanced missions.

The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre (IN-SPACe), set up in 2020, authorises and supervises private space activities and gives companies access to ISRO's facilities. NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), formed in 2019, is ISRO's commercial arm; it owns and operates satellites built for customers and markets launch services. The Indian Space Policy 2023 defined the roles of ISRO, IN-SPACe, NSIL and private companies, allowing private firms to undertake end-to-end activities, including building and launching rockets and satellites and operating ground stations. In 2024 the government eased foreign direct investment rules, allowing up to 74% FDI in satellite manufacturing and operations and up to 49% in launch vehicles through the automatic route, and up to 100% in components.

Indian start-ups have responded. Skyroot Aerospace launched Vikram-S, India's first privately built rocket, on a suborbital flight in November 2022. Agnikul Cosmos launched a rocket with a 3D-printed semi-cryogenic engine from its own launch pad at Sriharikota in May 2024. Companies such as Pixxel are building earth-imaging satellites. Private industry is also building PSLV rockets under a technology transfer arrangement with Hindustan Aeronautics and Larsen & Toubro.

India's space economy was estimated at about 8 billion US dollars, roughly 2% of the global space economy, and the government's target is 44 billion dollars by 2033. A national space activities law to provide legal certainty on licensing, liability and debris has been under discussion.

- IN-SPACe (2020) authorises private space activities; NSIL (2019) is ISRO's commercial arm.
- The Space Policy 2023 and 2024 FDI changes opened the sector to private and foreign investment.
- Skyroot and Agnikul have launched private rockets; the target is a 44 billion dollar space economy by 2033.

## satellites-navic-nisar | Satellites: NavIC, earth observation and NISAR

India operates a large fleet of satellites for communication, weather, earth observation and navigation. The Indian National Satellite System (INSAT) and GSAT series provide television, telecommunications and disaster warning. Earth observation satellites such as the Resourcesat, Cartosat, Oceansat and RISAT series map crops, forests, water, cities and coasts; radar satellites (RISAT) can see through clouds and at night, useful for floods and security. Weather satellites such as INSAT-3D and 3DR support the India Meteorological Department's forecasts.

NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation), earlier called the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System, is India's own satellite navigation system. Like the American GPS, it provides positioning and timing, but only over India and about 1,500 km around it. It reduces dependence on foreign systems, which could be denied in a conflict, as India experienced during the Kargil war in 1999. NavIC has struggled because several satellites' atomic clocks failed. New second-generation satellites are being launched: NVS-01 in 2023, and NVS-02 in January 2025 on ISRO's 100th launch from Sriharikota, though NVS-02 could not reach its intended orbit because of a valve problem. Smartphone makers have been encouraged to support NavIC.

NISAR, the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar, is the first joint earth observation satellite of the two agencies. Launched on 30 July 2025 on a GSLV rocket, it carries two radars, NASA's L-band and ISRO's S-band, and maps almost all of the Earth's land and ice every 12 days. It can detect movements of the surface of less than a centimetre, which helps study earthquakes, landslides, glaciers, ice sheets, crops and forests. Its data are freely available.

Space-based data are central to governance: crop insurance assessments, land records, disaster management, urban planning and tracking illegal mining all rely on them.

- INSAT/GSAT serve communication; Cartosat, Resourcesat and RISAT observe the Earth.
- NavIC is India's regional navigation system; clock failures and NVS-02's orbit problem have weakened it.
- NISAR, launched on 30 July 2025, is the first joint NASA-ISRO earth observation satellite.
W: Satellite systems, NavIC and NISAR are frequent Prelims topics.
Q: Why does India need its own satellite navigation system? What challenges has NavIC faced?

## missiles-strategic-systems | Missiles and strategic systems

India's missile programme took shape under the Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP), launched in 1983 and led by A. P. J. Abdul Kalam at the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO). It produced five missiles: Prithvi (short-range surface-to-surface), Agni (longer-range ballistic), Akash (medium-range surface-to-air), Trishul (short-range surface-to-air, later shelved) and Nag (anti-tank).

The Agni series forms the land-based leg of India's nuclear deterrent. Agni-V, an intercontinental-range ballistic missile with a range over 5,000 km, was first tested in 2012. In March 2024, in Mission Divyastra, DRDO tested Agni-V with MIRV technology (multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles), allowing one missile to carry several warheads aimed at different targets. Agni-Prime is a newer, lighter missile; in September 2025 it was test-fired from a rail-based mobile launcher. India's sea-based deterrent rests on nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines of the Arihant class, armed with K-series missiles.

BrahMos, a supersonic cruise missile developed by a joint venture of DRDO and Russia's NPO Mashinostroyeniya, flies at nearly three times the speed of sound and can be launched from land, ships, submarines and aircraft. Its range has been extended. India signed its first major export deal for BrahMos with the Philippines in 2022. In November 2024, DRDO flight-tested a long-range hypersonic missile, which flies at more than five times the speed of sound.

India also develops missile defence. Its two-tier Ballistic Missile Defence programme intercepts missiles at high and low altitudes, and India operates Russia's S-400 air defence system alongside indigenous systems such as Akash. During Operation Sindoor in May 2025, these air defence systems were widely reported to have intercepted Pakistani drones and missiles.

- The IGMDP (1983) produced Prithvi, Agni, Akash and Nag.
- Agni-V was tested with MIRV technology in March 2024; Agni-Prime was tested from a rail launcher in 2025.
- BrahMos is a supersonic Indo-Russian cruise missile, exported to the Philippines; India tested a hypersonic missile in 2024.

## defence-indigenisation | Defence indigenisation and exports

India has long been one of the world's largest arms importers; the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute ranked it the second-largest importer in 2020–24. Dependence on imports is costly and risky, as supplies can be cut off in a crisis, as the Russia-Ukraine war showed. Self-reliance in defence, part of Atmanirbhar Bharat, has therefore become a major goal.

Policy changes include the Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020, which gives preference to Indian-designed and Indian-made equipment; positive indigenisation lists that ban imports of hundreds of items, from artillery to radars, after set dates; reserving a large share of the defence capital budget for domestic procurement; raising the FDI limit in defence to 74% through the automatic route in 2020; and setting up defence industrial corridors in Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. The Ordnance Factory Board was split into seven corporate public sector companies in 2021. Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) funds start-ups.

Results have followed. According to the Defence Ministry, defence production reached about Rs 1.5 lakh crore in 2024-25, and defence exports reached a record of about Rs 23,622 crore, sent to around 100 countries; private companies account for a growing share. Major indigenous platforms include the Tejas light combat aircraft, the Dhruv and Prachand helicopters, the Arjun tank, Pinaka rocket systems, the aircraft carrier INS Vikrant (commissioned in 2022), and Scorpene and nuclear submarines built in India.

Challenges remain. Many "indigenous" systems still use imported engines and components; the Tejas uses an American engine, and delays in engine deliveries slowed production. Research spending is low, projects often face delays and cost overruns, and the services sometimes prefer proven foreign equipment. Developing jet engines, advanced semiconductors and other critical technologies is the next frontier.

- India was the world's second-largest arms importer in 2020–24.
- Positive indigenisation lists, DAP 2020 and 74% FDI support domestic production.
- Production reached about Rs 1.5 lakh crore and exports about Rs 23,622 crore in 2024-25, but engines and key components are still imported.
W: Defence indigenisation is a frequent GS III question on security and the economy.

## artificial-intelligence-indiaai | Artificial intelligence and the IndiaAI Mission

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, such as recognising images, understanding language and making predictions. Machine learning, in which systems learn patterns from data, drives most modern AI. Generative AI, such as large language models, can produce text, images and code. These systems require huge amounts of data and computing power, especially graphics processing units (GPUs).

The IndiaAI Mission, approved in March 2024 with an outlay of about Rs 10,372 crore over five years, aims to build an AI ecosystem. Its pillars include a large computing infrastructure, with tens of thousands of GPUs made available to researchers and start-ups at subsidised rates; IndiaAI datasets (AIKosh); support for developing Indian foundation models, including in Indian languages; skilling; start-up financing; and safe and trusted AI. Bhashini, a government platform, provides AI translation across Indian languages.

India's approach to AI regulation is "light-touch". In November 2025, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology released the India AI Governance Guidelines, which favour principles, voluntary measures and existing laws over a new standalone AI law, while proposing institutions such as an AI Safety Institute and an AI governance group to coordinate policy. India hosted the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi in February 2026, after earlier summits in the UK, South Korea and France.

AI offers large benefits in health diagnostics, agriculture advisories, education, weather forecasting and public services. But it raises serious concerns: job losses in routine work, including the IT services and business process sectors that employ millions of Indians; bias in algorithms; deepfakes and misinformation; privacy; copyright disputes over training data; concentration of power in a few global companies; and the energy and water demands of data centres.

- AI needs large datasets and computing power, especially GPUs.
- The IndiaAI Mission (2024, about Rs 10,372 crore) funds compute, datasets, foundation models and skills.
- India's November 2025 AI Governance Guidelines take a light-touch approach; India hosted the AI Impact Summit in 2026.
W: AI policy, regulation and its social impact are among the most important current GS III topics.
Q: Should India adopt a dedicated law to regulate artificial intelligence? Discuss.

## semiconductor-mission | Semiconductors and the India Semiconductor Mission

Semiconductors, or chips, are the foundation of modern electronics, from phones and cars to satellites and weapons. Making them involves several stages: design; fabrication in "fabs", which etch circuits onto silicon wafers and cost billions of dollars; and assembly, testing, marking and packaging (ATMP or OSAT). Production is highly concentrated. Taiwan, especially TSMC, makes most of the world's advanced chips, and South Korea, the United States, Japan, China and the Netherlands (which makes the most advanced lithography machines) control other key steps. The global chip shortage during the pandemic and US-China rivalry have made chip supply a strategic concern.

India has many chip designers, since global companies run large design centres in Bengaluru and Hyderabad, but until recently had no commercial fabrication. The India Semiconductor Mission was launched in December 2021 with an outlay of Rs 76,000 crore. It offers to fund up to half the project cost of fabs, display fabs and packaging units, with states adding more. The Design Linked Incentive scheme supports chip design start-ups.

The first approved project, Micron Technology's assembly and test plant at Sanand in Gujarat (approved in 2023), began commercial production of memory chips in early 2026. Tata Electronics, in partnership with Taiwan's PSMC, is building India's first commercial fab at Dholera in Gujarat, at a cost of about Rs 91,000 crore. By 2026 about a dozen projects had been approved under the first phase, in states including Gujarat, Assam, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha, Punjab and Andhra Pradesh. The Union Budget 2026-27 announced ISM 2.0, which focuses on equipment, materials, design and supply chains.

Challenges include the very high cost of fabs, the need for uninterrupted power and ultra-pure water, a shortage of skilled engineers for manufacturing, and dependence on imported materials and equipment. Most initial projects make older-generation or "legacy" chips, which are still widely used in cars and appliances.

- Chipmaking involves design, fabrication and packaging; production is concentrated in Taiwan and a few countries.
- The India Semiconductor Mission (2021, Rs 76,000 crore) funds up to half of project costs.
- Micron's Sanand plant began commercial production in 2026; Tata's Dholera fab is India's first commercial fab; ISM 2.0 was announced in 2026.
W: Semiconductors are a high-priority GS III topic, linking technology, industry and geopolitics.

## cyber-security-data-protection | Cyber security and data protection

As more government services, banking and infrastructure move online, cyber attacks have become a major security risk. Threats include malware and ransomware, which locks data until a ransom is paid; phishing; denial-of-service attacks that overwhelm websites; data breaches; and attacks on critical infrastructure such as power grids, ports and hospitals. In 2022, a ransomware attack shut down servers at AIIMS New Delhi for days. Online financial frauds, including "digital arrest" scams in which criminals pose as police or officials, have surged.

India's legal and institutional framework rests on the Information Technology Act, 2000. Under Section 70B, the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In), set up in 2004, is the national agency for responding to cyber incidents; since 2022, organisations must report incidents to it within six hours. The National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC), under Section 70A, protects critical sectors. The Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C), set up in 2020 under the Home Ministry, coordinates action against cybercrime and runs the national reporting portal and the 1930 helpline. The National Cyber Security Policy dates from 2013; a new strategy has been drafted but not yet released. The National Security Council Secretariat's National Cyber Security Coordinator oversees policy.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is India's first comprehensive data protection law. It applies to digital personal data and requires organisations (data fiduciaries) to obtain consent, use data only for stated purposes, protect it and notify breaches. It gives individuals rights to access, correct and erase their data, sets up a Data Protection Board, and provides penalties of up to Rs 250 crore. Critics point to broad exemptions for government agencies and its amendment of the RTI Act. The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, with obligations phased in over 18 months.

- Ransomware, phishing and financial frauds such as "digital arrest" scams are rising threats.
- CERT-In, NCIIPC and I4C are the main agencies; incidents must be reported to CERT-In within six hours.
- The DPDP Act (2023) sets consent-based data protection; its rules were notified in November 2025.
W: Cyber security and data protection are frequent GS III security and GS II governance questions.
Q: Are India's cyber security institutions adequate to protect critical infrastructure? Suggest improvements.
