# THEME 2
## tripartite-struggle-early-medieval | The tripartite struggle and early medieval kingdoms

Between the eighth and tenth centuries, three powers fought for control of Kannauj, the city in the Ganga plain that had been Harsha's capital and had become a symbol of imperial power. Historians call this the tripartite struggle. The Gurjara-Pratiharas ruled western India and Rajasthan, the Palas ruled Bengal and Bihar, and the Rashtrakutas ruled the Deccan.

Each power left a distinct legacy. The Palas were patrons of Buddhism: Dharmapala founded the Vikramashila monastery, and the Palas supported Nalanda. The Pratiharas resisted Arab expansion from Sindh for nearly two centuries. The Rashtrakutas built the Kailasa temple at Ellora, carved out of a single rock under Krishna I, and Arab travellers such as Al-Masudi described their kingdom as one of the great empires of the world.

None of the three managed to hold Kannauj for long. The long wars exhausted them, and by the tenth century they had given way to smaller regional dynasties: Rajput clans such as the Chauhans, Paramaras and Chandelas in the north, and the Cholas and later Chalukyas in the south. The Chandelas built the Khajuraho temples, a UNESCO site.

Historians describe this period through the idea of regionalisation. Land grants to Brahmins, temples and officials increased, local chiefs gained power, and regional languages and scripts developed. Some scholars call this feudalism; others reject the European term but agree that power became more local.

- Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas fought for Kannauj from the eighth to the tenth century.
- The Palas supported Buddhist learning; the Rashtrakutas built the Kailasa temple at Ellora.
- Power shifted to regional dynasties, and land grants became widespread.
W: The tripartite struggle and the "Indian feudalism" debate are standard topics for Prelims and the history optional.
Q: Why did no single power manage to dominate north India between the eighth and tenth centuries?

## delhi-sultanate | The Delhi Sultanate, 1206 to 1526

The Delhi Sultanate began after Muhammad of Ghor defeated Prithviraj Chauhan at the second Battle of Tarain in 1192. When Muhammad died in 1206, his general Qutbuddin Aibak became the first sultan. Five dynasties followed: the Mamluks (sometimes called the Slave dynasty), Khaljis, Tughlaqs, Sayyids and Lodis.

Iltutmish consolidated the state, introduced the silver tanka and copper jital, and organised the iqta system, in which officers were assigned revenue from territories in return for military service. His daughter Razia (1236–1240) was the only woman to rule from Delhi. Balban emphasised the sultan's divine dignity and crushed the power of the nobles known as the Forty.

Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316) defended the Sultanate against repeated Mongol invasions and campaigned into the Deccan through his general Malik Kafur. To pay a large standing army, he fixed market prices in Delhi, measured land for revenue and set the land tax at half the produce. Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351) is known for bold experiments that failed: shifting the capital to Daulatabad, introducing token copper currency, and planning distant campaigns. Firoz Shah Tughlaq built canals and towns but weakened central control.

Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398 left the Sultanate a shadow of itself. The Lodis, the last dynasty, were defeated by Babur at the first Battle of Panipat in 1526. The Sultanate left lasting marks: the Qutub Minar and other monuments, the iqta system, Persian as a language of administration, and new crafts and technologies such as the spinning wheel.

- The Sultanate lasted from 1206 to 1526 under five dynasties.
- Alauddin Khalji's market control and Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token currency are key reforms to know.
- The iqta system and Persian administration outlasted the Sultanate.
W: Sultanate rulers and their reforms are among the most frequently tested medieval topics.
Q: Assess the administrative and economic measures of Alauddin Khalji. Were they driven by military needs?

## vijayanagara-bahmani | Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom

Two great Deccan states rose in the fourteenth century as Delhi's hold weakened. Vijayanagara was founded in 1336 by the brothers Harihara and Bukka of the Sangama dynasty, with its capital at Hampi on the Tungabhadra. The Bahmani kingdom was founded in 1347 by Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah, with its capital at Gulbarga and later Bidar.

The two states fought for control of the fertile Raichur doab between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers. The Bahmani state reached its height under the minister Mahmud Gawan, who built a famous madrasa at Bidar. After his execution in 1481, the kingdom broke into five Deccan sultanates: Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmadnagar, Berar and Bidar.

Vijayanagara peaked under Krishnadeva Raya of the Tuluva dynasty (1509–1529). He was a successful general, a patron of Telugu literature whose court had poets known as the Ashtadiggajas, and the author of the Telugu work *Amuktamalyada* on statecraft. Foreign visitors such as the Portuguese Domingo Paes described Hampi as a city as large as Rome. Administration relied on nayakas, military chiefs who held territory in return for troops, a system that later produced independent Nayaka kingdoms in Madurai and Thanjavur.

In 1565 the combined Deccan sultanates defeated Vijayanagara at the Battle of Talikota (also called Rakshasa-Tangadi). Hampi was sacked, and its ruins are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The kingdom survived in reduced form further south for some decades.

- Vijayanagara (1336) and the Bahmani kingdom (1347) dominated the medieval Deccan.
- Krishnadeva Raya marks Vijayanagara's peak; the nayaka system was its administrative base.
- The Battle of Talikota in 1565 ended Vijayanagara's power.
W: Vijayanagara's administration, foreign travellers' accounts and Hampi's architecture are frequent exam topics.
Q: Describe the nayaka system of Vijayanagara. How did it contribute to both the strength and the decline of the empire?

## bhakti-movement | The Bhakti movement: devotion over ritual

Bhakti means loving devotion to a personal god. As a movement, it began in Tamil country between the sixth and ninth centuries with the Alvars, devotees of Vishnu, and the Nayanars, devotees of Shiva. They composed hymns in Tamil, travelled between temples, and came from many castes, including some considered low. Their hymns were collected as the *Divya Prabandham* and the *Tirumurai*.

Philosophers gave bhakti a theological base. Ramanuja (eleventh century) argued for qualified non-dualism (vishishtadvaita), in which devotion leads to liberation. In twelfth-century Karnataka, Basavanna founded the Virashaiva or Lingayat movement, which rejected caste, temple ritual and the authority of Brahmins, and expressed itself in Kannada vachanas.

From the fourteenth century, bhakti spread across north and west India. Historians often divide it into two streams. Saguna bhakti worshipped god with form, such as Rama or Krishna: Tulsidas wrote the *Ramcharitmanas* in Awadhi, Surdas sang of Krishna, Mirabai rejected royal convention for her devotion, and Chaitanya popularised Krishna devotion in Bengal. Nirguna bhakti worshipped a formless god: Kabir, a weaver, mocked both Hindu and Muslim ritualism in direct verse, and Guru Nanak's teachings founded Sikhism. In Maharashtra, Namdev, Eknath and Tukaram belonged to the Varkari tradition centred on Pandharpur. In Assam, Srimanta Sankardeva founded neo-Vaishnavism and the satra monasteries.

The movement's lasting impact was on language and society. It created literature in regional languages and gave women and lower castes a religious voice, though it rarely overturned the caste system itself.

- Bhakti began with the Alvars and Nayanars in Tamil country.
- Saguna saints worshipped god with form; nirguna saints such as Kabir and Nanak worshipped a formless god.
- The movement enriched regional languages and challenged, but did not end, caste hierarchy.
W: Saints, their regions, languages and teachings are standard Prelims questions; social impact suits Mains GS I.
Q: How did the Bhakti saints use regional languages to challenge social hierarchies? Give examples from different regions.

## sufi-movement | The Sufi movement in India

Sufism is the mystical tradition of Islam, emphasising love of God, inner purification and the guidance of a spiritual master (pir or shaikh). Sufis organised themselves into orders called silsilas and lived in hospices called khanqahs, where disciples, travellers and the poor were welcomed. Many Sufis kept their distance from rulers and the orthodox clergy.

Sufism reached India by the eleventh century. Al-Hujwiri, known as Data Ganj Bakhsh, settled in Lahore and wrote the *Kashf-ul-Mahjub*, one of the earliest Persian works on Sufism. The Chishti order, the most influential in India, was established by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, who settled in Ajmer in the late twelfth century. His successors included Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki in Delhi, Baba Farid in Punjab, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, and Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi. Nizamuddin's disciple Amir Khusrau was a poet and musician credited with developing qawwali and writing in Hindavi as well as Persian.

The Suhrawardi order, based in Multan, was more willing to accept royal patronage. Later orders such as the Naqshbandi, active in Mughal times, were closer to orthodoxy.

Sufi practices such as sama, devotional music that could lead to ecstatic states, and pilgrimage to the tombs (dargahs) of saints made Sufism popular among people of all faiths. The dargahs at Ajmer and Delhi still draw huge crowds. Sufism and Bhakti influenced each other, sharing ideas of love, equality and devotion that shaped the composite culture of medieval India.

- Sufism is Islamic mysticism organised into silsilas around khanqahs.
- The Chishti order, from Moinuddin Chishti to Nizamuddin Auliya, was the most influential in India.
- Sufi music, poetry and dargahs drew followers across religious lines.
W: Sufi orders, saints and their links to Bhakti are recurring topics in Prelims and in Mains questions on composite culture.
Q: "Bhakti and Sufi movements created a shared cultural space in medieval India." Discuss.

## mughals-babur-to-akbar | The Mughals from Babur to Akbar

Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, founded the Mughal empire by defeating Ibrahim Lodi at the first Battle of Panipat in 1526, using field artillery and cavalry tactics new to north India. He defeated Rana Sanga of Mewar at Khanwa in 1527 and wrote a frank memoir in Turkish, the *Baburnama*.

His son Humayun lost the empire to the Afghan Sher Shah Suri in 1540. In just five years, Sher Shah reorganised revenue on the basis of land measurement, issued a silver coin called the rupiya, improved the road from Bengal to Punjab that later became the Grand Trunk Road, and built sarais for travellers. Humayun returned with Persian help in 1555 but died the next year.

Akbar (1556–1605) came to the throne at thirteen. His forces won the second Battle of Panipat in 1556 against Hemu. Over the next decades he expanded the empire across north India, Gujarat, Bengal and parts of the Deccan. He built a centralised administration through the mansabdari system, in which every officer held a rank (mansab) that fixed both his pay and the number of cavalry he maintained. His finance minister Raja Todar Mal developed the zabt system, which assessed revenue on measured land and average yields.

Akbar's religious policy aimed at sulh-i-kul, or universal peace. He abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims in 1564, held debates among scholars of different faiths at the Ibadat Khana in Fatehpur Sikri, married into Rajput families and appointed Rajputs to high office. Abul Fazl's *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari* record his reign.

- Babur won at Panipat in 1526; Sher Shah's reforms in 1540–45 shaped later Mughal administration.
- Akbar's mansabdari and zabt systems were the backbone of Mughal government.
- Sulh-i-kul, the end of jizya and Rajput alliances underpinned Akbar's policy of inclusion.
W: Mughal administration and Akbar's religious policy are core Prelims and Mains topics.
Q: Explain how the mansabdari system helped Akbar build a centralised empire.

## later-mughals-decline | Jahangir to Aurangzeb, and the Mughal decline

Akbar's successors ruled the empire at its peak of wealth. Jahangir (1605–1627) is remembered for his chain of justice and his patronage of painting. His wife Nur Jahan was unusually powerful. Sir Thomas Roe, an English ambassador, visited his court in 1615 and obtained trading rights for the East India Company. Shah Jahan (1628–1658) built the Taj Mahal at Agra, the Red Fort and the Jama Masjid in his new capital Shahjahanabad (Delhi), and commissioned the Peacock Throne.

Aurangzeb (1658–1707) took the throne after a war of succession against his brothers. He expanded the empire to its greatest size by conquering Bijapur and Golconda in the 1680s, but spent the last twenty-five years of his life fighting in the Deccan against the Marathas. He reimposed the jizya in 1679 and followed a more orthodox religious policy. He executed the ninth Sikh Guru, Tegh Bahadur, in 1675. Revolts by the Jats, Satnamis, Sikhs and Rajputs grew during his reign.

After Aurangzeb's death in 1707 the empire weakened quickly. Historians debate the causes. Some stress Aurangzeb's religious policies and Deccan wars. Others point to a crisis in the jagirdari system, as there was not enough revenue-yielding land to pay a growing number of nobles. Others emphasise the rise of strong regional powers and weak successors. Nadir Shah's invasion and sack of Delhi in 1739, when he carried off the Peacock Throne, exposed the empire's weakness.

Successor states emerged in Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal, while the Marathas, Sikhs and later the British competed for power.

- Shah Jahan's reign saw the Taj Mahal and Shahjahanabad; Aurangzeb took the empire to its largest extent.
- Aurangzeb's Deccan wars and religious policy are part of the debate on decline.
- After 1707, regional states and Nadir Shah's 1739 invasion revealed Mughal weakness.
W: The causes of Mughal decline are a classic analytical question, and the later Mughal timeline is a Prelims staple.
Q: Was the decline of the Mughal empire caused more by structural problems or by the policies of individual rulers?

## sikh-gurus-and-sikh-state | The Sikh Gurus and the rise of Sikh power

Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak (1469–1539), born at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan. He taught devotion to one formless God, honest work, sharing with others, and the equality of all people regardless of caste or gender. He established the institution of langar, the community kitchen where everyone eats together.

Nine Gurus followed. Guru Angad developed the Gurmukhi script. Guru Ram Das founded the city of Amritsar. Guru Arjan Dev compiled the Adi Granth in 1604 and built the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple); he was executed on Jahangir's orders in 1606. Guru Hargobind adopted the symbols of both spiritual and temporal authority and built the Akal Takht. The ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, was executed in Delhi in 1675 under Aurangzeb.

Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, created the Khalsa in 1699 at Anandpur Sahib, a community of initiated Sikhs with a distinct identity and the five Ks. Before his death in 1708 he declared that the Guru Granth Sahib would be the eternal Guru after him.

Banda Singh Bahadur led a Sikh uprising in Punjab, abolished the zamindari system in areas he controlled and issued coins, before being executed in 1716. In the eighteenth century Sikh power was organised in misls (confederacies). Ranjit Singh united them, took Lahore in 1799 and built a powerful kingdom with a modern army trained partly by European officers. After his death in 1839, the British defeated the Sikhs in two Anglo-Sikh wars and annexed Punjab in 1849.

- Guru Nanak taught one God, equality and honest work; langar embodies these values.
- The Adi Granth was compiled in 1604, and the Khalsa was founded in 1699.
- Ranjit Singh's kingdom lasted from 1799 until British annexation of Punjab in 1849.
W: The Gurus and their contributions, the Khalsa and Ranjit Singh's state are frequent Prelims topics.
Q: How did the Sikh community evolve from a devotional movement into a political power between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries?

## marathas | The Marathas: from Shivaji to the Peshwas

Shivaji (1630–1680) built an independent Maratha state in western Maharashtra from a base of hill forts. He fought the Bijapur sultanate and the Mughals, escaped from Aurangzeb's custody in Agra in 1666, and was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad in 1674. He used guerrilla tactics suited to the hilly Sahyadri terrain and built a navy to protect the Konkan coast.

Shivaji's administration was efficient. He was advised by a council of eight ministers, the ashtapradhan, headed by the peshwa. He preferred paying officials in cash rather than granting jagirs, to keep them from becoming independent. Revenue was collected on measured land, and the Marathas also levied chauth (a quarter of revenue) and sardeshmukhi (an additional tenth) on neighbouring areas in return for protection.

After Shivaji, the Marathas survived a long war with Aurangzeb. In the eighteenth century real power passed to the peshwas, starting with Balaji Vishwanath. Under Baji Rao I, Maratha armies reached Delhi. By mid-century the Maratha confederacy, with chiefs such as the Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads and Bhonsles, was the largest power in India.

Their expansion north ended at the third Battle of Panipat in 1761, where Ahmad Shah Abdali defeated them with heavy losses. The Marathas recovered but were weakened by internal rivalry and lost three Anglo-Maratha wars, the last ending in 1818. The Maratha Military Landscapes, a group of twelve forts, were added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2025.

- Shivaji was crowned in 1674 and governed through the ashtapradhan.
- Chauth and sardeshmukhi extended Maratha revenue and influence.
- The third Battle of Panipat (1761) and the Anglo-Maratha wars ended Maratha supremacy by 1818.
W: Shivaji's administration and Maratha expansion are regularly asked, and the 2025 UNESCO listing links history to current affairs.
Q: Discuss the features of Shivaji's administration. How did it differ from the Mughal system?

## ahoms-north-east | The Ahom kingdom and the North-East

The Ahoms ruled the Brahmaputra valley in Assam for about six hundred years, from 1228, when their founder Sukaphaa (Chaolung Siu-Ka-Pha) arrived from the Shan region of present-day Myanmar, until the Burmese invasions and British annexation in the 1820s. This is one of the longest continuous dynasties in Indian history.

The Ahoms developed a distinctive administration. The paik system required every adult male to provide labour or military service to the state in turns, in return for land. This allowed the kingdom to raise armies and build roads, embankments and tanks without a large cash economy. The Ahoms also kept detailed court chronicles called buranjis, first in the Tai Ahom language and later in Assamese, a rare tradition of historical writing in medieval India.

The Ahoms are remembered for resisting Mughal expansion. In 1671, at the Battle of Saraighat on the Brahmaputra near Guwahati, the Ahom general Lachit Borphukan defeated a much larger Mughal force using river warfare and fortifications. The National Defence Academy awards the Lachit Borphukan gold medal to its best cadet.

Ahom royalty were buried in mound burials called moidams at Charaideo, their first capital. The Moidams were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024, the first cultural site in the North-East to be listed. Over time the Ahoms adopted Hinduism and the Assamese language, blending Tai and local traditions.

- The Ahoms ruled Assam from 1228 to the 1820s.
- The paik labour system and buranji chronicles were distinctive features.
- Lachit Borphukan's victory at Saraighat (1671) stopped Mughal expansion; the Charaideo Moidams are a UNESCO site.
W: North-East history is increasingly tested, and the Moidams link medieval history to recent heritage news.
Q: How did geography shape the military and administrative systems of the Ahom kingdom?
