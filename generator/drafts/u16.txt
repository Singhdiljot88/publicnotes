# THEME 16
## un-security-council-reform | The United Nations and Security Council reform

The United Nations was founded on 24 October 1945, when its Charter came into force; India was among the 51 founding members. It has 193 member states. Its six principal organs are the General Assembly, where every member has one vote; the Security Council, responsible for international peace and security; the Economic and Social Council; the Trusteeship Council, inactive since 1994; the International Court of Justice at The Hague; and the Secretariat, headed by the Secretary-General, currently António Guterres, whose second term ends on 31 December 2026.

The Security Council has 15 members: five permanent members with veto power (the United States, Russia, China, the United Kingdom and France) and ten non-permanent members elected for two-year terms by the General Assembly, with five elected each year. Its resolutions under Chapter VII of the Charter are binding on all members. India has served eight terms as a non-permanent member, most recently in 2021–22, and has announced its candidacy for 2028–29.

India argues that the Council reflects the world of 1945, not today's. Africa and Latin America have no permanent members, and Asia has only China. India, Brazil, Germany and Japan, known as the G4, support each other's bids for permanent seats. India is also part of the L.69 group of developing countries seeking reform. Africa's Common African Position, the Ezulwini Consensus, demands two permanent seats with veto. The Uniting for Consensus group, including Pakistan, Italy and Argentina, opposes new permanent members. Reform requires amending the Charter, which needs the approval of two-thirds of UN members and ratification by all five permanent members, so progress through the intergovernmental negotiations has been slow.

India is one of the largest contributors of troops to UN peacekeeping, having sent over 2.9 lakh personnel to more than 50 missions since the 1950s.

- The UN has 193 members; the Security Council has five veto-holding permanent members and ten elected members.
- India has served eight terms on the Council, most recently in 2021–22.
- The G4 seeks permanent seats, but Charter amendment requires P5 ratification.
W: UN structure and Security Council reform are among the most frequently asked IR topics.
Q: Why has Security Council reform stalled despite broad agreement that the Council is unrepresentative?

## world-trade-organization | The World Trade Organization

The World Trade Organization (WTO) was set up on 1 January 1995, replacing the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947, after the Uruguay Round of negotiations. It has 166 members and is based in Geneva. Its highest decision-making body is the Ministerial Conference, which meets roughly every two years, and decisions are generally made by consensus. Its core principles are non-discrimination, through most-favoured-nation treatment (no favouring one trading partner over others, with exceptions for free trade agreements and developing countries) and national treatment (treating imported goods no worse than domestic goods once they enter the market).

Key agreements include those on goods (GATT), services (GATS), intellectual property (TRIPS) and agriculture (the Agreement on Agriculture, or AoA). The AoA classifies farm subsidies into boxes: the amber box (trade-distorting support, subject to limits), the blue box (support linked to production limits) and the green box (minimally distorting support, such as research, allowed without limits). India's minimum support price procurement for public food stocks risks breaching its amber box limits. At the Bali Ministerial in 2013, members agreed a "peace clause" protecting developing countries' public stockholding programmes from legal challenge until a permanent solution is found, and India has invoked it.

The WTO's dispute settlement system was once its strongest feature. But the Appellate Body, its appeals court, has been unable to function since December 2019, because the United States has blocked the appointment of new members. Countries can now appeal panel rulings "into the void", leaving disputes unresolved. A group of members created an interim appeal arrangement (MPIA), which India has not joined.

India's priorities at the WTO include protecting food security and farmers, preserving special and differential treatment for developing countries, resisting new issues such as investment facilitation, and fighting subsidies to fishing fleets of rich countries while protecting small fishers.

- The WTO (1995) has 166 members and works mainly by consensus.
- The Agreement on Agriculture's boxes limit trade-distorting farm subsidies; the 2013 peace clause protects India's food stockholding.
- The Appellate Body has been paralysed since December 2019.
W: The WTO, especially agriculture and dispute settlement, is a recurring GS II and GS III topic.
Q: How does the WTO's Agreement on Agriculture affect India's food security policies?

## imf-world-bank-ndb | The IMF, the World Bank and new development banks

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank were created at the Bretton Woods conference in the United States in 1944, and are sometimes called the Bretton Woods institutions. India was a founding member.

The IMF promotes international monetary cooperation and financial stability. It monitors countries' economic policies (surveillance, through annual Article IV consultations), lends to countries facing balance of payments crises, usually with policy conditions, and provides technical assistance. India borrowed from the IMF during the 1991 crisis. The IMF also issues Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), an international reserve asset whose value is based on a basket of five currencies: the US dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi, Japanese yen and British pound. Voting power depends on quotas, which reflect countries' economic size; the United States has over 16% of votes, enough to veto major decisions that require an 85% majority. Reforms to give emerging economies a larger share have been slow. The IMF publishes the World Economic Outlook and the Global Financial Stability Report.

The World Bank Group finances development. Its main arms are the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD), lending to middle-income countries, and the International Development Association (IDA), providing low-cost loans and grants to the poorest. The group also includes the International Finance Corporation (lending to the private sector), MIGA (investment guarantees) and ICSID (investment disputes). India is among the largest cumulative borrowers. By tradition, the World Bank's president is American and the IMF's managing director is European.

Dissatisfaction with Western dominance led to new institutions. The BRICS-led New Development Bank, headquartered in Shanghai, began operations in 2015, with India's K. V. Kamath as its first president. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), headquartered in Beijing, began in 2016; India is its second-largest shareholder after China and one of its largest borrowers.

- The IMF and World Bank were created at Bretton Woods in 1944.
- The IMF lends for balance of payments crises and issues SDRs; votes depend on quotas.
- The New Development Bank (2015) and AIIB (2016) give emerging economies more voice.
W: Bretton Woods institutions and newer banks are standard Prelims and Mains IR topics.
Q: Do the IMF and World Bank need reform to reflect the rise of emerging economies? Discuss.

## saarc-bimstec | SAARC and BIMSTEC: regionalism in South Asia

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) was founded in Dhaka in December 1985 by Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka; Afghanistan joined in 2007. Its secretariat is in Kathmandu. Its charter excludes bilateral and contentious issues and requires unanimous decisions. It created the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA), in force from 2006, the SAARC Development Fund and the South Asian University in New Delhi.

SAARC has achieved little. Intra-regional trade remains around 5% of the region's total trade, far below ASEAN's. The main obstacle has been tension between India and Pakistan. The last SAARC summit was held in Kathmandu in 2014. The 2016 summit, due to be held in Islamabad, was cancelled after India and several other members pulled out following the terrorist attack on an army camp at Uri.

India has turned instead to BIMSTEC, the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation, founded in 1997 through the Bangkok Declaration. It has seven members: five from South Asia (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal and Sri Lanka) and two from South-East Asia (Myanmar and Thailand), linking India's Neighbourhood First and Act East policies. Its secretariat is in Dhaka. The BIMSTEC Charter was adopted at the Colombo summit in 2022 and came into force in 2024, giving the grouping a legal personality. The sixth summit was held in Bangkok in April 2025. Cooperation has been organised into seven pillars, with India leading security, including counter-terrorism and disaster management.

BIMSTEC also faces challenges: a free trade agreement has been under negotiation since 2004 without conclusion, and instability in Myanmar and political changes in Bangladesh complicate cooperation.

- SAARC (1985) has eight members but has not held a summit since 2014.
- India-Pakistan tensions, especially after Uri in 2016, have paralysed SAARC.
- BIMSTEC (1997) has seven members; its charter came into force in 2024.
W: SAARC and BIMSTEC are frequently asked in Prelims and in Mains questions on regional cooperation.
Q: Can BIMSTEC succeed where SAARC failed? Discuss the opportunities and obstacles.

## asean-act-east | ASEAN and India's Act East Policy

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) was founded in Bangkok in 1967. It now has eleven members: Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam and, since October 2025, Timor-Leste. ASEAN works by consensus and non-interference and sits at the centre of a network of regional forums, including the ASEAN Regional Forum, the East Asia Summit and the ASEAN Defence Ministers' Meeting Plus, giving it influence beyond its economic weight.

India's engagement began with the Look East Policy in 1991, aimed mainly at economic ties with South-East Asia after the Cold War. In 2014 it was upgraded to the Act East Policy, adding security, connectivity and cultural dimensions and extending to the wider Indo-Pacific, including Japan, South Korea and Australia. India became an ASEAN sectoral partner in 1992, a dialogue partner in 1996 and a summit-level partner in 2002. The relationship was upgraded to a comprehensive strategic partnership in 2022.

The ASEAN-India Trade in Goods Agreement came into force in 2010. India's trade deficit with ASEAN has grown, and both sides agreed to review the agreement. India withdrew from the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in 2019, citing concerns about cheap imports, especially from China, and inadequate protection for its farmers and industries.

Connectivity projects include the India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway and the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project, both delayed. India has also deepened security ties with individual countries: it sold BrahMos missiles to the Philippines, delivered from 2024, and has defence partnerships with Vietnam, Indonesia and Singapore. The North-East is meant to be India's gateway to South-East Asia.

- ASEAN (1967) has eleven members after Timor-Leste joined in October 2025.
- Look East (1991) became Act East (2014); the partnership became comprehensive and strategic in 2022.
- India left RCEP in 2019; connectivity projects such as the Trilateral Highway are delayed.
W: ASEAN and Act East are among the most frequent IR questions in both Prelims and Mains.
Q: Assess the achievements and shortcomings of India's Act East Policy.

## sco-central-asia | The SCO and India's Central Asia ties

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) grew out of the "Shanghai Five" grouping of China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, formed in 1996 to settle border issues. It became the SCO in 2001 with the addition of Uzbekistan. India and Pakistan became full members in 2017, Iran in 2023 and Belarus in 2024, taking membership to ten. Its two permanent bodies are the Secretariat in Beijing and the Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS) in Tashkent.

The SCO's focus is security, especially combating what it calls the "three evils" of terrorism, separatism and extremism, along with economic and cultural cooperation. It covers a large share of the world's population and of Eurasia's landmass.

India's membership gives it a platform in Central Asia and with Russia and China, and access to RATS for counter-terrorism cooperation. India chaired the SCO in 2023, hosting a virtual summit. But India's interests often diverge from China's and Pakistan's. India has refused to endorse China's Belt and Road Initiative in SCO declarations because the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor runs through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir. In June 2025, India's defence minister declined to sign an SCO defence ministers' statement that omitted the Pahalgam terror attack. The leaders' declaration at the Tianjin summit in September 2025, which Prime Minister Modi attended, condemned the Pahalgam attack.

Central Asia matters to India for energy, uranium (India imports uranium from Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan), connectivity and security, especially regarding Afghanistan. India hosted the first India-Central Asia Summit virtually in 2022. Connectivity is limited because Pakistan blocks overland routes, so India relies on Iran's Chabahar port and the International North-South Transport Corridor linking India, Iran, Russia and Central Asia.

- The SCO (2001) has ten members; India joined in 2017.
- Its focus is security against terrorism, separatism and extremism, through RATS in Tashkent.
- India uses the SCO to engage Central Asia but opposes BRI endorsements and pushes counter-terrorism language.
W: The SCO and Central Asia are frequently tested in IR, especially in the context of China, Russia and connectivity.
Q: How does India balance its interests in the SCO with its differences with China and Pakistan?

## india-us-relations | India-United States relations

India and the United States, the world's largest and oldest democracies, were often described as "estranged democracies" during the Cold War, when India was non-aligned and the US was allied with Pakistan. Relations improved after the Cold War and especially after the India-US Civil Nuclear Agreement of 2008, which ended India's nuclear isolation without requiring it to sign the NPT.

Defence ties have grown steadily. The US designated India a "Major Defence Partner" in 2016, and the two signed four foundational agreements: GSOMIA (2002) on military information security, LEMOA (2016) on logistics, COMCASA (2018) on secure communications, and BECA (2020) on geospatial intelligence. India buys American aircraft such as the C-17, P-8I and Apache, and the two hold the Malabar naval exercise with Japan and Australia. The Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET), launched in 2023 and later renamed TRUST, covers semiconductors, AI, space and defence production. In October 2025 the two signed a new ten-year defence framework.

The US is India's largest trading partner for goods, and the Indian diaspora of about five million in the US is among its most successful communities. Both countries are members of the Quad.

The relationship also has friction. Trade disputes are long-standing. In 2025, the US imposed tariffs of up to 50% on many Indian goods, including a penalty linked to India's purchases of Russian oil, before a trade arrangement announced in February 2026 reduced them. Differences persist over India's ties with Russia, immigration and H-1B visas, which most often go to Indian professionals, and the treatment of Indian migrants. India's policy of strategic autonomy means it partners with the US without becoming an ally.

- The 2008 civil nuclear deal transformed India-US relations.
- Four foundational defence agreements (GSOMIA, LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA) underpin military cooperation.
- Tariffs, Russian oil, visas and migration have caused friction, especially since 2025.
W: India-US relations are among the most important IR topics for both stages of the exam.
Q: "India and the United States are partners, not allies." Examine this statement in the light of recent developments.

## india-russia-relations | India-Russia relations

India's relationship with Russia, and earlier the Soviet Union, has been one of its most stable partnerships. The Soviet Union supported India diplomatically on Kashmir, using its Security Council veto several times, and the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation of August 1971 helped deter outside intervention during the Bangladesh war. The Soviet Union helped build steel plants at Bhilai and Bokaro and supplied much of India's military equipment.

After the Soviet collapse, the two signed a new treaty of friendship in 1993 and a Declaration on Strategic Partnership in 2000, upgraded to a "Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership" in 2010. They hold annual summits; President Putin visited New Delhi for the 23rd summit in December 2025.

Defence remains central. A large share of India's military equipment is of Soviet or Russian origin. Major projects include the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile (a joint venture), the S-400 air defence system (ordered in 2018 despite the threat of US sanctions under CAATSA), Sukhoi-30MKI fighters, and the AK-203 rifles made in Amethi. Russia also builds the Kudankulam nuclear power plant in Tamil Nadu. However, India has been diversifying its arms imports towards France, the United States and Israel, and towards domestic production, and the share of Russia in India's imports has fallen.

Trade surged after 2022, when India began buying large quantities of discounted Russian crude oil following Western sanctions over the Ukraine war, making Russia India's top oil supplier for a time. This created a large trade deficit and drew US pressure. India has not condemned Russia at the UN over Ukraine, calling for dialogue and diplomacy, and Prime Minister Modi told President Putin in 2022 that "today's era is not an era of war".

Concerns include Russia's growing closeness to China, payment difficulties due to sanctions, and delays in defence deliveries.

- The 1971 treaty and Soviet support shaped a lasting partnership; it became a "Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership" in 2010.
- Russia remains a key defence supplier (S-400, BrahMos), though India is diversifying.
- Discounted Russian oil since 2022 boosted trade but caused friction with the US.
W: India-Russia relations, especially in the context of Ukraine, sanctions and oil, are a frequent Mains topic.
Q: How has the Russia-Ukraine war affected India's relationship with Russia and the West?

## india-africa | India and Africa

India's ties with Africa are old: trade across the Indian Ocean dates back centuries, and Gandhi's political career began in South Africa. During the freedom struggle and after, India supported African liberation movements and opposed apartheid. A large Indian diaspora, nearly three million, lives in African countries such as South Africa, Mauritius, Kenya and Tanzania.

Modern engagement has expanded. India-Africa trade was around 100 billion US dollars in recent years, making India one of Africa's largest trading partners. India imports crude oil, gold, coal and minerals, and exports refined petroleum, pharmaceuticals, vehicles and machinery. Indian generic medicines are vital to African public health. India has extended lines of credit worth over 12 billion dollars for infrastructure projects, and Indian companies have invested heavily in telecoms, energy and agriculture.

Institutionally, India held three India-Africa Forum Summits, in 2008, 2011 and 2015, the last attended by all 54 African countries. A fourth summit has been repeatedly delayed. India's development partnership emphasises capacity building, through scholarships, training under the Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation (ITEC) programme, the Pan-African e-Network for tele-education and tele-medicine, and its successor e-VidyaBharti and e-ArogyaBharti. India has opened many new embassies in Africa since 2018.

During its G20 presidency in 2023, India championed the admission of the African Union as a permanent member of the G20, which was agreed at the New Delhi summit. India also presents itself as a voice of the Global South, hosting the Voice of the Global South summits.

India's engagement is often compared with China's much larger lending and infrastructure presence. India's approach is described as demand-driven and focused on skills and local ownership, but it has been criticised for slow project delivery.

- India-Africa trade is around 100 billion dollars, with India supplying generic medicines and receiving energy and minerals.
- Three India-Africa Forum Summits were held (2008, 2011, 2015); a fourth is pending.
- India pushed for the African Union's permanent G20 membership in 2023.
W: India-Africa relations are increasingly asked in GS II, often compared with China's role.
Q: How can India strengthen its partnership with Africa in the face of China's growing presence?

## nuclear-export-control-regimes | Nuclear policy and export control regimes

India conducted its first nuclear test in 1974 and a series of five tests, Operation Shakti, in May 1998, after which it declared itself a nuclear weapons state. India has not signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) of 1968, which recognises only five nuclear weapons states (those that tested before 1967) and requires others to give up nuclear weapons; India regards it as discriminatory. India has also not signed the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (1996), but maintains a voluntary moratorium on testing.

India's nuclear doctrine, adopted in 2003, is based on credible minimum deterrence and no first use: India will not use nuclear weapons first, but will retaliate massively against a nuclear attack on Indian territory or forces anywhere. India retains the option of nuclear retaliation against a major biological or chemical attack. Political control lies with the Nuclear Command Authority, headed by the Prime Minister. India's nuclear triad was completed when the submarine INS Arihant carried out its first deterrence patrol in 2018.

Export control regimes are informal groups of countries that coordinate controls on sensitive goods and technology. India joined the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR) in 2016, the Wassenaar Arrangement on conventional arms and dual-use goods in 2017, and the Australia Group on chemical and biological weapons in 2018. It has not been admitted to the Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG), which controls nuclear trade, mainly because China insists that non-NPT members be treated uniformly, linking India's case to Pakistan's. However, the NSG granted India a special waiver in September 2008, allowing civil nuclear trade, after the India-US deal and India's separation of civil and military facilities under IAEA safeguards.

India has civil nuclear cooperation agreements with several countries, including the US, France, Russia, Japan and Australia, which supplies uranium.

- India tested in 1974 and 1998; it has not signed the NPT or CTBT.
- India's doctrine is credible minimum deterrence and no first use, under the Nuclear Command Authority.
- India joined the MTCR (2016), Wassenaar (2017) and Australia Group (2018), but not the NSG.
W: Nuclear doctrine and export control regimes are frequently tested in Prelims and Mains.
Q: Why does India seek membership of the Nuclear Suppliers Group despite already having a waiver?
