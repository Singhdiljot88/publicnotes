# THEME 22
## dna-genomics-genome-india | DNA, genomics and Genome India

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) carries the genetic instructions of living things. It is a double helix, a structure described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953 using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. Each strand is a chain of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C), with A pairing with T and G with C. A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein. DNA is first copied into messenger RNA (transcription), which is then read to build a protein (translation). The human genome has about 3 billion base pairs and around 20,000 protein-coding genes.

The Human Genome Project, an international effort, produced a nearly complete sequence of the human genome in 2003. Since then the cost of sequencing a genome has fallen from billions of dollars to a few hundred, making genomics a practical tool in medicine, agriculture and forensics.

Most genetic research has been done on people of European ancestry, so it may not reflect the genetic diversity of other populations. India, with thousands of endogamous communities, is genetically very diverse, and some communities carry high rates of particular inherited disorders, such as sickle cell disease among several tribal groups. The Genome India Project, led by the Department of Biotechnology and launched in 2020, sequenced the genomes of 10,000 healthy Indians from 99 communities; the data were released to researchers in January 2025 through the Indian Biological Data Centre. The data can help identify disease-causing variants, design genetic tests, and develop medicines suited to Indian populations.

Genomics raises ethical questions: consent, privacy of genetic data, the risk of discrimination by employers or insurers, and misuse of genetic information to support ideas of caste or racial purity. India does not yet have a specific law on genetic data.

- DNA is a double helix of four bases; genes code for proteins via RNA.
- The Human Genome Project was completed in 2003; sequencing costs have collapsed since.
- The Genome India Project sequenced 10,000 Indian genomes, released in January 2025.
W: Genetics basics and the Genome India Project are frequent Prelims topics, and genomic ethics suits GS III and IV.

## gene-editing-crispr | Gene editing and CRISPR

Gene editing means making precise changes to an organism's DNA, such as deleting, inserting or altering a few bases. The most important tool is CRISPR-Cas9, adapted from a natural defence system that bacteria use against viruses. A guide RNA leads the Cas9 enzyme to a specific spot in the genome, where it cuts the DNA; the cell's repair process then disables the gene or inserts a new sequence. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing it. CRISPR is cheaper, faster and more precise than earlier methods.

In medicine, CRISPR offers treatments for genetic diseases. In 2023 the first CRISPR-based therapy, Casgevy, was approved in the United Kingdom and the United States for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia. India, which has a large number of sickle cell patients, launched the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission in 2023, and Indian scientists are developing a low-cost CRISPR therapy. In agriculture, gene editing can produce crops resistant to drought, disease and pests.

India distinguishes between kinds of gene editing. In 2022 the government exempted SDN-1 and SDN-2 genome-edited plants, which involve small changes to the plant's own genes without inserting foreign DNA, from the strict biosafety rules that apply to genetically modified (GM) organisms. In May 2025, India released two genome-edited rice varieties developed by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research: DRR Dhan 100 (Kamala), with higher yield, and Pusa DST Rice 1, with better tolerance of drought and salinity. Critics say the exemption removes needed safety checks.

The biggest ethical concern is editing human embryos, whose changes would pass to future generations. In 2018 a Chinese scientist, He Jiankui, announced the birth of gene-edited babies and was jailed. India's guidelines prohibit germline editing for reproduction.

- CRISPR-Cas9 cuts DNA at a precise spot guided by RNA; it won the 2020 Nobel Prize.
- India exempted SDN-1 and SDN-2 edited plants from GM rules in 2022 and released two genome-edited rice varieties in 2025.
- Germline editing of human embryos is prohibited in India and widely condemned.

## gm-crops-debate | GM crops: Bt cotton, GM mustard and the debate

Genetically modified (GM) crops contain genes from another organism, introduced to give them a new trait. The best-known example in India is Bt cotton, which carries a gene from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* that produces a protein toxic to bollworms. Approved in 2002, it is the only GM crop legally cultivated in India and now covers over 90% of the country's cotton area. It initially raised yields and cut pesticide use, helping India become one of the world's largest cotton producers. Later, pink bollworms developed resistance, and illegal herbicide-tolerant cotton has spread.

GM crops are regulated under rules made under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. The Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC), under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, approves field trials and commercial release. Bt brinjal was approved by GEAC in 2009, but the government imposed an indefinite moratorium in 2010 after public consultations raised safety concerns.

GM mustard, DMH-11, was developed by Delhi University scientists using a genetic system to make hybrid seeds easier to produce, which could raise yields. GEAC approved its environmental release in 2022. The approval was challenged in the Supreme Court, and in July 2024 a two-judge bench gave a split verdict; it asked the government to frame a national policy on GM crops after consultation.

Supporters argue that GM crops can raise yields, reduce pesticide use and help India cut its large edible oil imports. Opponents cite risks to biodiversity and health, the spread of herbicide resistance, contamination of non-GM crops, farmers' dependence on seed companies, and the lack of independent long-term safety studies. India imports GM soybean oil and allows GM cotton seed oil for consumption, which supporters say shows the inconsistency of the current policy.

- Bt cotton (2002) is India's only approved GM crop and covers over 90% of cotton area.
- GEAC regulates GM crops; Bt brinjal has been under moratorium since 2010.
- GM mustard DMH-11 was approved in 2022, but the Supreme Court gave a split verdict in 2024.
W: GM crops are a recurring GS III topic linking science, agriculture, environment and regulation.

## vaccines-immunisation | Vaccines and India's immunisation programme

A vaccine trains the immune system to recognise and fight a disease-causing organism without causing the illness. Types include live attenuated vaccines (weakened organisms, such as the measles and oral polio vaccines), inactivated vaccines (killed organisms, such as injectable polio vaccine and Covaxin), subunit and conjugate vaccines (using pieces of the organism, such as hepatitis B and HPV vaccines), viral vector vaccines (such as Covishield, which used a harmless adenovirus to carry instructions), and mRNA vaccines, which instruct cells to make a viral protein. India approved its first indigenous mRNA vaccine, Gemcovac, for COVID-19 in 2022.

India's Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP), started in 1985 from the Expanded Programme on Immunisation of 1978, is one of the largest in the world. It now provides free vaccines against twelve diseases, including tuberculosis (BCG), polio, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b, measles, rubella, rotavirus diarrhoea, pneumococcal pneumonia and, in some districts, Japanese encephalitis. Mission Indradhanush, launched in 2014, targeted children who had missed vaccines, and full immunisation coverage rose to about 76% in NFHS-5 (2019–21). The U-WIN digital platform records vaccinations.

India is often called the "pharmacy of the world" and is the largest vaccine producer by volume; companies such as the Serum Institute of India supply a large share of the world's vaccines. In 2022 India approved Cervavac, the first indigenous vaccine against human papillomavirus (HPV), which causes cervical cancer, a leading cancer among Indian women.

During COVID-19, India administered over 220 crore doses, mainly of Covishield and Covaxin, through the CoWIN platform, and exported vaccines under Vaccine Maitri. Challenges include vaccine hesitancy, reaching urban slums and remote areas, and maintaining the cold chain.

- Vaccines may be live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, viral vector or mRNA.
- The Universal Immunisation Programme (1985) covers twelve diseases; full coverage was about 76% in NFHS-5.
- India is the world's largest vaccine producer by volume; Cervavac is its first indigenous HPV vaccine.
W: Vaccine types and India's immunisation programmes are standard Prelims science and health questions.

## antimicrobial-resistance | Antimicrobial resistance: the silent pandemic

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) happens when bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites evolve to survive the medicines meant to kill them. Infections then become harder or impossible to treat. Bacteria that resist many antibiotics are called "superbugs". A study published in *The Lancet* estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019 and contributed to nearly 5 million. The World Health Organization lists AMR among the top global public health threats.

AMR develops naturally, but human behaviour speeds it up. Major causes include overuse and misuse of antibiotics in people, often for viral infections such as colds where they do not work; easy over-the-counter sale without prescriptions; patients not completing courses; heavy use of antibiotics in poultry, livestock and fish farming to promote growth; poor infection control in hospitals; and pharmaceutical waste released into rivers. India is one of the world's largest consumers of antibiotics, and high resistance has been reported in common infections. The NDM-1 enzyme, which makes bacteria resistant to powerful carbapenem antibiotics, was first identified in a patient who had been treated in India.

India's response includes the National Action Plan on AMR (2017), based on the One Health approach linking human, animal and environmental health; Schedule H1 of the drug rules, which requires stricter record-keeping for the sale of certain antibiotics; the "Red Line" campaign to mark prescription-only antibiotics; a ban on colistin as a growth promoter in animal feed from 2019; and surveillance networks run by the Indian Council of Medical Research. Kerala was the first state to adopt its own AMR action plan, in 2018.

Few new antibiotics are being developed because they are less profitable for drug companies, so preserving existing ones matters greatly.

- AMR directly caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019.
- Misuse in humans and animals, over-the-counter sales and pharmaceutical waste drive AMR.
- India's National Action Plan (2017) uses a One Health approach; Kerala adopted the first state plan.
W: AMR is a frequent GS II and GS III topic, linking health, agriculture and the environment.

## bioeconomy-bioe3 | Biotechnology and the bioeconomy: BioE3 policy

Biotechnology uses living organisms or their parts to make products and solve problems. It is often grouped by colour: red biotechnology for health (vaccines, diagnostics, biologic drugs), green for agriculture (improved seeds, biofertilisers), white for industry (enzymes, biofuels, biodegradable materials) and blue for marine resources.

India's bioeconomy, the value of goods and services produced using biotechnology, was estimated by the Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council (BIRAC) at about 165 billion US dollars in 2024, and the government aims for 300 billion dollars by 2030. The sector has grown with India's strength in vaccines and generic biologic medicines (biosimilars), and the number of biotech start-ups has risen sharply. The Department of Biotechnology (1986) and BIRAC (2012) fund research and start-ups.

In August 2024 the Union Cabinet approved the BioE3 policy, short for Biotechnology for Economy, Environment and Employment. It aims to promote high-performance biomanufacturing, using engineered microbes and cells to produce chemicals, materials, food and medicines in place of processes based on fossil fuels. Its focus areas include bio-based chemicals and enzymes, smart proteins and functional foods, precision biotherapeutics, climate-resilient agriculture, carbon capture, and marine and space research. It plans biomanufacturing hubs, bio-foundries and Bio-AI hubs that use artificial intelligence in biology.

Other initiatives include the National Biopharma Mission, the Biotech-KISAN programme to connect scientists with farmers, and support for ethanol blending in petrol, which reached the target of 20% (E20) in 2025.

Challenges include low private investment in research, regulatory delays, a shortage of manufacturing facilities for advanced products and limited links between universities and industry.

- Biotechnology is grouped as red (health), green (agriculture), white (industry) and blue (marine).
- India's bioeconomy was about 165 billion dollars in 2024, with a target of 300 billion by 2030.
- The BioE3 policy (August 2024) promotes biomanufacturing to replace fossil-based processes.
W: Biotechnology policy and the bioeconomy are emerging GS III topics.

## quantum-nanotechnology | Quantum technology and nanotechnology

Quantum technology uses the strange properties of particles at the smallest scales. Two ideas are central. Superposition means a quantum bit, or qubit, can be in a combination of 0 and 1 at the same time, unlike an ordinary bit. Entanglement links two particles so that measuring one instantly affects the other, however far apart they are. Quantum computers could solve certain problems, such as simulating molecules for drug discovery or optimising complex systems, far faster than classical computers. They also threaten today's encryption, which relies on problems that are hard for classical computers, which is why governments are developing "post-quantum" cryptography. Quantum communication uses entangled photons to create keys that reveal any eavesdropping, and quantum sensors can measure time, gravity and magnetic fields with extreme precision.

India approved the National Quantum Mission in April 2023 with an outlay of about Rs 6,003 crore for 2023-24 to 2030-31. It aims to develop quantum computers with 50–1,000 physical qubits over eight years, satellite-based secure quantum communication over 2,000 km between ground stations, inter-city quantum key distribution, and quantum sensors and materials. Four thematic hubs have been set up at leading institutions, including IISc Bengaluru and IIT Madras. The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for discoveries in quantum effects in electric circuits, the basis of many quantum computers.

Nanotechnology works with materials at the scale of 1 to 100 nanometres (a nanometre is a billionth of a metre). At this scale, materials behave differently: gold nanoparticles change colour, and carbon forms super-strong nanotubes and graphene. Applications include targeted drug delivery, faster electronics, water filters, stronger materials and better solar cells. India's Nano Mission was launched in 2007. Concerns include the unknown health and environmental effects of nanoparticles.

- Qubits use superposition and entanglement; quantum computers could break current encryption.
- The National Quantum Mission (2023, about Rs 6,003 crore) targets quantum computers, communication and sensors.
- Nanotechnology works at 1–100 nm, where materials gain new properties.
W: Quantum technology and nanotechnology are frequent Prelims topics, and the Quantum Mission is a likely Mains question.
Q: Why is quantum technology considered strategically important for India?

## intellectual-property-patents | Intellectual property, patents and access to medicines

Intellectual property rights (IPR) protect creations of the mind. Patents protect inventions, usually for 20 years. Copyright protects literary, artistic and musical works and software. Trademarks protect brand names and logos. Geographical Indications protect products linked to a place. Industrial designs protect the look of products. The World Trade Organization's TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights), in force from 1995, set minimum standards that members must follow.

India's Patents Act, 1970 allowed patents only on processes, not products, for food, medicines and chemicals. This let Indian companies make cheaper versions of patented drugs using different processes and built India's generic pharmaceutical industry. To comply with TRIPS, India amended the law in 2005 to allow product patents in pharmaceuticals.

The 2005 amendment included safeguards. Section 3(d) denies patents for new forms of known substances unless they show significantly enhanced efficacy, to prevent "evergreening", where companies extend patents through minor changes. In Novartis v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court used Section 3(d) to reject a patent for a new form of the cancer drug Glivec. Section 84 allows compulsory licences, letting another company make a patented product if it is not available at a reasonable price; in 2012 India granted its first compulsory licence to Natco Pharma for a generic version of Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar. Pre-grant opposition allows anyone to challenge a patent application. The TRIPS Agreement's flexibilities were confirmed by the 2001 Doha Declaration on public health.

India's National IPR Policy (2016) aims to raise awareness and speed up patent examination. Patent filings have grown, and Indian residents now file more applications than foreigners. India is regularly placed on the US Trade Representative's "Priority Watch List", which India rejects, saying its laws comply with TRIPS.

- Patents last 20 years; TRIPS set global minimum IPR standards from 1995.
- India allowed pharmaceutical product patents in 2005, with Section 3(d) against evergreening.
- Novartis (2013) and the Natco compulsory licence (2012) protected access to affordable medicines.
W: IPR, Section 3(d) and compulsory licensing are recurring GS III questions.
Q: How has India balanced intellectual property protection with access to affordable medicines?

## research-development-anrf | Research and development: ANRF and India's innovation system

India's gross expenditure on research and development (GERD) is about 0.64–0.7% of GDP, far below China (over 2%), the United States (over 3%), Israel and South Korea (around 5%). Government accounts for most of India's research spending, through agencies such as the Defence Research and Development Organisation, the Department of Atomic Energy, the Indian Space Research Organisation, the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research and the Indian Council of Agricultural Research. Private companies contribute a much smaller share than in leading research economies, where industry funds most research.

India's research output has grown. It is now among the top three countries in the number of scientific publications and has risen in the Global Innovation Index, reaching 38th place in 2025 according to WIPO. But concerns include low numbers of researchers per million people, weak links between universities and industry, slow research funding and the quality of some publications.

The Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF) was established by an act of Parliament in 2023, replacing the Science and Engineering Research Board. Chaired by the Prime Minister, it aims to fund research in universities and colleges, including those that have had little research funding, and to mobilise private and philanthropic money. The planned budget was about Rs 50,000 crore over five years, with a large share expected from non-government sources.

In July 2025, the Cabinet approved the Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) scheme with a corpus of Rs 1 lakh crore to give long-term, low-cost or zero-interest finance to private companies for research in strategic and sunrise sectors. Other programmes include the Atal Innovation Mission (2016), which set up Atal Tinkering Labs in schools and incubators, and the Prime Minister's Research Fellowship for doctoral students.

- India spends about 0.64–0.7% of GDP on R&D, mostly by government.
- The ANRF (2023) replaced SERB to fund university research and attract private money.
- The RDI scheme (2025) created a Rs 1 lakh crore corpus for private sector research.
W: India's research ecosystem is a recurring GS III question, often linked to innovation and self-reliance.
Q: Why is private sector investment in R&D low in India? How can it be raised?

## one-health-zoonoses | One Health and zoonotic diseases

Zoonotic diseases are infections that pass from animals to humans. Around 60% of known infectious diseases and about three-quarters of new and emerging ones are thought to be zoonotic. Examples include rabies, bird flu, Nipah virus, Kyasanur Forest Disease (monkey fever), anthrax, brucellosis, scrub typhus, Ebola, mpox and, most likely, COVID-19.

India faces a heavy zoonotic burden. Rabies, mostly spread by dog bites, kills thousands of Indians each year, a large share of the world's rabies deaths; the National Action Plan for Dog Mediated Rabies Elimination aims to end such deaths by 2030. Nipah virus, carried by fruit bats, has caused repeated outbreaks in Kerala since 2018, with high death rates, and earlier in West Bengal; there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment. Kyasanur Forest Disease, transmitted by ticks, affects forest areas of the Western Ghats.

The causes of rising zoonoses include deforestation and encroachment into wildlife habitats, which bring people and animals into closer contact; intensive livestock farming; trade in wildlife; urbanisation; and climate change, which shifts the range of disease-carrying insects such as mosquitoes and ticks.

The One Health approach recognises that the health of people, animals and the environment is linked, and that preventing disease requires these sectors to work together. Globally, the WHO, the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Organisation for Animal Health and the UN Environment Programme form the "Quadripartite" for One Health. In India, the government has set up a National One Health Mission to coordinate surveillance across ministries, and the foundation of the National Institute for One Health in Nagpur was laid in 2023. The National Centre for Disease Control runs the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, and the Pandemic Treaty adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2025 aims to improve global preparedness.

- Around 60% of infectious diseases and most emerging ones are zoonotic.
- Rabies, Nipah and Kyasanur Forest Disease are major zoonotic threats in India.
- The One Health approach links human, animal and environmental health; India has a One Health mission and a new national institute.
W: Zoonoses and One Health are frequent GS II and GS III topics after COVID-19.
Q: How does environmental degradation increase the risk of zoonotic diseases? What should India do?
