# THEME 6
## integration-princely-states | Integrating the princely states

At independence, about 565 princely states covered roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent's area. When British paramountcy lapsed, they were technically free to join India or Pakistan or remain independent. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Home Minister and Minister of States, and his secretary V. P. Menon persuaded almost all rulers within India's borders to sign an Instrument of Accession, which handed over defence, foreign affairs and communications. Rulers were offered privy purses and the retention of some privileges. Most states were later merged into neighbouring provinces or grouped into unions.

Three states posed problems. Junagadh, a Hindu-majority state in Gujarat whose nawab acceded to Pakistan, was taken over by India, and a plebiscite in February 1948 voted overwhelmingly to join India. Hyderabad, the largest state, ruled by the Nizam, tried to stay independent while the Razakar militia terrorised the population. In September 1948, Indian forces carried out a police action called Operation Polo, and Hyderabad acceded within days.

Jammu and Kashmir was a Muslim-majority state ruled by a Hindu maharaja, Hari Singh, who delayed his decision. In October 1947, tribal raiders backed by Pakistan invaded. The maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, and Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar. India took the matter to the United Nations in January 1948, and a ceasefire came into effect on 1 January 1949, leaving part of the state under Pakistani control.

Later, Pondicherry and other French territories were transferred to India by 1954 (formally in 1962), Goa was taken from Portugal in 1961, and Sikkim became a state in 1975.

- About 565 princely states were integrated, largely through Patel and V. P. Menon's diplomacy.
- Junagadh, Hyderabad (Operation Polo, 1948) and Kashmir required special handling.
- French territories, Goa (1961) and Sikkim (1975) joined later.

## linguistic-states | The linguistic reorganisation of states

The Congress had organised its own provincial committees by language since 1920 and had promised linguistic provinces. After Partition, however, national leaders feared that redrawing states on language lines would encourage division. The Dhar Commission (1948) and a Congress committee of Nehru, Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya (the JVP committee, 1949) both advised caution.

Popular pressure forced the issue. Potti Sriramulu, a Gandhian, went on a hunger strike for a separate state for Telugu speakers from the Madras state and died on 15 December 1952 after 58 days. Riots followed, and the government announced the creation of Andhra State, which came into being on 1 October 1953.

The government then appointed the States Reorganisation Commission under Fazl Ali, with K. M. Panikkar and H. N. Kunzru as members. Its report led to the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, which created 14 states and six union territories, broadly along linguistic lines. Further changes followed: Bombay state was split into Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960 after the Samyukta Maharashtra movement; Nagaland became a state in 1963; Punjab was divided to create Haryana, with some areas going to Himachal Pradesh, in 1966; and several north-eastern states were created in 1972.

The fear that linguistic states would weaken the nation proved largely unfounded. Most historians now argue that accommodating linguistic identity strengthened the union by giving people a stake in it. Newer states, such as Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Uttarakhand (2000) and Telangana (2014), were created more on grounds of development and regional identity than language.

- Potti Sriramulu's death in 1952 led to Andhra State in 1953.
- The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 created 14 states and 6 union territories.
- Linguistic states are now widely seen as having strengthened national unity.

## planning-era | The planning era: five-year plans and the mixed economy

After independence, India chose a mixed economy, with a large public sector alongside private enterprise, and state planning to guide investment. The Planning Commission was set up by a Cabinet resolution in March 1950, chaired by the Prime Minister. It was not a constitutional or statutory body. The National Development Council, formed in 1952, brought chief ministers into the planning process.

The First Five-Year Plan (1951–56), based on the Harrod-Domar model, focused on agriculture and irrigation, and broadly met its targets. The Second Plan (1956–61), designed by the statistician P. C. Mahalanobis, emphasised rapid industrialisation through heavy industry in the public sector, such as steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur. The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 reserved key industries for the state.

The Third Plan (1961–66) was disrupted by the 1962 war with China, the 1965 war with Pakistan and droughts, leading to a "plan holiday" of three annual plans (1966–69). Later plans focused on poverty (the Fifth Plan's "garibi hatao"), employment and, from the Eighth Plan (1992–97), liberalisation. Plans became increasingly indicative rather than directive. The Twelfth Plan (2012–17) was the last.

On 1 January 2015, the Planning Commission was replaced by the NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India), a think tank meant to promote cooperative federalism. Unlike the Planning Commission, it does not allocate funds to states. Supporters say this fits a market economy; critics say it removed a forum for states to bargain for resources.

- The Planning Commission (1950) was set up by Cabinet resolution, not the Constitution.
- The Mahalanobis model of the Second Plan prioritised heavy industry.
- NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015 and does not allocate funds.

## nehru-nam | Nehru's foreign policy and Non-Alignment

Jawaharlal Nehru, Prime Minister and Foreign Minister from 1947 to 1964, shaped India's early foreign policy. In a world divided between the United States and the Soviet Union, he chose non-alignment: India would not join either military bloc, and would judge each issue on its merits. Non-alignment was not neutrality; India took positions, and sought aid and friendship from both sides.

Nehru championed decolonisation and opposition to racial discrimination, raising apartheid in South Africa at the United Nations as early as 1946. He hosted the Asian Relations Conference in Delhi in 1947. In 1954, India and China signed an agreement on trade with Tibet that included the Panchsheel, five principles of peaceful coexistence: mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference in internal affairs, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence. India played a key role at the Bandung Conference of Asian and African nations in 1955.

The Non-Aligned Movement was formally founded at Belgrade in 1961. Its founding leaders were Nehru, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, Sukarno of Indonesia and Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana. It grew into the largest grouping of states outside the UN, with around 120 members today.

Non-alignment gave newly independent India a voice beyond its military or economic weight. But the 1962 war with China exposed weaknesses in defence preparedness, and India moved closer to the Soviet Union in later decades, signing a treaty of friendship in 1971. Today's policy of strategic autonomy is often described as a descendant of non-alignment.

- Non-alignment meant staying out of Cold War blocs while taking independent positions.
- The Panchsheel (1954) set out five principles of peaceful coexistence.
- NAM was founded at Belgrade in 1961 by Nehru, Nasser, Tito, Sukarno and Nkrumah.

## wars-1962-1965-1971 | India's wars: 1962, 1965 and 1971

India fought three major wars in its first quarter-century. The 1962 war with China grew out of disputed boundaries in the western sector (Aksai Chin) and eastern sector (the McMahon Line in what is now Arunachal Pradesh), tensions after the Dalai Lama fled to India in 1959, and India's "forward policy" of setting up posts in contested areas. In October-November 1962, Chinese forces advanced in both sectors before declaring a unilateral ceasefire and withdrawing in the east. The defeat led to the resignation of Defence Minister V. K. Krishna Menon and a major expansion of India's armed forces.

In 1965, Pakistan launched Operation Gibraltar, sending infiltrators into Kashmir, followed by an armoured attack in the Chhamb sector. India responded by crossing the international border towards Lahore and Sialkot. Major tank battles, including at Asal Uttar, followed before a UN-brokered ceasefire. Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri and Pakistan's President Ayub Khan signed the Tashkent Declaration in January 1966, with Soviet mediation; Shastri died in Tashkent the next day. His slogan "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan" dates from this period.

The 1971 war arose from the crisis in East Pakistan, where the Pakistani army's crackdown after the Awami League's election victory drove around ten million refugees into India. India signed a treaty of friendship with the Soviet Union in August 1971. After Pakistan's air strikes on Indian bases on 3 December 1971, India went to war. On 16 December, about 93,000 Pakistani personnel surrendered in Dhaka, and Bangladesh became independent. The Simla Agreement of 1972 between Indira Gandhi and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto committed both sides to resolve disputes bilaterally and converted the ceasefire line in Kashmir into the Line of Control.

- The 1962 war with China exposed defence weaknesses and changed India's security policy.
- The 1965 war ended with the Tashkent Declaration (1966).
- The 1971 war created Bangladesh; the Simla Agreement (1972) set up the Line of Control.

## emergency-1975 | The Emergency, 1975 to 1977

On 12 June 1975, the Allahabad High Court set aside Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's election to the Lok Sabha for electoral malpractice, in a case brought by Raj Narain. The Supreme Court granted a conditional stay. At the same time, a movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan, which had grown out of student protests in Gujarat and Bihar, demanded her resignation and called for "total revolution".

On the night of 25 June 1975, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed proclaimed a national emergency under Article 352 on the ground of "internal disturbance", on the advice of the Prime Minister, before the Cabinet had been consulted. Fundamental rights were suspended, opposition leaders including J. P. Narayan, Morarji Desai and Atal Bihari Vajpayee were jailed under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), and the press was censored. In the ADM Jabalpur case (1976), the Supreme Court held that detainees could not challenge their detention while the emergency lasted; Justice H. R. Khanna's lone dissent became famous. A slum clearance and forced sterilisation drive, associated with Sanjay Gandhi, caused deep resentment.

The 42nd Amendment of 1976 made sweeping changes. It added "socialist", "secular" and "integrity" to the Preamble, added fundamental duties, and tried to curb judicial review.

Elections were called in January 1977. The Janata Party, a coalition of opposition groups, won, and Morarji Desai became the first non-Congress Prime Minister. The Janata government's 44th Amendment of 1978 replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for emergency, required written Cabinet advice to the President, and made the rights under Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable. The Supreme Court formally overruled ADM Jabalpur in its 2017 Puttaswamy privacy judgment, and in 2024 the government declared 25 June as Samvidhan Hatya Diwas.

- The Emergency was declared on 25 June 1975 under Article 352 on grounds of internal disturbance.
- Civil liberties were suspended, and the 42nd Amendment made sweeping constitutional changes.
- The 44th Amendment (1978) made emergencies harder to declare and protected Articles 20 and 21.

## indira-gandhi-era | The Indira Gandhi years: populism and centralisation

Indira Gandhi became Prime Minister in 1966. The Congress had been weakened in the 1967 elections, losing power in several states, and was split in 1969 between her supporters and the older party bosses known as the Syndicate. She moved left to build her own base.

In July 1969, fourteen major commercial banks were nationalised by ordinance, to extend credit to agriculture, small businesses and rural areas. Six more were nationalised in 1980. In 1971 the 26th Amendment abolished the privy purses and privileges of former princes. With the slogan "Garibi hatao" (remove poverty), she won a large majority in the 1971 Lok Sabha election, and the victory in the Bangladesh war that December made her dominant. In May 1974, India conducted its first nuclear test at Pokhran, called a "peaceful nuclear explosion" and codenamed Smiling Buddha. Sikkim became a state of India in 1975.

Her years also saw the centralisation of power. Chief ministers were often chosen by the party high command, President's rule was used frequently against opposition governments, and the commitment of the judiciary and bureaucracy to government policy was openly sought. In 1973 three senior judges were superseded in appointing the Chief Justice, after the Kesavananda Bharati judgment limited Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

After the Emergency and her defeat in 1977, Indira Gandhi returned to power in 1980. Her second term was dominated by the crisis in Punjab. She was assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, after Operation Blue Star, the army action in the Golden Temple.

- Bank nationalisation (1969) and abolition of privy purses (1971) were key populist measures.
- India conducted its first nuclear test at Pokhran in 1974.
- Power became centralised, culminating in the Emergency.

## punjab-assam-north-east-accords | The 1980s: Punjab, Assam and the north-eastern accords

The 1980s tested Indian unity in several regions. In Punjab, demands for greater state autonomy, set out in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution of 1973, became entangled with militancy led by Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale. In June 1984, the army carried out Operation Blue Star to remove armed militants from the Golden Temple in Amritsar, damaging the Akal Takht and causing many deaths. Indira Gandhi's assassination that October was followed by anti-Sikh violence in Delhi and elsewhere in which thousands of Sikhs were killed. The Rajiv Gandhi-Longowal Accord of 1985 sought a settlement, but Sant Harchand Singh Longowal was assassinated soon after, and militancy continued until the early 1990s.

In Assam, the All Assam Students' Union (AASU) led a movement from 1979 to 1985 against illegal immigration from Bangladesh, demanding the detection and deportation of foreigners. Violence included the Nellie massacre of 1983. The Assam Accord of August 1985 set 25 March 1971 as the cut-off date: people who entered after that date would be identified as foreigners. Section 6A of the Citizenship Act was inserted to implement this, and the Supreme Court upheld it in 2024. The accord also promised safeguards for Assamese identity.

In Mizoram, the Mizo National Front led by Laldenga had fought an insurgency since 1966. The Mizo Accord of 1986 ended it, Mizoram became a state in 1987, and Laldenga became chief minister. The accord is often cited as one of India's most successful peace settlements.

These accords showed both the potential and the limits of negotiated settlements: they brought peace where they were implemented in good faith, but left lasting disputes where commitments were incomplete.

- Operation Blue Star (1984) and the Rajiv-Longowal Accord (1985) marked the Punjab crisis.
- The Assam Accord (1985) set 25 March 1971 as the cut-off date for identifying foreigners.
- The Mizo Accord (1986) is considered among India's most successful peace deals.

## mandal-coalition-era | Mandal, mandir and the coalition era

The late 1980s and 1990s transformed Indian politics. The Mandal Commission, appointed in 1979 under B. P. Mandal, recommended in its 1980 report that 27% of central government jobs be reserved for Other Backward Classes (OBCs). The report lay unimplemented for a decade, until Prime Minister V. P. Singh announced its implementation in August 1990. Protests by upper-caste students followed, including self-immolations. In Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992), the Supreme Court upheld OBC reservation but excluded the "creamy layer" and set a general ceiling of 50% on total reservations.

At the same time, the movement to build a Ram temple at Ayodhya on the site of the Babri Masjid grew. L. K. Advani's rath yatra in 1990 mobilised support. On 6 December 1992 the mosque was demolished by kar sevaks, followed by communal riots in Mumbai and elsewhere. In 2019, the Supreme Court awarded the disputed land for a temple and directed that five acres be given for a mosque elsewhere; the Ram temple was consecrated in January 2024.

These two developments, often summarised as "Mandal and mandir", reshaped party politics. Backward-caste parties such as the Samajwadi Party, Rashtriya Janata Dal and Bahujan Samaj Party grew strong in north India, while the BJP rose to become a national party. The Congress's dominance ended. From 1989 to 2014, no single party won a Lok Sabha majority, and India was governed by coalitions such as the National Front, the United Front, the BJP-led National Democratic Alliance and the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance.

The coalition era increased the influence of regional parties and states in national politics, and showed that diverse coalitions could govern and complete full terms.

- The Mandal Commission's OBC reservation was implemented in 1990 and upheld in Indra Sawhney (1992) with a 50% ceiling.
- The demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992 and the Ayodhya verdict of 2019 reshaped religious politics.
- From 1989 to 2014, no party won a Lok Sabha majority alone.

## economic-reforms-1991 | The 1991 economic reforms

In 1991 India faced a severe balance of payments crisis. The Gulf War had raised oil prices and cut remittances from Gulf workers, political instability had shaken confidence, and foreign exchange reserves fell to barely enough for about two weeks of imports. India pledged gold with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan to raise emergency funds and approached the IMF for a loan.

The new government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, used the crisis to launch structural reforms, often summarised as LPG: liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. The rupee was devalued in two steps in July 1991. The New Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 abolished industrial licensing for all but a few industries, reduced the sectors reserved for the public sector, and eased limits on large companies under the MRTP Act. The Union Budget the same day cut import duties and opened the door to foreign investment, allowing automatic approval for up to 51% foreign equity in many industries. Over the following years, quantitative restrictions on imports were removed, the rupee became convertible on the current account (1994), SEBI was given statutory powers (1992) and the financial sector was reformed on the advice of the Narasimham Committee.

The results were significant. Growth accelerated, foreign exchange reserves rose, and sectors such as information technology and telecommunications boomed. Critics point to uneven benefits, slow job creation in manufacturing, agricultural distress and rising inequality.

The reforms are often described as a turning point comparable to independence in economic terms, and most later governments, of all parties, continued them.

- A balance of payments crisis in 1991 forced India to pledge gold and seek IMF help.
- The New Industrial Policy (24 July 1991) ended most industrial licensing; the rupee was devalued and trade opened.
- Growth accelerated, though critics highlight uneven gains and weak job creation.
