# THEME 5
## congress-1885-moderates | The founding of the Congress and the moderate phase

The Indian National Congress held its first session in Bombay in December 1885, with 72 delegates and W. C. Bonnerjee as president. A retired British civil servant, Allan Octavian Hume, helped organise it. Some historians once argued that Hume meant it as a "safety valve" to release Indian discontent harmlessly; most now see the Congress as the product of growing nationalist networks, such as the Indian Association of Surendranath Banerjea and the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, which used Hume's help to gain official tolerance.

The early leaders, called moderates, included Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Surendranath Banerjea and M. G. Ranade. They believed in British justice and used constitutional methods: petitions, speeches, resolutions and lobbying in Britain. Their demands included more Indian representation in legislative councils, holding the civil service examination in India as well as London, separating the judiciary from the executive, reducing military spending and land revenue, and protecting Indian industry.

Their greatest contribution was economic. Naoroji's drain theory and the work of R. C. Dutt and Ranade exposed the economic exploitation behind British rule, undermining the claim that colonialism benefited India. The moderates also built a national platform that brought together leaders from different regions.

Their achievements in policy were limited: the Indian Councils Act of 1892 slightly enlarged councils and allowed indirect election. Critics, especially younger nationalists, argued that petitioning had failed and that a more assertive approach was needed.

- The Congress first met in Bombay in 1885 under W. C. Bonnerjee.
- Moderates used constitutional methods and trusted British fairness.
- Their economic critique of colonialism was their most lasting contribution.
W: The founding of the Congress and the moderates' methods and achievements are frequently tested.
Q: Evaluate the contribution of the moderates to the Indian national movement.

## partition-of-bengal-swadeshi | Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi movement

In 1905, Viceroy Lord Curzon partitioned Bengal, officially for administrative convenience, as the province was very large. Nationalists saw it as an attempt to divide Bengali Hindus and Muslims and weaken the centre of Indian nationalism. The partition took effect on 16 October 1905, observed as a day of mourning; Rabindranath Tagore asked people to tie rakhis to each other as a symbol of unity.

The Swadeshi movement that followed used new methods: boycott of British goods, especially cloth, public burning of foreign goods, promotion of Indian-made products, national schools and colleges, and self-help organisations called samitis. Indian enterprises grew, including the Bengal Chemicals factory of P. C. Ray. Songs such as Tagore's *Amar Sonar Bangla*, now Bangladesh's national anthem, spread the message.

The movement brought the extremists, or assertive nationalists, to the fore: Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai (together known as Lal-Bal-Pal) and Aurobindo Ghosh. They demanded swaraj and mass action rather than petitions. Tilak's slogan "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" became famous. Differences between moderates and extremists led to a split at the Surat session of the Congress in 1907.

The government responded with repression: Tilak was sentenced to six years in Mandalay in 1908. Under continued pressure, the partition was annulled in 1911, and the capital was moved from Calcutta to Delhi. The movement had limits, as it reached few peasants and Muslims, but it introduced methods that Gandhi later used on a much larger scale.

- Curzon's 1905 partition of Bengal triggered the Swadeshi and boycott movement.
- The Congress split into moderates and extremists at Surat in 1907.
- The partition was annulled in 1911, and the capital moved to Delhi.
W: The Swadeshi movement's methods and the Surat split are standard questions in modern history.

## muslim-league-home-rule | The Muslim League, separate electorates and Home Rule

The All-India Muslim League was founded in Dhaka in December 1906, led by the Aga Khan and Nawab Salimullah of Dhaka, among others. Earlier that year, a delegation of Muslim leaders (the Simla deputation) had asked Viceroy Minto for separate representation. The Indian Councils Act of 1909, known as the Morley-Minto reforms, enlarged the legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims, in which only Muslims could vote for Muslim candidates. Nationalists saw this as a deliberate attempt to divide communities, and separate electorates became a long-term source of communal politics.

Relations between the Congress and the League improved during the First World War. In the Lucknow Pact of 1916, the two agreed on a joint scheme of reforms, and the Congress accepted separate electorates. The same session reunited the moderates and extremists in the Congress. Tilak and Muhammad Ali Jinnah were key figures in this cooperation.

In 1916 two Home Rule Leagues were also founded: one by Tilak in April, working in Maharashtra, Karnataka and central India, and one by the Theosophist Annie Besant in September, working elsewhere. They demanded self-government within the British Empire, on the lines of Ireland's Home Rule movement, and spread nationalist ideas through newspapers, discussion groups and libraries, reaching new areas and younger people. Besant's internment in 1917 increased their popularity.

The British responded with the Montagu Declaration of August 1917, promising "the progressive realisation of responsible government in India". This led to the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms and the Government of India Act 1919, which introduced dyarchy in the provinces.

- The Muslim League was founded in 1906; the 1909 reforms introduced separate electorates.
- The Lucknow Pact (1916) brought the Congress and League together.
- Tilak's and Besant's Home Rule Leagues (1916) spread nationalism and prompted the 1917 Montagu Declaration.
W: Separate electorates, the Lucknow Pact and the Home Rule movement are high-frequency Prelims topics.
Q: How did the introduction of separate electorates in 1909 shape communal politics in India?

## gandhi-early-satyagrahas | Gandhi's return and the early satyagrahas

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India in January 1915 after two decades in South Africa, where he had developed satyagraha, a method of non-violent resistance grounded in truth and the willingness to suffer rather than inflict harm. On the advice of his political mentor Gokhale, he spent a year travelling across India.

His first campaigns in India were local. In Champaran, Bihar, in 1917, he took up the cause of peasants forced by European planters to grow indigo on part of their land under the tinkathia system. His inquiry led to a government committee and the end of the system. In Ahmedabad in 1918, he supported textile mill workers in a wage dispute, using a hunger strike for the first time in India. In Kheda, Gujarat, in 1918, he led peasants in refusing to pay land revenue after crop failure; Vallabhbhai Patel emerged as a leader here.

The first all-India campaign came in 1919 against the Rowlatt Act, which allowed detention without trial. Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal. In Punjab, protests led to the arrest of local leaders. On 13 April 1919, in Amritsar's Jallianwala Bagh, troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer fired without warning on a peaceful crowd that had gathered on the day of the Baisakhi festival. Official figures put the dead at 379; Indian estimates were much higher. Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest. The Hunter Committee inquiry was widely seen as a whitewash.

These events turned Gandhi from a cooperator into an opponent of British rule, and set the stage for the Non-Cooperation movement.

- Gandhi's first Indian satyagrahas were in Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad and Kheda (1918).
- The Rowlatt satyagraha of 1919 was his first all-India campaign.
- The Jallianwala Bagh massacre on 13 April 1919 transformed Indian opinion.
W: Gandhi's early campaigns and the Rowlatt-Jallianwala sequence are among the most tested events.
Q: Why did Gandhi's early local satyagrahas prove important for his later leadership of the national movement?

## non-cooperation-khilafat | The Non-Cooperation and Khilafat movements

After the First World War, Indian Muslims were angered by the harsh treatment of the defeated Ottoman Sultan, whom many regarded as the Khalifa, the spiritual head of Islam. The Khilafat movement, led by the Ali brothers (Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali), Maulana Azad and others, demanded the protection of the Khalifa's position. Gandhi saw an opportunity to unite Hindus and Muslims against British rule.

The Congress launched the Non-Cooperation movement in 1920, approved at a special session in Calcutta in September and confirmed at the Nagpur session in December. Its programme included surrendering titles and honours, boycotting government schools, colleges, law courts and legislative councils, boycotting foreign cloth and promoting khadi and the charkha. Gandhi promised swaraj within a year. The Nagpur session also reorganised the Congress on a linguistic basis and opened it to mass membership.

The response was enormous. Thousands of students left government institutions for national schools such as Jamia Millia Islamia and Kashi Vidyapith. Lawyers including Motilal Nehru and C. R. Das gave up their practices. Foreign cloth was burnt in bonfires. Peasants, workers and tribal communities joined, sometimes going beyond Gandhi's programme.

In February 1922, at Chauri Chaura in the United Provinces, a crowd set fire to a police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi called off the movement, believing the people were not ready for non-violence. Many leaders disagreed. Gandhi was arrested and sentenced to six years in prison. The Khilafat issue ended when Turkey abolished the caliphate in 1924. Within the Congress, C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru formed the Swaraj Party in 1923 to contest council elections.

- Non-Cooperation (1920–22) combined with the Khilafat movement to unite Hindus and Muslims.
- It used boycott of institutions, titles and foreign cloth, and promoted khadi.
- Gandhi withdrew it after Chauri Chaura in February 1922.
W: The programme, spread and withdrawal of Non-Cooperation are core topics for Prelims and Mains.
Q: Was Gandhi right to withdraw the Non-Cooperation movement after Chauri Chaura? Present both sides.

## civil-disobedience | From the Simon Commission to Civil Disobedience

In 1927 the British government appointed the Simon Commission to review India's constitutional progress. It had no Indian members, which outraged Indian opinion. When it arrived in 1928, it was met with black flags and the slogan "Simon go back". In Lahore, Lala Lajpat Rai was injured in a police lathi charge and died weeks later. Indian leaders responded with their own constitutional proposal, the Nehru Report of 1928, which asked for dominion status.

Younger leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose pressed for full independence. At the Lahore session in December 1929, the Congress declared purna swaraj, complete independence, as its goal, and 26 January 1930 was celebrated as Independence Day.

To launch the struggle, Gandhi chose salt, a necessity of every household, taxed by the government. On 12 March 1930 he set out from Sabarmati Ashram with 78 followers and walked about 385 km to Dandi on the Gujarat coast, where he broke the salt law on 6 April. Civil disobedience spread across India: salt was made illegally, foreign cloth and liquor shops were picketed, and peasants refused to pay taxes. Women took part in large numbers. In the North-West Frontier, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars joined, and in the North-East, the young Naga leader Rani Gaidinliu resisted.

The Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March 1931 suspended the movement, and Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London, which failed. The movement resumed in 1932 and was suppressed. The Communal Award of 1932 gave separate electorates to the depressed classes; Gandhi fasted against it, leading to the Poona Pact with B. R. Ambedkar, which replaced separate electorates with reserved seats.

- The all-white Simon Commission (1927) was boycotted, and the Nehru Report (1928) followed.
- The Dandi March (12 March–6 April 1930) began Civil Disobedience.
- The Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931) and the Poona Pact (1932) were key outcomes.
W: The Dandi March, Round Table Conferences and Poona Pact are frequently asked in both Prelims and Mains.
Q: Why was salt an effective symbol for a mass movement? What does this tell us about Gandhi's political strategy?

## revolutionary-nationalism | Revolutionary nationalists

Alongside the Gandhian movement, revolutionary groups believed armed action was needed to end British rule. The first wave emerged after the partition of Bengal. Groups such as the Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar in Bengal and the Abhinav Bharat society founded by V. D. Savarkar in Maharashtra carried out attacks on officials. Khudiram Bose was hanged in 1908, aged eighteen, after a failed attempt on a British magistrate. The Alipore bomb case put Aurobindo Ghosh on trial; he was acquitted.

Abroad, the Ghadar Party was founded in 1913 on the Pacific coast of North America by Indian immigrants, with Lala Har Dayal and Sohan Singh Bhakna among its leaders. During the First World War, it tried to start an uprising in Punjab, which failed. Its newspaper, *Ghadar*, spread revolutionary ideas among the diaspora.

A second wave followed the end of Non-Cooperation. The Hindustan Republican Association carried out the Kakori train robbery in 1925; Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan and others were executed. In 1928 it became the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) under Chandrashekhar Azad and Bhagat Singh, adding socialist goals. Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev, with Rajguru, killed police officer J. P. Saunders in 1928 to avenge Lajpat Rai's death. In April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw harmless bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly and courted arrest to use the trial as propaganda. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged on 23 March 1931. Azad died in a gunfight in Allahabad in 1931. In Bengal, Surya Sen led the Chittagong armoury raid in 1930.

The revolutionaries did not overthrow British rule, but their courage inspired many, and Bhagat Singh's writings on socialism and atheism remain widely read.

- Early revolutionaries included Anushilan Samiti, Jugantar and Abhinav Bharat; the Ghadar Party was founded abroad in 1913.
- The HSRA under Azad and Bhagat Singh combined revolution with socialism.
- Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were executed on 23 March 1931.
W: Revolutionary organisations, events and leaders appear regularly in Prelims.
Q: Assess the contribution of revolutionary nationalism to India's freedom struggle.

## gov-of-india-act-1935 | The Government of India Act 1935 and the 1937 elections

The Government of India Act 1935 was the longest act the British Parliament had passed. It drew on the Simon Commission, the Round Table Conferences and a White Paper of 1933. Much of its structure later found its way into the Constitution of India.

The act proposed an All-India Federation of British provinces and princely states, with power divided into three lists: federal, provincial and concurrent. The federation never came into being because not enough princes joined. It abolished dyarchy in the provinces and introduced provincial autonomy: elected Indian ministers responsible to provincial legislatures would run all provincial subjects, though governors kept special powers. Dyarchy was introduced at the centre instead, but this part was never implemented. The act also set up a Federal Court (established in 1937) and a Reserve Bank of India (which had been created separately by the RBI Act of 1934), extended separate electorates, and enlarged the electorate to roughly a tenth of the population. Burma was separated from India.

The Congress criticised the act as a "charter of slavery" but contested the 1937 provincial elections. It won majorities in five provinces and was the largest party in others, eventually forming ministries in eight of eleven provinces. The Muslim League performed poorly, even in Muslim-majority provinces. The Congress ministries introduced reforms in areas such as tenancy, prohibition and civil liberties.

The ministries resigned in 1939 when the Viceroy declared India a party to the Second World War without consulting Indian leaders. The Muslim League celebrated their resignation as "Deliverance Day".

- The 1935 Act introduced provincial autonomy and proposed an All-India Federation that never formed.
- Its three lists, Federal Court and governor's powers influenced the Constitution of India.
- The Congress formed ministries after the 1937 elections and resigned in 1939.
W: The 1935 Act is a key source of the Indian Constitution and a frequent Prelims and Mains topic.
Q: Which features of the Government of India Act 1935 were carried into the Constitution of India, and why?

## quit-india-ina | Quit India and the Indian National Army

In March 1942, as Japan advanced through South-East Asia, Britain sent Sir Stafford Cripps to win Indian support for the war. The Cripps Mission offered dominion status after the war and the right of provinces to stay out of a new union. The Congress rejected it; Gandhi reportedly called it a "post-dated cheque".

On 8 August 1942, at the Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, the All-India Congress Committee passed the Quit India resolution. Gandhi gave the call "Do or die". The next morning, the entire Congress leadership was arrested. Without leaders, the movement became a spontaneous uprising: railway lines and telegraph wires were cut, police stations were attacked, and parallel governments were set up in places such as Ballia, Tamluk and Satara. Young leaders such as Aruna Asaf Ali, who hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank, Jayaprakash Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia worked underground, and Usha Mehta ran a secret Congress radio. The movement was crushed within months, with around a thousand killed by official figures (far more by nationalist estimates) and tens of thousands arrested, but it showed that British rule could no longer rely on Indian consent.

Subhas Chandra Bose, who had been Congress president in 1938 and 1939, escaped from house arrest in 1941 and sought help from Germany and Japan. In 1943 he took command of the Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj), formed from Indian prisoners of war, and proclaimed the Provisional Government of Free India in Singapore. The INA fought alongside Japan on the Imphal-Kohima front in 1944 but was defeated. The trials of INA officers at Delhi's Red Fort in 1945 caused nationwide protests, and the Royal Indian Navy mutiny of February 1946 showed that the armed forces could no longer be relied on.

- The Quit India resolution was passed on 8 August 1942 with the call "Do or die".
- Leaderless, the movement became a mass uprising and was suppressed with force.
- Bose's INA, the 1945 Red Fort trials and the 1946 naval mutiny weakened British control.
W: Quit India and the INA are core topics for both Prelims and Mains, often asked as the final phase of the struggle.
Q: How did the INA trials and the naval mutiny of 1946 hasten the end of British rule?

## independence-and-partition | The road to independence and Partition

After the Second World War, the new Labour government in Britain wanted to leave India. Elections in 1945–46 showed that the Congress dominated the general seats and the Muslim League had won almost all Muslim seats, strengthening its demand for Pakistan, first formally made in the Lahore resolution of 1940.

The Cabinet Mission of 1946 proposed a united India with a weak centre and groups of provinces with considerable autonomy. Both parties initially accepted it, but disagreed on its interpretation. The League called for "Direct Action" on 16 August 1946, which led to large-scale communal killings in Calcutta, followed by violence in Noakhali, Bihar and elsewhere. An interim government led by Nehru took office in September 1946, and the Constituent Assembly first met in December 1946, boycotted by the League.

Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, arrived in March 1947. His plan of 3 June 1947 accepted Partition. Punjab and Bengal would be divided, and the date of transfer was brought forward to 15 August 1947. The Indian Independence Act was passed by the British Parliament in July 1947. Princely states were free to join India or Pakistan. Sir Cyril Radcliffe, who had never visited India, drew the boundaries in about five weeks; the awards were announced only on 17 August, after independence.

Partition caused one of the largest migrations in history, with around 15 million people displaced and between several hundred thousand and two million killed, by different estimates. Gandhi spent independence day in Calcutta trying to stop communal violence, and was assassinated on 30 January 1948.

- The Cabinet Mission (1946) failed, and Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) triggered communal violence.
- The Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947) and the Indian Independence Act led to independence and Partition on 15 August 1947.
- Partition displaced around 15 million people amid massive violence.
W: The sequence from the Cabinet Mission to the Independence Act is a standard Prelims topic, and Partition is a frequent Mains theme.
Q: Was Partition inevitable by 1946? Examine the roles of the Congress, the Muslim League and the British.
