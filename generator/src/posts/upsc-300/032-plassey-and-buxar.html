Plassey and Buxar: how the Company won Bengal

Note 032 · Theme 4: Colonial India & 1857 · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Bengal was the richest province of the late Mughal empire, and by the 1750s it was ruled almost independently by its nawabs. The young nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah, who took power in 1756, objected to the Company's fortification of Calcutta and misuse of trade privileges (dastaks). He captured Calcutta in 1756. The story of the "Black Hole", in which British prisoners died in a cramped cell, was later used as propaganda, though its scale is disputed by historians.

Robert Clive retook Calcutta and, at the Battle of Plassey on 23 June 1757, defeated Siraj. The battle was decided less by fighting than by conspiracy: Siraj's commander Mir Jafar had agreed to betray him in return for the throne. Mir Jafar became nawab, paid huge sums to the Company and its officers, and was later replaced by Mir Qasim.

Mir Qasim tried to reassert control, moved his capital to Monghyr and abolished internal duties for everyone, removing the Company's unfair advantage. War followed. At the Battle of Buxar in October 1764, the Company defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. Buxar was a genuine military victory and more decisive than Plassey.

By the Treaty of Allahabad in 1765, Shah Alam granted the Company the diwani, the right to collect revenue, of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Clive set up a "dual government" in which the Company had revenue power and the nawab had responsibility for administration without resources. The result was misrule, and the famine of 1770 killed an estimated one-third of Bengal's population.

Key takeaways

  • Plassey (1757) was won largely through Mir Jafar's betrayal.
  • Buxar (1764) was the decisive military victory over three Indian powers.
  • The diwani of 1765 made the Company the real ruler of Bengal, and the 1770 famine followed dual government.

Why it matters for UPSC: Plassey, Buxar and the diwani are among the most tested events in modern Indian history.

Practice question: "Plassey was a transaction; Buxar was a battle." Examine this statement.
