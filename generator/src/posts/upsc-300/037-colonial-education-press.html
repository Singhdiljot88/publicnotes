Colonial education and the press

Note 037 · Theme 4: Colonial India & 1857 · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The Company at first left education to Indian institutions, but by the 1830s a debate arose between Orientalists, who favoured learning in Sanskrit, Persian and Arabic, and Anglicists, who favoured Western learning in English. Thomas Babington Macaulay's minute of 1835 argued for English education, aiming to create "a class of persons Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes". Bentinck's government adopted English as the medium of higher education.

Wood's Despatch of 1854, sometimes called the Magna Carta of English education in India, proposed a structured system from primary schools to universities, with vernacular languages at the primary level and grants-in-aid for private schools. The first universities were set up in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1857. The Hunter Commission of 1882 focused on primary and secondary education. Lord Curzon's Indian Universities Act of 1904 increased government control and was resented by nationalists.

The English-educated middle class that emerged did not become the loyal intermediaries Macaulay had imagined. Instead, it produced social reformers and the leaders of the national movement, who used Western ideas of liberty and rights to criticise colonial rule.

The press played a similar role. Hicky's Bengal Gazette (1780) was the first newspaper in India. Indian-language papers grew rapidly in the nineteenth century, and nationalists such as Tilak (Kesari) and Gandhi (Young India) used newspapers to mobilise opinion. The government responded with restrictions, including Lord Lytton's Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which targeted Indian-language papers and was repealed in 1882.

Key takeaways

  • Macaulay's minute (1835) and Wood's Despatch (1854) shaped colonial education.
  • The first universities were founded in 1857 in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.
  • The press became a tool of nationalism, despite laws such as the Vernacular Press Act (1878).

Why it matters for UPSC: Education policies and press laws are frequently tested, and they explain the rise of the nationalist middle class.

Practice question: Did colonial education policy achieve its intended goals? Discuss its unintended consequences.
