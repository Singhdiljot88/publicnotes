The 1991 economic reforms

Note 060 · Theme 6: India after 1947 · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In 1991 India faced a severe balance of payments crisis. The Gulf War had raised oil prices and cut remittances from Gulf workers, political instability had shaken confidence, and foreign exchange reserves fell to barely enough for about two weeks of imports. India pledged gold with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan to raise emergency funds and approached the IMF for a loan.

The new government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, used the crisis to launch structural reforms, often summarised as LPG: liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. The rupee was devalued in two steps in July 1991. The New Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 abolished industrial licensing for all but a few industries, reduced the sectors reserved for the public sector, and eased limits on large companies under the MRTP Act. The Union Budget the same day cut import duties and opened the door to foreign investment, allowing automatic approval for up to 51% foreign equity in many industries. Over the following years, quantitative restrictions on imports were removed, the rupee became convertible on the current account (1994), SEBI was given statutory powers (1992) and the financial sector was reformed on the advice of the Narasimham Committee.

The results were significant. Growth accelerated, foreign exchange reserves rose, and sectors such as information technology and telecommunications boomed. Critics point to uneven benefits, slow job creation in manufacturing, agricultural distress and rising inequality.

The reforms are often described as a turning point comparable to independence in economic terms, and most later governments, of all parties, continued them.

Key takeaways

  • A balance of payments crisis in 1991 forced India to pledge gold and seek IMF help.
  • The New Industrial Policy (24 July 1991) ended most industrial licensing; the rupee was devalued and trade opened.
  • Growth accelerated, though critics highlight uneven gains and weak job creation.

Why it matters for UPSC: The 1991 reforms are central to GS III economy and post-independence history questions.
