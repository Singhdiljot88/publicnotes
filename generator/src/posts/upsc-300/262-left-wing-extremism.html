Left-wing extremism: rise and decline

Note 262 · Theme 27: Internal security · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Left-wing extremism (LWE), commonly called Naxalism, takes its name from Naxalbari in West Bengal, where a peasant uprising led by Charu Mazumdar and Kanu Sanyal began in 1967. The movement drew on Maoist ideology, aiming to overthrow the state through armed struggle. After splits and repression, the main armed groups merged in 2004 to form the Communist Party of India (Maoist), which built a "Red Corridor" across forested tribal areas of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Bihar. In 2006 Prime Minister Manmohan Singh called it the single biggest internal security challenge facing India.

The movement's roots lay in real grievances: landlessness, displacement by mines and dams, denial of forest rights, poor schools and health care, and the near-absence of the state in remote tribal areas. Its violence included attacks on security forces, such as the killing of 76 CRPF personnel at Dantewada in 2010, and the Darbha valley attack of 2013 that killed senior Congress leaders in Chhattisgarh.

The government's response combined security operations with development. The 2015 National Policy and Action Plan included more security camps and roads in affected areas, the Special Central Assistance for the most affected districts, mobile towers, skill development, and surrender and rehabilitation schemes. Violence declined steadily. The number of LWE-affected districts fell sharply, and by April 2025 the government said only six districts remained "most affected". In 2025, security forces killed several top Maoist leaders, including the party's general secretary, and hundreds of cadres surrendered.

The Union Home Minister had set 31 March 2026 as the deadline to end Naxalism. On 30 March 2026, he told the Lok Sabha that India had become "Naxal-free", while saying a formal announcement would follow once the process was complete. Analysts caution that the underlying grievances of tribal communities must be addressed to prevent a revival.

Key takeaways

  • Naxalism began at Naxalbari in 1967; the CPI (Maoist) was formed in 2004.
  • Security operations, roads, camps and development schemes steadily reduced violence and affected districts.
  • On 30 March 2026, the Home Minister told Parliament India had become "Naxal-free"; addressing tribal grievances remains essential.

Why it matters for UPSC: LWE is a core internal security topic, and its 2026 status is a likely question in both stages.

Practice question: "Security operations can defeat insurgents, but only development can defeat insurgency." Discuss with reference to left-wing extremism.
