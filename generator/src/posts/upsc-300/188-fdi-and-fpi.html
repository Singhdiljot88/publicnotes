Foreign investment: FDI and FPI

Note 188 · Theme 19: Public finance & external sector · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Foreign investment comes in two main forms. Foreign direct investment (FDI) involves a lasting interest and significant influence in a business, such as building a factory or buying at least 10% of a listed company. Foreign portfolio investment (FPI) involves buying smaller stakes in shares and bonds for financial returns, without control. FDI is generally considered more stable and brings technology, management skills and jobs; FPI is more volatile, sometimes called "hot money", because it can leave quickly when global conditions change, causing market falls and pressure on the rupee.

India's FDI policy, administered by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT), allows FDI through two routes. Under the automatic route, no prior government approval is needed; this covers most sectors, often up to 100%. Under the government route, approval is required, for example in sectors such as media, some defence investments and multi-brand retail. A few sectors are prohibited, including lottery, gambling, chit funds, real estate business (as distinct from construction development) and atomic energy. Limits have been raised over time: for example, to 74% in defence under the automatic route in 2020 and to 100% in insurance in 2025. Since April 2020, under Press Note 3, any investment from countries sharing a land border with India, mainly aimed at China, requires government approval.

Gross FDI inflows have been strong, but net FDI, after subtracting repatriation by foreign investors and outward investment by Indian companies, has fallen sharply in recent years. RBI data showed net FDI of only about 7.7 billion dollars in 2025-26. Singapore, Mauritius and the United States are among the largest sources of FDI, partly because of tax treaties.

FPIs register with SEBI and face limits on holdings in individual companies. Their flows into Indian equities and government bonds, which have been included in global bond indices since 2024, can swing sharply with global interest rates and risk sentiment.

Key takeaways

  • FDI brings lasting control and is stable; FPI is portfolio investment and more volatile.
  • Most sectors allow FDI under the automatic route; investment from neighbouring land-border countries needs approval since 2020.
  • Gross FDI remains strong, but net FDI fell to about 7.7 billion dollars in 2025-26.

Why it matters for UPSC: FDI routes, caps and trends are frequently asked in Prelims and in Mains questions on investment.

Practice question: Why has net FDI into India fallen even as gross inflows remain high? What are the implications?
