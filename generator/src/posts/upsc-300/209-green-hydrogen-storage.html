Green hydrogen and energy storage

Note 209 · Theme 21: Industry, infrastructure & energy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Hydrogen can be used as a fuel and as an industrial feedstock without emitting carbon dioxide at the point of use. Most hydrogen today is "grey", made from natural gas or coal with large emissions. "Blue" hydrogen captures those emissions. "Green" hydrogen is made by splitting water through electrolysis using renewable electricity, producing almost no emissions.

India launched the National Green Hydrogen Mission in January 2023 with an initial outlay of about Rs 19,744 crore. It targets production capacity of at least 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen a year by 2030, supported by about 125 GW of additional renewable capacity. The SIGHT programme (Strategic Interventions for Green Hydrogen Transition) gives incentives for manufacturing electrolysers and producing green hydrogen. The main early uses are in sectors that are hard to electrify: fertilisers (ammonia), oil refining, steel, shipping fuels and long-haul transport. India also hopes to export green hydrogen and ammonia, and several green hydrogen hubs are planned near ports.

The main obstacle is cost: green hydrogen is still more expensive than grey hydrogen, because electrolysers and renewable electricity are costly and the process needs water. Storage and transport are difficult because hydrogen is light and leaks easily.

Energy storage more broadly is crucial for renewable energy. Battery energy storage systems (BESS) store solar power for evening use; the government has offered viability gap funding for battery storage projects and a PLI scheme for advanced chemistry cell batteries. Pumped storage hydropower, which pumps water uphill when power is cheap and releases it through turbines when needed, is another option, and several projects are under way. Storage costs have fallen sharply, and tenders for solar-plus-storage have become common.

Key takeaways

  • Green hydrogen is made by electrolysis using renewable power; most hydrogen today is grey.
  • The National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023, about Rs 19,744 crore) targets 5 million tonnes a year by 2030.
  • Cost is the main barrier; batteries and pumped storage are key to balancing renewable power.

Why it matters for UPSC: Green hydrogen and storage are frequent recent GS III questions on energy transition.

Practice question: What role can green hydrogen play in decarbonising Indian industry? What are the barriers?
