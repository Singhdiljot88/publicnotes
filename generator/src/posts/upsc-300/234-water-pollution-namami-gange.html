Water pollution and cleaning the Ganga

Note 234 · Theme 24: Environment & ecology · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Water pollution in India comes from three main sources: untreated sewage from towns and cities, industrial effluents, and run-off of fertilisers and pesticides from farms. Sewage is the largest single source. Cities generate far more wastewater than their treatment plants can handle, and much of the untreated remainder flows straight into rivers. Common measures of pollution include biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), which shows how much oxygen microbes need to break down organic waste, dissolved oxygen, and faecal coliform counts. Groundwater faces its own threats, including arsenic in parts of the Ganga-Brahmaputra plains and fluoride in many dry districts.

The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 was India's first major environmental law. It set up the Central and State Pollution Control Boards, which give consent to industries to set up and operate and monitor their discharges. The Ganga Action Plan of 1985 was the first big river clean-up effort, but it had limited success because sewage treatment capacity lagged and plants were poorly run.

Namami Gange, approved in 2014, is an integrated mission covering sewage treatment, riverfront development, river surface cleaning, afforestation, biodiversity and public awareness. It is run by the National Mission for Clean Ganga. A key innovation is the hybrid annuity model for sewage treatment plants, under which the government pays private operators over 15 years linked to performance, so that plants are maintained after they are built. The National Ganga Council, chaired by the Prime Minister, oversees the effort, and the Ganga river dolphin, India's national aquatic animal, is a sign of river health.

Results have been mixed: water quality has improved at some stretches, but faecal coliform levels remain high in many places, and keeping enough water in the river (ecological flow) is still a challenge.

Key takeaways

  • Untreated sewage is the largest source of river pollution; BOD, dissolved oxygen and faecal coliform measure it.
  • The Water Act, 1974 created pollution control boards; the 1985 Ganga Action Plan had limited success.
  • Namami Gange (2014) uses the hybrid annuity model to keep sewage plants running, but ecological flow remains a weak link.

Why it matters for UPSC: River pollution links urban governance, federal cooperation and environmental law, and is a frequent GS III case study.

Practice question: Why did earlier Ganga clean-up efforts fail, and how does Namami Gange try to address those weaknesses?
