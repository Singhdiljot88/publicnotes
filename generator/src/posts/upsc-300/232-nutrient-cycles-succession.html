Nutrient cycles and ecological succession

Note 232 · Theme 24: Environment & ecology · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Unlike energy, matter is recycled. Biogeochemical cycles move elements between living things, the air, water and rocks. In the carbon cycle, plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis, and respiration, decomposition and the burning of fossil fuels return it to the atmosphere. Oceans and forests act as large carbon sinks. Human activity, mainly fossil fuel burning and deforestation, has pushed atmospheric carbon dioxide well above pre-industrial levels, which is the main driver of climate change.

The nitrogen cycle shows how a gas that makes up about 78% of the air must be "fixed" before plants can use it. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Rhizobium, living in root nodules of legumes, and free-living cyanobacteria convert it to ammonia. Nitrifying bacteria turn ammonia into nitrites and nitrates, and denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen to the air. The industrial Haber-Bosch process now fixes huge amounts of nitrogen for fertiliser. The phosphorus cycle has no major gaseous phase; phosphorus moves slowly from rocks to soil, water and organisms. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus running off farms causes eutrophication: algal blooms that use up oxygen and kill fish.

Ecological succession is the gradual, predictable change in the species that make up a community over time. Primary succession starts on bare surfaces with no soil, such as rock or cooled lava, with pioneer species like lichens and mosses. Secondary succession begins where soil remains after a disturbance, such as an abandoned field or a burnt forest, and proceeds faster. Succession in water is called hydrarch and on dry land xerarch. Both move towards a relatively stable climax community suited to the local climate.

Key takeaways

  • Energy flows one way, but nutrients cycle; human activity has disrupted the carbon and nitrogen cycles.
  • Nitrogen must be fixed by bacteria (or industry) before plants can use it; nutrient run-off causes eutrophication.
  • Primary succession starts on bare rock with pioneers like lichens; secondary succession starts on existing soil and is faster.

Why it matters for UPSC: Nutrient cycles connect directly to climate change, fertiliser policy and water pollution, all recurring GS III themes.

Practice question: How has human intervention in the nitrogen cycle created both food security gains and environmental problems?
