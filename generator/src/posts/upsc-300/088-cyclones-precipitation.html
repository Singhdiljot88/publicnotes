Clouds, precipitation and cyclones

Note 088 · Theme 9: Physical geography · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

When moist air rises, it cools and its water vapour condenses into tiny droplets around particles such as dust or salt, forming clouds. Clouds are classified by height and shape: cirrus (high, wispy), cumulus (puffy, often vertical), stratus (flat layers) and nimbus (rain-bearing). Cumulonimbus clouds produce thunderstorms. Precipitation falls when droplets or ice crystals grow heavy enough. Rainfall is grouped into three types: convectional rain, from rising heated air, common in equatorial regions; orographic or relief rain, when air is forced up a mountain, leaving a dry rain shadow on the other side, as with the Western Ghats; and cyclonic or frontal rain.

Tropical cyclones are intense low-pressure systems that form over warm oceans, usually with sea surface temperatures above about 26–27°C. They need a large supply of warm, moist air, the Coriolis force (so they do not form within about 5 degrees of the equator) and low vertical wind shear. Winds spiral inward around a calm centre called the eye. They are called hurricanes in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoons in the western Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean. Their greatest dangers are storm surges, strong winds and heavy rain. In the north Indian Ocean, cyclones are named from a list contributed by member countries of a WMO/ESCAP panel, and the India Meteorological Department in New Delhi is the regional centre that issues advisories.

The Bay of Bengal sees more cyclones than the Arabian Sea, though Arabian Sea cyclones have become more frequent and intense in recent years, which scientists link to warming waters.

Temperate cyclones form in the mid-latitudes where warm and cold air masses meet along fronts. They are larger and less intense, and bring the western disturbances that give north India winter rain and snow in the Himalayas.

Key takeaways

  • Rainfall is convectional, orographic or cyclonic.
  • Tropical cyclones need warm seas, the Coriolis force and low wind shear; IMD is the regional centre for the north Indian Ocean.
  • Temperate cyclones bring western disturbances and winter rain to north India.

Why it matters for UPSC: Cyclones and rainfall types link geography with disaster management and agriculture.

Practice question: Why are cyclones in the Arabian Sea becoming more frequent and intense? What are the implications for India's west coast?
