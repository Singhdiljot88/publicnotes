The tools of monetary policy

Note 173 · Theme 18: Money, banking & markets · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and liquidity by the central bank to manage inflation and support growth. The RBI's tools are usually divided into quantitative instruments, which affect overall money and credit, and qualitative instruments, which direct credit to particular uses.

The most important instrument is the policy repo rate, the rate at which the RBI lends money to banks overnight against government securities. Changes in the repo rate are meant to pass through to the interest rates banks charge borrowers and pay depositors. Under the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF), banks can also park surplus funds with the RBI. Since April 2022, the floor of the interest rate corridor has been the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF), which absorbs surplus funds without requiring the RBI to give collateral, replacing the fixed-rate reverse repo in that role. The ceiling is the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF), an emergency window through which banks can borrow overnight at a higher rate, even dipping into their statutory reserves. The bank rate is now aligned with the MSF rate.

Reserve requirements are another tool. The cash reserve ratio (CRR) is the share of deposits banks must keep with the RBI as cash, earning no interest. The statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) is the share of deposits banks must hold in safe liquid assets such as government securities, cash and gold. Raising either reduces the funds banks can lend.

Open market operations (OMOs) are purchases or sales of government securities by the RBI to inject or absorb liquidity. Variable rate repo and reverse repo auctions fine-tune liquidity. Foreign exchange operations also affect domestic liquidity: when the RBI sells dollars to support the rupee, it absorbs rupees from the system.

Qualitative tools include margin requirements on loans against securities, credit ceilings, moral suasion and priority sector lending norms, which require banks to direct 40% of their lending to sectors such as agriculture, small businesses and weaker sections.

Key takeaways

  • The repo rate is the main policy rate; the SDF is the floor and the MSF the ceiling of the corridor.
  • CRR and SLR are reserve requirements that limit how much banks can lend.
  • OMOs and forex operations adjust liquidity; priority sector lending directs 40% of credit.

Why it matters for UPSC: Monetary policy instruments are asked almost every year in Prelims.
