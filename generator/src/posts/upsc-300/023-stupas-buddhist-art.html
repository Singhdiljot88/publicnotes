Stupas and early Buddhist art

Note 023 · Theme 3: Art & culture · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

A stupa is a hemispherical mound built over relics of the Buddha or a revered monk. Its parts carry meaning. The dome (anda) represents the cosmos, the railing (vedika) encloses a sacred path for walking around it (pradakshina), and the umbrella (chhatra) on top symbolises honour. Gateways called toranas mark the four directions.

Ashoka is credited by Buddhist tradition with building many stupas to distribute the Buddha's relics. The Great Stupa at Sanchi in Madhya Pradesh was begun in his time and enlarged later. Its four carved toranas, added around the first century BCE, show Jataka tales and scenes from the Buddha's life. Bharhut in Madhya Pradesh and Amaravati in Andhra Pradesh are other major early stupas; the Amaravati carvings in white limestone are known for their graceful movement.

In early Buddhist art the Buddha was not shown in human form. He was represented by symbols: a lotus or elephant for his birth, a horse for his renunciation, the Bodhi tree for enlightenment, a wheel for his first sermon and a stupa for his death (parinirvana). This aniconic phase gave way to images of the Buddha in human form in the first and second centuries CE, in the Gandhara and Mathura schools.

Sanchi is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Sarnath, where the Buddha gave his first sermon and where the Dhamek stupa stands, was added in 2026 as India's 45th World Heritage Site.

Key takeaways

  • A stupa's dome, railing, umbrella and gateways each carry symbolic meaning.
  • Early Buddhist art showed the Buddha through symbols, not human images.
  • Sanchi, Bharhut and Amaravati are key early stupas; Sarnath was inscribed by UNESCO in 2026.

Why it matters for UPSC: Stupa architecture, Buddhist symbols and sites appear often in Prelims and connect to current heritage news.

Practice question: Trace the shift from aniconic to iconic representation of the Buddha in Indian art.
