Ocean currents and tides

Note 089 · Theme 9: Physical geography · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Ocean currents are large-scale movements of seawater. Surface currents are driven mainly by planetary winds, shaped by the Coriolis force and the shape of continents. Deep currents are driven by differences in temperature and salinity, which affect water density; this is called thermohaline circulation, sometimes described as a global "conveyor belt" that redistributes heat.

Currents are warm or cold. Warm currents flow from the tropics towards the poles along the eastern coasts of continents in low and middle latitudes, such as the Gulf Stream along North America and the Kuroshio near Japan. The Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, keep north-western Europe much warmer than other places at the same latitude. Cold currents flow towards the equator along western coasts, such as the Humboldt (Peru) current off South America, the Benguela off south-west Africa and the California current. Cold currents are associated with coastal deserts, such as the Atacama and Namib, because they cool the air and reduce rainfall. Where cold and warm currents meet, or where cold water rises (upwelling), nutrients support rich fishing grounds, as on the Grand Banks off Newfoundland and off Peru.

In the northern Indian Ocean, currents reverse with the monsoon winds, flowing clockwise in summer and anticlockwise in winter, a unique feature.

Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level, caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun. Spring tides, with the greatest range, occur at new moon and full moon when the Sun, Moon and Earth are aligned; neap tides, with the smallest range, occur at the quarter moons. The Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch have high tidal ranges, giving them potential for tidal energy.

Key takeaways

  • Surface currents are wind-driven; deep currents are driven by temperature and salinity.
  • Warm currents flow along eastern coasts; cold currents along western coasts, often near deserts.
  • Spring tides occur at new and full moon; neap tides at quarter moons.
