Police reform and the new criminal laws

Note 136 · Theme 14: Governance & public policy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Policing in India is a state subject, but its legal foundation was long the colonial Police Act of 1861, designed to control rather than serve the population. Commissions such as the National Police Commission (1977–81) recommended reforms, but few were implemented.

In Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006), the Supreme Court issued seven directives: set up a State Security Commission to insulate the police from political pressure; select the Director General of Police on merit from officers empanelled by the UPSC, with a minimum two-year tenure; give a minimum two-year tenure to other key officers; separate investigation from law-and-order functions; set up a Police Establishment Board for transfers and postings; create Police Complaints Authorities at state and district level; and establish a National Security Commission for central forces. Compliance by states has been patchy, and many have passed laws that dilute the directives.

Other problems include large vacancies, a low police-to-population ratio, poor forensic capacity, custodial violence, and low representation of women, who make up only about 12% of the force according to Bureau of Police Research and Development data.

In 2023 Parliament replaced the three main criminal laws. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860; the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973; and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. They came into force on 1 July 2024. Key changes include new offences such as organised crime, terrorism and mob lynching; community service as a punishment for some offences; removal of sedition in its old form (replaced by an offence of endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity); mandatory forensic investigation for offences punishable with seven years or more; zero FIRs and e-FIRs; timelines for investigation and trials; and recognition of electronic evidence. Critics argue the laws extend police custody periods and retain broad powers, and that most of the text repeats the old codes.

Key takeaways

  • Prakash Singh (2006) issued seven directives for police reform, poorly implemented by states.
  • Women are only about 12% of the police, and vacancies and forensic gaps are large.
  • The BNS, BNSS and BSA replaced the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act from 1 July 2024.

Why it matters for UPSC: Police reform and the new criminal laws are among the most important recent topics in GS II and GS III.

Practice question: Will the new criminal laws make the justice system faster and fairer? Critically examine.
