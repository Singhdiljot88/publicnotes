Attitude: content, structure and influence

Note 273 · Theme 28: Ethics: foundations · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

An attitude is a learned tendency to evaluate something, a person, group, object or idea, favourably or unfavourably. The syllabus asks about the content, structure and function of attitudes, their influence on thought and behaviour, moral and political attitudes, and social influence and persuasion.

Psychologists often describe attitudes with the ABC model. The affective component is how we feel about something; the behavioural component is how we tend to act towards it; and the cognitive component is what we believe about it. These do not always align. A person may believe in gender equality yet feel uneasy with women in authority, or support cleanliness yet litter. Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance explains the discomfort people feel when attitudes and actions clash, and how they reduce it, often by changing the attitude rather than the behaviour.

Attitudes serve functions. They help us make sense of the world quickly (knowledge function), express our identity and values (value-expressive function), protect our self-image (ego-defensive function), and fit in with groups (adjustive function). Moral attitudes concern right and wrong, such as views on honesty or animal welfare; political attitudes concern power and public policy, such as views on the role of the state.

Attitudes can be changed by persuasion. Its effectiveness depends on the source (credibility and attractiveness), the message (clear arguments, emotional appeal, two-sided presentation for informed audiences), and the audience (prior beliefs, education, involvement). Social influence works through conformity, compliance and obedience; Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments of the 1960s showed how readily ordinary people follow authority even against their conscience. Public campaigns such as Swachh Bharat or anti-tobacco messaging apply these ideas.

Key takeaways

  • An attitude has affective, behavioural and cognitive components (the ABC model).
  • Cognitive dissonance arises when attitudes and actions conflict.
  • Persuasion depends on the source, the message and the audience; social influence can override conscience.

Why it matters for UPSC: Attitude is a core GS IV concept, and its links to persuasion and behaviour change appear in theory and case questions.

Practice question: How can persuasion be used ethically by the government to change public behaviour? Illustrate with a campaign.
