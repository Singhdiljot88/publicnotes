Regionalism

Note 078 · Theme 8: Indian society · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Regionalism is a feeling of loyalty to a particular region, based on language, culture, history, economic interests or a sense of neglect. In itself it need not be harmful: regional pride can coexist with national identity, and regional demands can be a legitimate way to seek development and recognition within a democracy. It becomes a problem when it turns into hostility towards people from other regions or into secessionist demands.

Regionalism in India has taken several forms. Linguistic movements led to the reorganisation of states in the 1950s and 1960s. Demands for new states based on development and identity led to the creation of Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Uttarakhand in 2000 and Telangana in 2014; similar demands continue, for example for Gorkhaland or Vidarbha. Some movements, as in parts of the North-East and Punjab in the past, became secessionist. "Sons of the soil" politics, which demands priority for locals in jobs, has sometimes led to hostility against migrants, and several states have passed laws reserving private-sector jobs for locals, which courts have examined.

The causes include uneven economic development, perceived neglect by the Centre, fear of cultural dominance, migration and the use of regional identity by political parties. Regional parties, such as the DMK in Tamil Nadu or the TDP in Andhra Pradesh, have become important players in national coalitions.

India has managed regionalism largely by accommodation: linguistic states, special provisions under Articles 371 to 371J, autonomous councils under the Sixth Schedule, and federal fiscal transfers. Balanced regional development is seen as the most lasting solution.

Key takeaways

  • Regionalism is loyalty to a region; it can be healthy or divisive depending on its form.
  • It has driven the creation of linguistic and new states, and sometimes hostility to migrants.
  • India accommodates regionalism through federalism, special provisions and autonomous councils.

Why it matters for UPSC: Regionalism is a GS I syllabus topic and often linked to federalism questions in GS II.

Practice question: Is regionalism a threat to national integration or a strength of Indian democracy? Argue with examples.
