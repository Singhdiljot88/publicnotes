Money laundering and terror financing

Note 267 · Theme 27: Internal security · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Money laundering is the process of making illegally obtained money look legitimate, usually in three stages: placement (putting cash into the financial system), layering (moving it through complex transactions to hide its origin) and integration (bringing it back as apparently clean money). Terror financing is the raising and moving of funds for terrorist activities, which may come from legal or illegal sources. Both threaten security and the integrity of the financial system.

India's main law is the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), enforced by the Directorate of Enforcement (ED). It allows the attachment and confiscation of proceeds of crime linked to listed "scheduled offences", and makes bail difficult through "twin conditions" in Section 45. In Vijay Madanlal Choudhary (2022), the Supreme Court upheld the ED's wide powers of arrest, search and attachment, and held that the ED's case report (ECIR) need not be shared with the accused at the outset. Critics argue that the ED's conviction rate is very low compared with the number of cases registered, and that the law has been used disproportionately against opposition politicians. The Financial Intelligence Unit-India receives and analyses suspicious transaction reports from banks and other entities.

Internationally, the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), set up by the G7 in 1989, sets global standards and evaluates countries. Countries with serious weaknesses are placed under increased monitoring (the "grey list") or called for action (the "black list"). India's mutual evaluation, adopted in June 2024, placed it in the "regular follow-up" category, the best outcome, while asking it to speed up prosecutions and avoid misusing laws against non-profit organisations. Pakistan was on the grey list from 2018 to 2022, and India has pressed FATF to scrutinise Pakistan's action against terror financing.

New challenges include cryptocurrencies, hawala networks, online gaming and betting platforms, and cross-border fraud. Virtual digital asset providers were brought under PMLA in 2023.

Key takeaways

  • Money laundering has three stages: placement, layering and integration.
  • PMLA (2002) is enforced by the ED; the Supreme Court upheld its wide powers in 2022.
  • FATF's 2024 evaluation placed India in "regular follow-up", its best rating.

Why it matters for UPSC: PMLA, the ED and FATF are frequent GS III topics, and India's 2024 evaluation is a likely Prelims question.

Practice question: How effective is India's anti-money laundering framework? Discuss the concerns about the use of PMLA.
