The world's maritime chokepoints

Note 105 · Theme 11: Resources & world geography · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

About 80% of world trade by volume moves by sea, and much of it passes through a few narrow passages called chokepoints. Disruption at any one of them can raise freight costs and energy prices worldwide.

The Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and Oman, links the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Around a fifth of the world's oil and much of its liquefied natural gas pass through it, including a large share of India's crude and LPG imports. The Bab-el-Mandeb strait, between Yemen and Djibouti, links the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden; attacks on shipping by Yemen's Houthi movement from late 2023 forced many ships to reroute around Africa's Cape of Good Hope. The Suez Canal in Egypt, opened in 1869, links the Red Sea with the Mediterranean and greatly shortens the route between Asia and Europe.

The Strait of Malacca, between Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia's Sumatra, is the main route between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific, carrying a large share of China's energy imports; China's worry about dependence on it is called the "Malacca dilemma". India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands lie near its western entrance. Other key straits include Lombok and Sunda in Indonesia, the Strait of Gibraltar between Spain and Morocco, the Bosphorus and Dardanelles linking the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, the Panama Canal linking the Atlantic and Pacific, and the Mozambique Channel.

India's interest in chokepoints explains its naval deployments in the Gulf of Aden against piracy since 2008, its partnerships with countries around the Indian Ocean, and its focus on the security of sea lanes in policies such as SAGAR, now expanded as MAHASAGAR.

Key takeaways

  • Around 80% of world trade by volume moves by sea through a few chokepoints.
  • Hormuz is vital for oil and gas, including India's imports; Malacca links the Indian Ocean and Pacific.
  • Red Sea attacks since 2023 showed how disruptions raise costs worldwide.

Why it matters for UPSC: Chokepoints are a frequent map-based Prelims question and key to Mains questions on energy security and maritime strategy.

Practice question: How do disruptions at maritime chokepoints affect India's economy? What can India do to reduce its vulnerability?
