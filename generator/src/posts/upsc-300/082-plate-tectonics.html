Continental drift and plate tectonics

Note 082 · Theme 9: Physical geography · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift: the continents had once formed a single supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by an ocean called Panthalassa, which broke apart and drifted to their present positions. His evidence included the jigsaw fit of the coastlines of South America and Africa, matching rock formations and fossils (such as the reptile Mesosaurus) on continents now separated by oceans, and glacial deposits in tropical regions. But he could not explain what force moved the continents, and most scientists rejected the idea.

Evidence from the ocean floor revived it. In the 1960s, Harry Hess proposed seafloor spreading: new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges as magma rises, and spreads outwards. Magnetic stripes on the ocean floor, recording reversals of the Earth's magnetic field, are symmetrical on either side of ridges, and the ocean floor is youngest at the ridges and oldest near continents.

These ideas combined into plate tectonics. The lithosphere is broken into about seven major plates and many minor ones that move a few centimetres a year over the asthenosphere, driven mainly by mantle convection, ridge push and slab pull. Plates meet at three types of boundaries. At divergent boundaries, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, plates move apart. At convergent boundaries, plates collide: oceanic crust sinks beneath continental crust in subduction zones, creating trenches and volcanoes, while continent-continent collision raises fold mountains. At transform boundaries, such as the San Andreas Fault, plates slide past each other.

The Indian plate separated from Gondwana, moved rapidly north and collided with the Eurasian plate around 50 million years ago, raising the Himalayas and the Tibetan plateau. It is still moving north at about 4–5 cm a year, which is why the Himalayas remain seismically active.

Key takeaways

  • Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912 but lacked a mechanism.
  • Seafloor spreading and magnetic stripes provided the evidence for plate tectonics.
  • The Indian plate's collision with Eurasia created the Himalayas and continues today.

Why it matters for UPSC: Plate tectonics explains earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains, and is a staple of Prelims and Mains geography.

Practice question: How does plate tectonics explain the formation of the Himalayas and their seismic activity?
