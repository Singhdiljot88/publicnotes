Protecting Scheduled Castes: law and reality

Note 141 · Theme 15: Social justice & welfare · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Scheduled Castes are castes notified by the President under Article 341 as having suffered untouchability and severe social disadvantage. The 2011 Census counted about 20 crore SC people, 16.6% of the population. The Constitution protects them through the abolition of untouchability (Article 17), reservation in education, public jobs, promotions, Parliament and state legislatures, and the National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338). Reservation of seats in legislatures, originally meant for ten years, has been extended by amendment every decade, most recently to 2030.

The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 defines atrocities, from social boycott and humiliation to violence, and provides special courts, stricter punishment and no anticipatory bail. In Subhash Kashinath Mahajan (2018), the Supreme Court introduced safeguards against alleged misuse, including preliminary inquiries before registering cases. After nationwide protests, Parliament passed an amendment in 2018 restoring the original provisions, which the Court upheld in 2020. National Crime Records Bureau data show tens of thousands of crimes against SCs registered each year, with low conviction rates.

Manual scavenging, the cleaning of human excreta by hand, overwhelmingly done by Dalits, was banned by the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013. But deaths in sewers and septic tanks continue to be reported. In 2023 the Supreme Court raised compensation for sewer deaths to Rs 30 lakh.

In August 2024 a seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court held that states can sub-classify SCs to give priority to the most backward among them, and several judges suggested applying a creamy layer to SCs and STs, an idea the Union government has said it will not adopt.

The persistence of discrimination in schools, workplaces, housing and marriage shows that legal equality has not yet produced social equality.

Key takeaways

  • SCs were 16.6% of the population in 2011; legislative reservation is extended to 2030.
  • The Atrocities Act (1989) was restored by a 2018 amendment after the Court diluted it.
  • Manual scavenging is banned but sewer deaths continue; states may now sub-classify SCs.

Why it matters for UPSC: Laws and judgments on SCs are central to GS II social justice and appear frequently in both stages.

Practice question: Why has the Prevention of Atrocities Act not fully succeeded in preventing caste violence?
