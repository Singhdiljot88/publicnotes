Soils of India

Note 095 · Theme 10: Geography of India · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Soil forms from the weathering of parent rock, shaped by climate, vegetation, relief and time. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) classifies Indian soils into eight major groups.

Alluvial soils are the most widespread, covering about 40% of the country, mainly the Northern Plains and river deltas. Deposited by rivers, they are very fertile and rich in potash but often poor in nitrogen and phosphorus. Older alluvium is called bhangar and newer, more fertile alluvium is called khadar. Black soils, also called regur or black cotton soils, formed from the basalt of the Deccan Traps and cover much of Maharashtra, Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh. They retain moisture well, swell when wet and crack when dry, are rich in lime, iron, magnesium and potash, and are ideal for cotton.

Red and yellow soils form on ancient crystalline rocks in the peninsula; the red colour comes from iron oxide. Laterite soils form in areas of high temperature and heavy rainfall, where intense leaching removes nutrients, leaving iron and aluminium oxides; they are found in the Western Ghats, Odisha and the North-East, and suit crops such as cashew, tea and coffee. Laterite is also cut into bricks for building. Arid soils are sandy and saline, found in western Rajasthan. Saline and alkaline soils occur where poor drainage brings salts to the surface, often worsened by over-irrigation. Peaty and marshy soils form in humid areas with lots of organic matter, as in parts of Kerala and the Sundarbans. Forest and mountain soils vary with altitude.

Soil degradation is a major problem: erosion, salinity, nutrient depletion from imbalanced fertiliser use and loss of organic matter. The Soil Health Card scheme, launched in 2015, provides farmers with soil test results and fertiliser recommendations.

Key takeaways

  • Alluvial soils are the most widespread and fertile; black soils are ideal for cotton.
  • Laterite soils form through intense leaching in hot, wet areas.
  • Soil degradation from erosion, salinity and imbalanced fertiliser use is a major concern.

Why it matters for UPSC: Soil types, their distribution and suitable crops are standard Prelims questions.
