The Industrial Revolution

Note 061 · Theme 7: World history · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the second half of the eighteenth century and transformed how goods were made. It started in cotton textiles, where inventions such as James Hargreaves's spinning jenny (1760s), Richard Arkwright's water frame and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule mechanised spinning, and Edmund Cartwright's power loom mechanised weaving. James Watt's improved steam engine, patented in 1769, provided reliable power for factories, mines and, later, railways and steamships. George Stephenson's locomotives helped launch the railway age; the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830.

Historians explain Britain's lead through several factors: abundant coal and iron ore, an agricultural revolution that raised food output and released labour, capital from trade and colonies, a large merchant class, protected property rights, a culture of practical invention, and access to markets and raw materials through its empire.

The social effects were profound. Cities such as Manchester grew rapidly, often with crowded, unsanitary housing. Factory work brought long hours, child labour and harsh discipline, prompting reforms such as the Factory Acts and the rise of trade unions. New ideas responded to these conditions, from liberal economics (Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, 1776) to socialism; Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published the Communist Manifesto in 1848.

Industrialisation spread to Belgium, France, Germany, the United States and Japan in the nineteenth century, in what is sometimes called a second industrial revolution based on steel, chemicals, electricity and oil. For colonies such as India, it meant markets for manufactured goods and pressure to supply raw materials, contributing to deindustrialisation.

Key takeaways

  • The Industrial Revolution began in Britain's textile industry in the late eighteenth century.
  • Coal, capital, markets, colonies and invention explain Britain's lead.
  • It produced urbanisation, new social classes and new ideologies, and reshaped colonial economies.

Why it matters for UPSC: The Industrial Revolution is a foundation topic for world history and helps explain colonialism and modern ideologies.

Practice question: How did the Industrial Revolution in Britain affect the economies of its colonies?
