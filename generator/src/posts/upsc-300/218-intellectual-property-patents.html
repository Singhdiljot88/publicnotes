Intellectual property, patents and access to medicines

Note 218 · Theme 22: Science & technology · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Intellectual property rights (IPR) protect creations of the mind. Patents protect inventions, usually for 20 years. Copyright protects literary, artistic and musical works and software. Trademarks protect brand names and logos. Geographical Indications protect products linked to a place. Industrial designs protect the look of products. The World Trade Organization's TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights), in force from 1995, set minimum standards that members must follow.

India's Patents Act, 1970 allowed patents only on processes, not products, for food, medicines and chemicals. This let Indian companies make cheaper versions of patented drugs using different processes and built India's generic pharmaceutical industry. To comply with TRIPS, India amended the law in 2005 to allow product patents in pharmaceuticals.

The 2005 amendment included safeguards. Section 3(d) denies patents for new forms of known substances unless they show significantly enhanced efficacy, to prevent "evergreening", where companies extend patents through minor changes. In Novartis v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court used Section 3(d) to reject a patent for a new form of the cancer drug Glivec. Section 84 allows compulsory licences, letting another company make a patented product if it is not available at a reasonable price; in 2012 India granted its first compulsory licence to Natco Pharma for a generic version of Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar. Pre-grant opposition allows anyone to challenge a patent application. The TRIPS Agreement's flexibilities were confirmed by the 2001 Doha Declaration on public health.

India's National IPR Policy (2016) aims to raise awareness and speed up patent examination. Patent filings have grown, and Indian residents now file more applications than foreigners. India is regularly placed on the US Trade Representative's "Priority Watch List", which India rejects, saying its laws comply with TRIPS.

Key takeaways

  • Patents last 20 years; TRIPS set global minimum IPR standards from 1995.
  • India allowed pharmaceutical product patents in 2005, with Section 3(d) against evergreening.
  • Novartis (2013) and the Natco compulsory licence (2012) protected access to affordable medicines.

Why it matters for UPSC: IPR, Section 3(d) and compulsory licensing are recurring GS III questions.

Practice question: How has India balanced intellectual property protection with access to affordable medicines?
