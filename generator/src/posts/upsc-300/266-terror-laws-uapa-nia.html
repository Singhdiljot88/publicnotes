Anti-terror laws: UAPA and the NIA

Note 266 · Theme 27: Internal security · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's main anti-terror law is the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (UAPA). It was originally a law against secessionist organisations. After the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act (TADA, 1985–95) lapsed amid allegations of abuse, and the Prevention of Terrorism Act (POTA, 2002) was repealed in 2004, their anti-terror provisions were moved into UAPA through amendments in 2004, 2008 and later.

UAPA defines terrorist acts and unlawful activities, allows the government to ban organisations as terrorist or unlawful, and provides stringent procedures. Police can detain an accused for up to 180 days before filing a charge sheet, compared with 60 or 90 days under ordinary law. Section 43D(5) makes bail very difficult: courts must refuse bail if there are reasonable grounds to believe the accusation is "prima facie true". The 2019 amendment allowed the government to designate individuals, not only organisations, as terrorists.

The National Investigation Agency (NIA) was set up by the NIA Act, 2008, after the Mumbai attacks, as a central agency to investigate and prosecute terrorism and related offences across states without needing their consent. The 2019 amendment allowed it to investigate offences such as human trafficking, counterfeit currency, manufacture of prohibited arms and cyber terrorism, and crimes against Indians and Indian interests abroad. Special NIA courts try its cases.

Critics, including some judges, argue that UAPA is used against journalists, activists and students, that cases take years while the accused remain in jail, and that official data show a large gap between the number of people arrested and those convicted. The Supreme Court has granted bail in some long-pending cases, citing the right to a speedy trial under Article 21 (Union of India v. K. A. Najeeb, 2021). Supporters argue that terrorism requires special laws, as networks are hard to prove quickly.

Key takeaways

  • UAPA (1967) absorbed anti-terror provisions after TADA and POTA; it allows 180-day detention and makes bail very difficult.
  • The 2019 amendments allowed designating individuals as terrorists and widened the NIA's powers.
  • Low conviction rates and long pre-trial detention have led courts to grant bail on speedy trial grounds.

Why it matters for UPSC: UAPA and the NIA are central to internal security questions and to debates on civil liberties.

Practice question: How can anti-terror laws be made effective without undermining civil liberties?
