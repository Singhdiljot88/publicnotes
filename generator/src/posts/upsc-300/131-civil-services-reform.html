The civil services and their reform

Note 131 · Theme 14: Governance & public policy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's permanent civil service is the backbone of administration. The All India Services, the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS) and Indian Forest Service (IFoS), are recruited by the Union Public Service Commission but serve both the Union and the states. Article 312 allows Parliament to create new All India Services if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority. Central services, such as the Indian Foreign Service and Indian Revenue Service, serve the Union alone, and state services are recruited by state public service commissions. Article 311 protects civil servants from dismissal without an inquiry and by an authority lower than the one that appointed them.

Sardar Patel defended the All India Services in the Constituent Assembly as a "steel frame" that would hold the country together and give honest advice without fear. Critics today describe a bureaucracy that is rule-bound rather than results-focused, slow, sometimes corrupt, subject to political pressure through frequent transfers, and lacking domain expertise because generalist officers move between very different jobs.

Many reforms have been proposed. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005–09) recommended performance-based appraisal, fixed tenures, a lower age limit for entry and mid-career reviews. The Supreme Court in T. S. R. Subramanian (2013) recommended fixed minimum tenures and civil services boards to curb arbitrary transfers. The government has introduced lateral entry of specialists at joint secretary and director level from 2018, although a large advertisement for lateral entry posts was withdrawn in 2024 after criticism that it bypassed reservations. Mission Karmayogi, the National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building launched in 2020, shifts training from rules to roles, with an online learning platform called iGOT.

The goal of reform is a civil service that is professional, accountable and politically neutral, yet responsive to elected governments.

Key takeaways

  • The All India Services serve both the Union and the states; Article 311 protects civil servants.
  • Criticisms include rigidity, political interference and lack of specialisation.
  • Lateral entry and Mission Karmayogi (2020) are recent reforms; fixed tenures remain a key demand.

Why it matters for UPSC: Civil services reform is a recurring GS II and GS IV theme, often asked in the context of good governance.

Practice question: Is the generalist civil servant still relevant in an age of specialised governance? Discuss.
