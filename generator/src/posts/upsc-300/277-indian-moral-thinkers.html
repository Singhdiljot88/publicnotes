Indian moral thinkers

Note 277 · Theme 28: Ethics: foundations · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Indian traditions offer a rich body of ethical thought that UPSC expects candidates to know and apply.

The Bhagavad Gita teaches nishkama karma: doing one's duty without attachment to the fruits of action. For an administrator, this suggests acting rightly regardless of personal reward or fear. The Buddha taught the Middle Path, the Eightfold Path including right speech, right action and right livelihood, and compassion (karuna) for all beings. Mahavira emphasised ahimsa (non-violence) and anekantavada, the idea that truth has many sides, which encourages tolerance. Thiruvalluvar's Tirukkural offers practical guidance on virtue, wealth and love, including on just rule. Kautilya's Arthashastra held that the king's happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects, and described in detail how officials embezzle public money, recommending close supervision.

In modern times, Swami Vivekananda called for service to humanity as service to God and stressed strength, self-reliance and character building. Rabindranath Tagore championed universal humanism and education that frees the mind. Mahatma Gandhi made ethics the centre of politics: truth (satya), non-violence (ahimsa), the purity of means as well as ends, trusteeship of wealth, and the talisman: when in doubt, recall the face of the poorest person you have seen and ask whether your step will help them. He also listed "seven social sins", including politics without principles, commerce without morality and knowledge without character.

B. R. Ambedkar grounded ethics in liberty, equality and fraternity, insisted on constitutional morality, and warned that political democracy cannot last without social democracy. Other thinkers cited include Basavanna on dignity of labour (kayaka) and Sree Narayana Guru on social equality.

These ideas are best used in answers not as quotations alone, but to show how a principle would guide a concrete decision.

Key takeaways

  • The Gita's nishkama karma and the Buddha's Middle Path and compassion remain widely applied.
  • Gandhi stressed truth, non-violence, pure means and the talisman of the poorest person.
  • Ambedkar's constitutional morality and fraternity are central to public ethics.

Why it matters for UPSC: Indian thinkers are regularly asked in GS IV, and a well-chosen idea strengthens case study answers.

Practice question: How is Gandhi's talisman relevant to decision-making by civil servants today?
