The power sector and the discom problem

Note 206 · Theme 21: Industry, infrastructure & energy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's electricity system has three parts: generation, transmission and distribution. Generation has expanded enormously; India is the world's third-largest producer of electricity, and installed capacity crossed 550 GW in 2026. The national grid became a single synchronous grid in 2013 under the "One Nation, One Grid, One Frequency" idea, allowing power to flow across regions. Electricity is a concurrent subject. The Electricity Act, 2003 separated regulation from government through independent central and state electricity regulatory commissions and opened generation to private players.

The weak link is distribution. Most electricity distribution companies (discoms) are owned by state governments. They lose money because tariffs for farmers and households are kept below cost for political reasons, state governments pay subsidies late, bills go uncollected, and a large share of power is lost to technical losses and theft. These are measured as aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses. Discoms' accumulated losses run into several lakh crore rupees, and they delay payments to generators, which harms the whole sector.

Several rescue packages have been tried. The Ujwal DISCOM Assurance Yojana (UDAY) of 2015 had states take over most discom debt in return for reform targets, but losses rose again. The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), launched in 2021 with an outlay of about Rs 3 lakh crore, funds smart prepaid meters and network upgrades, linked to reductions in losses. AT&C losses have fallen to around 16% in recent years, according to the Power Ministry. Late payment surcharge rules from 2022 have cut overdue payments to generators.

Long-term solutions discussed include cost-reflective tariffs, direct benefit transfer of power subsidies to farmers instead of free power, competition in distribution, and solar pumps for agriculture under the PM-KUSUM scheme.

Key takeaways

  • India's capacity crossed 550 GW in 2026; the grid has been one synchronous system since 2013.
  • State discoms lose money through below-cost tariffs, unpaid subsidies and AT&C losses.
  • UDAY (2015) and RDSS (2021) are the main reform schemes; losses have fallen to around 16%.

Why it matters for UPSC: Power sector reform and discom finances are a recurring GS III topic, linked to energy transition and agriculture.

Practice question: Why do power distribution companies in India keep running into losses? Suggest lasting solutions.
