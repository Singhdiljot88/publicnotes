Nuclear power and the SHANTI Act

Note 208 · Theme 21: Industry, infrastructure & energy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's civil nuclear programme was shaped by Homi Bhabha's three-stage plan, designed around India's small uranium reserves and large thorium reserves. Stage one uses pressurised heavy water reactors (PHWRs) fuelled by natural uranium. Stage two uses fast breeder reactors fuelled by plutonium from stage one; the 500 MW Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor at Kalpakkam began core loading in 2024. Stage three would use thorium-based reactors. India also operates foreign-designed reactors, such as Russia's VVERs at Kudankulam in Tamil Nadu.

Nuclear power remains small. India has 24 operable reactors with a capacity of about 8 GW, around 3% of electricity generation. Growth was slowed by limited uranium supply until the 2008 NSG waiver, high costs, long construction times, local opposition (as at Kudankulam and Jaitapur) and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010, whose provisions on supplier liability discouraged foreign vendors.

The government has set a target of 100 GW of nuclear capacity by 2047. The Union Budget 2025-26 launched a Nuclear Energy Mission with Rs 20,000 crore for small modular reactors (SMRs), with the aim of having at least five indigenously designed SMRs operational by 2033. SMRs are smaller, factory-built reactors that can be installed faster and placed at sites such as retiring coal plants.

In December 2025 Parliament passed the Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act. It repeals the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010, allows private companies to build and operate nuclear plants and take part in parts of the fuel cycle, keeps sensitive activities under government control, replaces the earlier liability framework with a graded system, and gives the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board statutory status. Supporters say it will attract investment; critics worry about safety and how accident liability will be shared.

Key takeaways

  • Bhabha's three-stage programme aims to use India's large thorium reserves.
  • India has 24 reactors (about 8 GW); the target is 100 GW by 2047, with SMRs a priority.
  • The SHANTI Act (December 2025) opened nuclear power to private firms and replaced the 2010 liability law.

Why it matters for UPSC: The SHANTI Act and nuclear energy goals are a major recent GS III topic.

Practice question: Can private participation help India meet its nuclear energy target? Discuss the safety and liability concerns.
