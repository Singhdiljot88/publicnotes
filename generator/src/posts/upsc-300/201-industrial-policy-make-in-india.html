Industrial policy: from licence raj to Make in India

Note 201 · Theme 21: Industry, infrastructure & energy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's industrial policy has passed through three broad phases. The Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956 gave the state the leading role. The 1956 resolution reserved 17 industries, including iron and steel, heavy machinery, mining and railways, for the public sector, and private firms needed licences to set up or expand capacity. Over time this became the "licence-permit raj", which protected incumbents, discouraged competition and slowed growth, even as it built a broad industrial base.

The New Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 dismantled most of this system. Industrial licensing was abolished for all but a handful of industries, the list reserved for the public sector was cut sharply (today only atomic energy and railway operations remain fully reserved), and foreign investment was welcomed. Manufacturing grew, but its share of GDP stayed stuck at around 15–17%, while services drove the economy.

Make in India, launched in September 2014, aimed to raise manufacturing's share and create jobs by easing business rules, improving infrastructure and opening sectors to foreign direct investment. India rose sharply in the World Bank's Ease of Doing Business rankings before the report was discontinued in 2021. The Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes, launched from 2020 with an outlay of about Rs 1.97 lakh crore across 14 sectors, pay companies a share of their additional sales for a fixed period. Mobile phones are the best-known success: India moved from importing most of its phones to becoming one of the largest exporters of smartphones.

Critics argue that PLI favours large firms and assembly rather than deep manufacturing, and that tariff increases since 2018 risk a return to protectionism. The debate now is how to build supply chains as companies diversify away from China.

Key takeaways

  • The 1956 resolution gave the public sector the commanding heights; the 1991 policy ended most licensing.
  • Make in India (2014) and PLI schemes (from 2020, about Rs 1.97 lakh crore across 14 sectors) aim to raise manufacturing.
  • Electronics, especially mobile phones, is the clearest PLI success; manufacturing's GDP share has barely moved.

Why it matters for UPSC: Industrial policy is a core GS III topic, often asked through PLI, Make in India or the China-plus-one opportunity.

Practice question: Why has manufacturing's share of India's GDP remained stagnant despite repeated policy pushes?
