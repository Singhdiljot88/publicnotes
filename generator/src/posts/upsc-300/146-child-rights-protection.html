Children's rights: education, protection and nutrition

Note 146 · Theme 15: Social justice & welfare · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India has the largest child population in the world. The Constitution protects children through Article 21A (free and compulsory education for ages six to fourteen, added in 2002), Article 24 (prohibiting employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines and hazardous work), Article 39(e) and (f) (directive principles on protecting children from abuse and exploitation), and Article 45 (early childhood care and education until age six).

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE Act) sets norms for schools, bans capitation fees and screening, and requires private unaided schools to reserve 25% of entry-level seats for children from disadvantaged groups and weaker sections, with costs reimbursed by the state. In 2019 the "no-detention" policy was amended to allow states to hold back students in classes 5 and 8.

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 provides a child-friendly process for reporting and trying sexual offences against children, with special courts; amendments in 2019 introduced the death penalty for aggravated penetrative sexual assault. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 allows children aged 16–18 accused of heinous crimes to be tried as adults after an assessment, and governs adoption and child care institutions. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 banned employment of children below 14 in all occupations except family enterprises outside school hours, and of adolescents aged 14–18 in hazardous work. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 sets the minimum marriage age.

Nutrition is a major challenge. NFHS-5 (2019–21) found 35.5% of children under five stunted, 19.3% wasted and 32.1% underweight, and 67% of children aged 6–59 months anaemic. Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 integrate the anganwadi system and nutrition programmes, and the PM POSHAN scheme provides cooked meals in schools.

Key takeaways

  • Article 21A and the RTE Act (2009) guarantee education for ages 6–14, with 25% seats in private schools for weaker sections.
  • POCSO (2012), the JJ Act (2015) and the 2016 child labour amendment protect children.
  • NFHS-5: 35.5% of under-fives stunted and 67% of young children anaemic.

Why it matters for UPSC: Child rights, education and nutrition are frequent GS II topics, and NFHS data are useful in many answers.

Practice question: Why does child malnutrition remain high in India despite large nutrition programmes?
