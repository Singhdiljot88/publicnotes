Quit India and the Indian National Army

Note 049 · Theme 5: The freedom struggle · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In March 1942, as Japan advanced through South-East Asia, Britain sent Sir Stafford Cripps to win Indian support for the war. The Cripps Mission offered dominion status after the war and the right of provinces to stay out of a new union. The Congress rejected it; Gandhi reportedly called it a "post-dated cheque".

On 8 August 1942, at the Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, the All-India Congress Committee passed the Quit India resolution. Gandhi gave the call "Do or die". The next morning, the entire Congress leadership was arrested. Without leaders, the movement became a spontaneous uprising: railway lines and telegraph wires were cut, police stations were attacked, and parallel governments were set up in places such as Ballia, Tamluk and Satara. Young leaders such as Aruna Asaf Ali, who hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank, Jayaprakash Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia worked underground, and Usha Mehta ran a secret Congress radio. The movement was crushed within months, with around a thousand killed by official figures (far more by nationalist estimates) and tens of thousands arrested, but it showed that British rule could no longer rely on Indian consent.

Subhas Chandra Bose, who had been Congress president in 1938 and 1939, escaped from house arrest in 1941 and sought help from Germany and Japan. In 1943 he took command of the Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj), formed from Indian prisoners of war, and proclaimed the Provisional Government of Free India in Singapore. The INA fought alongside Japan on the Imphal-Kohima front in 1944 but was defeated. The trials of INA officers at Delhi's Red Fort in 1945 caused nationwide protests, and the Royal Indian Navy mutiny of February 1946 showed that the armed forces could no longer be relied on.

Key takeaways

  • The Quit India resolution was passed on 8 August 1942 with the call "Do or die".
  • Leaderless, the movement became a mass uprising and was suppressed with force.
  • Bose's INA, the 1945 Red Fort trials and the 1946 naval mutiny weakened British control.

Why it matters for UPSC: Quit India and the INA are core topics for both Prelims and Mains, often asked as the final phase of the struggle.

Practice question: How did the INA trials and the naval mutiny of 1946 hasten the end of British rule?
