Financing infrastructure: PPPs, NIP and asset monetisation

Note 203 · Theme 21: Industry, infrastructure & energy · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India needs enormous investment in roads, railways, power, ports, water and cities. The National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP), released in 2019–20, identified projects worth about Rs 111 lakh crore for 2020–25. Since the pandemic, public capital expenditure has carried most of the load: the Union government's capex budget rose steeply, to over Rs 11 lakh crore by 2025-26.

Public-private partnerships (PPPs) were meant to bring private capital and efficiency. Common models include build-operate-transfer (BOT), where a private firm builds a project, collects tolls or fees for a fixed period and then hands it back; BOT-annuity, where the government pays the firm fixed annual sums; and the hybrid annuity model (HAM), introduced for highways in 2016, in which the government pays 40% of the cost during construction and the rest as annuities, while the private partner bears less traffic risk. The PPP boom of the 2000s ended badly, with stalled projects, aggressive bidding, disputes and bad loans in banks. The Vijay Kelkar Committee (2015) recommended better risk sharing and dispute resolution.

The National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP), announced in August 2021, aims to raise about Rs 6 lakh crore over four years by leasing existing public assets, such as highways, railway stations, pipelines and transmission lines, to private operators while the government keeps ownership. Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs) and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) allow investors to buy units in income-earning assets. The National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development (NaBFID), set up by a 2021 law, provides long-term finance, filling a gap left after development finance institutions such as ICICI and IDBI became commercial banks.

The challenge is to attract patient private and foreign capital without repeating the mistakes of earlier PPPs.

Key takeaways

  • The NIP identified about Rs 111 lakh crore of projects; public capex has led recent investment.
  • BOT, annuity and the hybrid annuity model are the main PPP forms.
  • Asset monetisation (NMP, about Rs 6 lakh crore), InvITs and NaBFID are newer financing tools.

Why it matters for UPSC: Infrastructure financing and PPP models are frequent GS III questions.

Practice question: What lessons should India learn from the failures of PPP projects in the 2000s?
