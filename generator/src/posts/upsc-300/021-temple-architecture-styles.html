Temple architecture: Nagara, Dravida and Vesara

Note 021 · Theme 3: Art & culture · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Hindu temple architecture developed from simple shrines in the Gupta period into elaborate complexes. Every temple has a few core parts: the garbhagriha, a small dark sanctum housing the main deity; the mandapa, a hall for worshippers; and a superstructure rising above the sanctum. The shape of that superstructure is the easiest way to tell the main styles apart.

The Nagara style, common in north India, has a curvilinear tower called a shikhara, topped by a ribbed stone disc called an amalaka and a kalasha. Nagara temples usually stand on a raised platform and lack elaborate boundary walls. Examples include the Kandariya Mahadeva temple at Khajuraho, the Sun Temple at Konark (in the Odisha sub-style, where the tower is called a deul) and the Dilwara Jain temples at Mount Abu.

The Dravida style, common in Tamil Nadu and the south, has a pyramid-shaped tower called a vimana, rising in storeys, and is enclosed by high walls entered through gateways called gopurams. In later centuries, as at Madurai's Meenakshi temple, the gopurams grew taller than the vimana itself. The Brihadeeswara temple at Thanjavur and the Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram are classic examples.

The Vesara style, found in the Deccan, blends elements of both. It developed under the Chalukyas of Badami and later the Hoysalas. Hoysala temples at Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura are known for their star-shaped plans and richly carved soapstone; the Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

Key takeaways

  • Nagara temples have a curvilinear shikhara; Dravida temples have a stepped vimana and gopurams.
  • Vesara blends both and is linked to the Chalukyas and Hoysalas.
  • The garbhagriha, mandapa and superstructure are common to all styles.

Why it matters for UPSC: Identifying temple styles and matching temples to dynasties are among the most common Prelims culture questions.

Practice question: Distinguish between the Nagara and Dravida styles of temple architecture with suitable examples.
