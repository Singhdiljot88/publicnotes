Money and money supply: M0 to M4

Note 171 · Theme 18: Money, banking & markets · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Money performs four functions: it is a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value and a standard of deferred payment. Modern money is fiat money, which has value because the government declares it legal tender and people trust it, not because it is backed by gold. In India, the RBI issues currency notes, while the Government of India issues coins and one-rupee notes.

The RBI measures the money supply in several ways. Reserve money (M0), also called high-powered money or base money, consists of currency in circulation, bankers' deposits with the RBI and other deposits with the RBI. It is called high-powered because banks can create several times this amount of money through lending.

The broader measures follow. M1 (narrow money) is currency with the public, demand deposits with banks (such as current accounts and the demand portion of savings accounts) and other deposits with the RBI. M2 adds savings deposits with post office savings banks. M3 (broad money) adds time deposits with banks, such as fixed deposits, and is the most commonly used measure of money supply. M4 adds all deposits with post office savings banks. Liquidity falls as you move from M1 to M4: currency can be spent instantly, while fixed deposits take time to convert.

The money multiplier links M0 and M3. When a bank receives a deposit, it keeps a fraction as reserves (because of the cash reserve ratio and its own needs) and lends the rest, which is deposited elsewhere and lent again. The smaller the reserve ratio, the larger the multiplier. This is why the RBI's tools, such as the cash reserve ratio, affect the money supply.

Demonetisation in November 2016 withdrew Rs 500 and Rs 1,000 notes, about 86% of currency by value. In May 2023 the RBI also withdrew the Rs 2,000 note from circulation, though it remains legal tender.

Key takeaways

  • Money's functions are exchange, unit of account, store of value and deferred payment.
  • M0 is reserve money; M1 is narrow money; M3 is broad money, the most used measure.
  • The money multiplier explains how banks create money from reserves.

Why it matters for UPSC: Money supply measures and the multiplier are frequent Prelims questions.

Practice question: How does the cash reserve ratio influence the money multiplier and the money supply?
