Conscience, crisis of conscience and moral dilemmas

Note 279 · Theme 28: Ethics: foundations · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Conscience is the inner sense of right and wrong that judges our own actions. It can be seen as the voice of reason, of religious or moral teaching, or of internalised social norms. Gandhi spoke of the "inner voice" guiding his decisions. The syllabus refers to conscience as a source of ethical guidance and asks about crisis of conscience.

A moral dilemma arises when a person must choose between two or more obligations or values, and acting on one means failing the other. Examples in administration include whether to follow a superior's lawful but unjust order, whether to report a corrupt colleague who is also a friend, or whether to evict encroachers who are poor in order to clear a public drain. An ethical dilemma differs from a temptation: in a temptation, the right course is clear but costly; in a dilemma, the right course itself is unclear.

A crisis of conscience occurs when a person's conscience conflicts sharply with what their role, organisation or law demands. Responses range from voice (raising concerns internally), to whistleblowing (reporting outside), to exit (resigning), to loyalty (complying while disagreeing). Albert Hirschman's framework of exit, voice and loyalty is often used to analyse such choices.

Conscience is not infallible. People can sincerely believe wrong things, and conscience shaped by prejudice can mislead. So conscience should be informed by reason, law, constitutional values and dialogue. In a democracy, a civil servant who disagrees with a lawful policy should record dissent through proper channels and implement it faithfully, unless it requires clearly illegal or grossly immoral action.

A structured approach helps in dilemmas: identify the facts and stakeholders, the values in conflict, the legal position, and the options; evaluate each option's consequences and consistency with principles; and choose the option that best upholds the public interest and constitutional values.

Key takeaways

  • Conscience is an inner moral sense, but it must be informed by reason and law.
  • A dilemma involves conflicting obligations; a temptation involves a clear right course that is costly.
  • Exit, voice and loyalty describe responses to a crisis of conscience.

Why it matters for UPSC: Conscience and dilemmas are central to GS IV theory and essential for structuring case study answers.

Practice question: Is following one's conscience always the right course for a civil servant? Discuss.
