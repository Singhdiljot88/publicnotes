India's disaster management framework

Note 257 · Theme 26: Climate change & disaster management · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India is one of the most disaster-prone countries in the world. About 58% of its land is prone to earthquakes of moderate to very high intensity, around 12% to floods, about 5,700 km of its 7,500 km coastline to cyclones and tsunamis, and about 68% of its cultivable land to drought, according to the National Disaster Management Authority.

For decades, India's approach was relief-centred. The Orissa super cyclone of 1999, the Gujarat earthquake of 2001 and the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004 led to a shift towards preparedness and risk reduction. The Disaster Management Act, 2005 created a three-tier structure: the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), chaired by the Prime Minister; State Disaster Management Authorities chaired by chief ministers; and District Disaster Management Authorities chaired by district collectors. The National Executive Committee, headed by the Union Home Secretary, coordinates the response. The National Institute of Disaster Management handles training and research. The Act also created the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF), a specialised force drawn from the central armed police forces, which now has 16 battalions.

Funding comes through the State Disaster Response Fund and the National Disaster Response Fund for relief, and, on the recommendation of the 15th Finance Commission, separate mitigation funds at national and state levels. The Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025 gave the NDMA and state authorities statutory responsibility for preparing national and state disaster plans and databases, and provided for urban disaster management authorities in large cities.

Internationally, India follows the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030), which sets seven global targets, including reducing disaster deaths and economic losses. Prime Minister Modi's ten-point agenda on disaster risk reduction (2016) and India's leadership of the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI), launched in 2019, reflect its growing role.

Key takeaways

  • India is exposed to earthquakes, floods, cyclones and droughts across most of its territory.
  • The DM Act (2005) created the NDMA, state and district authorities and the NDRF; it was amended in 2025.
  • India follows the Sendai Framework (2015–2030) and leads the CDRI.

Why it matters for UPSC: Disaster management is a named GS III syllabus topic, and the institutional framework is frequently asked.

Practice question: How has India's approach to disaster management shifted from relief to risk reduction? What gaps remain?
