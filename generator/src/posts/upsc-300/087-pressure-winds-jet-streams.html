Pressure belts, winds and jet streams

Note 087 · Theme 9: Physical geography · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Air moves from high pressure to low pressure, and these movements are winds. Uneven heating of the Earth creates a pattern of pressure belts. Near the equator, intense heating causes air to rise, creating the equatorial low, also called the doldrums or Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ). Around 30 degrees north and south, sinking air creates the subtropical high-pressure belts, where many of the world's hot deserts lie. Around 60 degrees there are subpolar low-pressure belts, and at the poles, polar highs.

The Earth's rotation deflects moving air through the Coriolis force: to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. It is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles. As a result, winds blowing from the subtropical highs to the equator become the north-east and south-east trade winds; winds blowing towards the poles become the westerlies; and cold winds from the poles become the polar easterlies. In the southern hemisphere, the westerlies blow strongly over open ocean and are called the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Screaming Sixties.

Jet streams are narrow bands of very fast winds in the upper troposphere, flowing from west to east. The subtropical westerly jet stream and the polar front jet stream are the main ones. In summer, a tropical easterly jet develops over peninsular India. Jet streams influence weather systems below them; the shift of the subtropical westerly jet north of the Himalayas in early summer is linked to the onset of the Indian monsoon.

Local winds also matter. The loo is a hot, dry wind over north India in summer. The chinook and foehn are warm, dry winds descending mountain slopes. Land and sea breezes reverse daily along coasts.

Key takeaways

  • Pressure belts at the equator, 30°, 60° and the poles create the global wind pattern.
  • The Coriolis force deflects winds right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern.
  • Jet streams in the upper troposphere influence weather, including the Indian monsoon.

Why it matters for UPSC: Planetary winds, the Coriolis force and jet streams are frequently tested and essential for understanding the monsoon.

Practice question: How do jet streams influence the onset and behaviour of the Indian monsoon?
