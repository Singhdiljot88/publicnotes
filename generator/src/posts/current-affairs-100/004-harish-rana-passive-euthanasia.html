Harish Rana: India's first court-approved passive euthanasia

Note 004 · Theme 1: Polity, Parliament & courts · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

On 11 March 2026, the Supreme Court allowed life support to be withdrawn for Harish Rana, a 32-year-old man who had been in a persistent vegetative state for 13 years after falling from the fourth floor of his paying-guest accommodation. He was fed through tubes and had shown no improvement. It was the first time an Indian court had applied the passive euthanasia framework to permit withdrawal of treatment for a specific patient. He died later that month in the care of AIIMS, New Delhi.

The bench of Justices J. B. Pardiwala and K. V. Viswanathan built on the Constitution Bench judgment in Common Cause (2018), which recognised the right to die with dignity as part of Article 21 and permitted passive euthanasia (withdrawing or withholding treatment) and advance directives or "living wills". In January 2023 the Court simplified those guidelines, requiring approval by a primary and a secondary medical board.

In Harish Rana's case, both boards and his parents agreed that treatment, including clinically administered nutrition, should be stopped. The Court allowed this, waived the 30-day reconsideration period, and asked AIIMS to provide palliative care under a plan that preserved his dignity. It said that normally, when both boards agree, courts need not be involved. It also directed High Courts to have judicial magistrates receive intimations from hospitals in such cases, asked the Union government to ensure every district maintains panels of doctors for secondary boards, and recommended that Parliament pass a comprehensive law.

Active euthanasia, deliberately causing death, remains illegal in India.

Key takeaways

  • On 11 March 2026 the Supreme Court permitted withdrawal of life support for Harish Rana, the first such case.
  • It applied the Common Cause (2018) framework, as simplified in 2023, with two medical boards.
  • The Court urged Parliament to legislate; active euthanasia remains illegal.

Why it matters for UPSC: The case combines Article 21, medical ethics and the separation of powers, useful for GS II and GS IV.
