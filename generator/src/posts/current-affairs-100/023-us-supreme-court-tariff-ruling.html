The US Supreme Court tariff ruling and the 10% global duty

Note 023 · Theme 4: Trade deals & tariffs · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

On 20 February 2026, the Supreme Court of the United States ruled 6–3 that President Trump had exceeded his authority by using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) of 1977 to impose sweeping "reciprocal" tariffs on trading partners. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion. The ruling removed the legal basis for the country-specific tariffs announced since April 2025, including those on India.

The administration responded within hours. It imposed a temporary 10% tariff on imports from almost all countries under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows the President to impose duties of up to 15% for up to 150 days to deal with balance-of-payments problems. The new duty took effect on 24 February 2026. According to Indian officials quoted by ANI and reported by Outlook Business, the 10% rate replaced the proposed 18% rate from the February India–US joint statement rather than being added to it, since the 18% rate had never been implemented.

The ruling mattered for India in three ways. First, it lowered the tariff Indian exporters faced, at least temporarily. Second, it weakened the leverage of the threat of tariffs in bilateral negotiations, prompting Indian commentators to argue that India should reassess the commitments it had offered. Third, it created uncertainty, because Section 122 tariffs could last only 150 days, and the administration signalled it would use other laws, such as Section 301 investigations, to impose longer-lasting duties. That is what happened in July 2026.

The case also illustrated a constitutional principle familiar to Indian students: the power to tax lies with the legislature, and courts can check executive action that goes beyond the authority granted by law.

Key takeaways

  • On 20 February 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that IEEPA did not authorise the reciprocal tariffs.
  • A temporary 10% global tariff under Section 122 took effect on 24 February, for up to 150 days.
  • For India, the 10% rate replaced the proposed 18% rate.

Why it matters for UPSC: The ruling connects international trade with separation of powers, useful for GS II comparisons of constitutional systems.

Discussion question: What does the US Supreme Court's tariff ruling show about the limits of executive power over taxation? Compare with India.
