What the Election Commission does, and what it does not do

Note 011 · Theme 2: Election Commission & voter rolls · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

The Election Commission of India (ECI) is a constitutional body created under Article 324 of the Constitution. It was established on 25 January 1950, and its headquarters are at Nirvachan Sadan, New Delhi. Today it consists of a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and two Election Commissioners. The CEC chairs the Commission, and decisions are taken, as far as practicable, unanimously, or otherwise by majority.

Article 324 gives the Commission the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President. In practice, that means the ECI prepares and revises voter lists, announces election schedules, sets up polling stations, enforces the Model Code of Conduct during elections, registers political parties for electoral purposes, allots symbols, and supervises polling and counting. It works through Chief Electoral Officers in each State, District Election Officers, Electoral Registration Officers (EROs), and Booth Level Officers (BLOs) on the ground.

The Commission does not do everything connected with democracy. It does not make laws — that is Parliament's and State Legislatures' job. It does not run governments, decide policy, or grant citizenship; checking eligibility for the electoral roll is a different, narrower task from deciding citizenship under citizenship law. It does not try election petitions — those go to courts — and it cannot change the Constitution or the basic qualifications for voting, which are set by the Constitution and the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951.

Its independence is protected in several ways, including the difficult removal process for the CEC, discussed in Note 8. At the same time, it works within laws made by Parliament and is subject to judicial review. That combination — a strong constitutional mandate, but limited to elections — is why debates about the ECI often turn on the same question: has it stayed inside its electoral role, and has it exercised its powers fairly and transparently?

Key takeaways

  • The ECI runs elections and voter rolls under Article 324, but does not legislate, govern, or grant citizenship.
  • It works through State and district officers, BLOs, and EROs, not by itself alone.
  • Its powers are broad for elections, yet bounded by law and reviewable by courts.

Why it matters for civil studies: Understanding the ECI's exact mandate prevents two common errors — expecting it to solve non-electoral problems, and judging it by standards that belong to courts or legislatures.

Discussion question: If the ECI's job is only elections, which other institutions should a citizen approach for a problem that is not about voting?
