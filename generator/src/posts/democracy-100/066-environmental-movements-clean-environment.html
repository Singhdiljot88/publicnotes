Environmental movements and the right to a clean environment

Note 066 · Theme 7: Civil society & protest · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Environmental movements in India often begin with a very local concern — a forest, a river, a dam, a mine or air in a city — and grow into a question about who decides how natural resources are used. For communities that depend on forests, water and land for food, fuel, fodder and livelihoods, environmental damage is also an economic and social issue. For governments and project developers, the same project may be presented as needed for irrigation, electricity, jobs or national development. That clash of framings is central to environmental politics.

The Chipko movement of the early 1970s in the Garhwal Himalayas is a famous example. Villagers, including many women led in Reni village by Gaura Devi, hugged trees to prevent commercial felling, arguing that forests protected soil, water and local livelihoods. The movement helped popularise community-based conservation in India and abroad. The Narmada Bachao Andolan, associated with Medha Patkar and others from the 1980s onwards, opposed large dams on the Narmada, beginning with demands for proper rehabilitation and resettlement for displaced people and expanding to questions about the environmental and social costs of large dams. Supporters of the Sardar Sarovar Dam argued that it would provide water and power to Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Rajasthan. Critics argued that displacement and ecological harm were undercounted. The World Bank withdrew from the project after an independent review, and the dam was eventually completed and opened in 2017, while debates about rehabilitation continued.

Indian courts have linked environment to the right to life. Article 21 guarantees the right to life and personal liberty, and the Supreme Court has interpreted it to include the right to a clean and healthy environment and, in Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2000), the right to water as part of the right to life. Principles such as sustainable development, the precautionary principle and intergenerational equity have also shaped environmental jurisprudence, though their application in specific projects remains contested. More recent movements focus on air pollution in cities, climate justice, mining in forest areas and the rights of communities affected by infrastructure. Young readers should note that "environmental" does not always mean only conservation: many movements combine ecology with livelihoods, health and democratic participation in project decisions.

Key takeaways

  • Environmental movements often start locally but raise national questions about development, displacement and participation.
  • Chipko linked forest protection to livelihoods; Narmada Bachao Andolan linked dams to rehabilitation and ecology.
  • Courts have read a clean environment and water into Article 21, but project-by-project outcomes remain contested.

Why it matters for civil studies: Environmental movements show how rights, development and local knowledge collide, and why courts and public participation matter when costs and benefits fall on different people.

Discussion question: When a dam helps millions downstream but displaces thousands upstream, what process would make that decision democratically legitimate?
