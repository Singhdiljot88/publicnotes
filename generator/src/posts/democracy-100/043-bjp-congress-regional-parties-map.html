The BJP, Congress, and regional parties: a map for beginners

Note 043 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

A beginner's map of Indian party politics has three broad layers: the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), the Indian National Congress, and a wide set of regional and state parties. That map is useful, but it is a simplification. Alliances shift, parties split and merge, and the same party can be dominant in one state and marginal in another. Treat it as a starting point, not a fixed picture.

The BJP is currently India's largest party in the Lok Sabha. In 2024 it won 240 seats, down from 303 in 2019, and it leads the NDA coalition government. Commentators often describe its support as resting on national leadership, organisational reach, cultural nationalism, development promises, and welfare delivery — but the weight of each factor is disputed and varies by state. The party was founded in 1980 and traces its lineage to earlier organisations, a history its supporters emphasise and its critics examine differently.

The Congress is India's oldest major party, founded in 1885, and it led the independence movement and governed for much of the period after 1947. In 2024 it won 99 seats, up from 52 in 2019, and it leads the INDIA opposition alliance. Its leaders present it as a broad, centrist, secular and welfare-oriented alternative; its critics argue it has struggled with organisation and leadership renewal. Both descriptions are contested claims, not settled facts, and readers should compare them with results and party programmes.

Regional parties are not a single bloc. Examples include the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Tamil Nadu, the Telugu Desam Party in Andhra Pradesh, the Samajwadi Party in Uttar Pradesh, the Trinamool Congress in West Bengal, and the Aam Aadmi Party, also a recognised national party but strongest in Delhi and Punjab. Some focus on language and state identity, others on caste, social justice, or a model of governance. Some ally with the NDA, some with INDIA, and some keep their distance from both.

Two cautions help beginners. First, labels such as "left", "centre", and "right" mean different things in different states, and a party's position on one issue does not predict its position on every issue. Second, leadership matters, but institutions, state governments, courts, and voters also shape outcomes.

Key takeaways

  • The BJP and Congress are the two main national poles, but neither explains Indian politics alone.
  • Regional parties differ widely in ideology, base, and alliance choices.
  • State-level strength, not just national labels, decides much of what happens in elections.

Why it matters for civil studies: reading a national result without the state map leads to misleading conclusions about who voters chose and why.

Discussion question: If you had to redraw this map using issues rather than party names, what would the main dividing lines be?
