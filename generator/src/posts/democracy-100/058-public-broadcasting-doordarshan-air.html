Public broadcasting: Doordarshan, AIR, and their role today

Note 058 · Theme 6: Media & information · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Public broadcasting is meant to serve citizens, not only advertisers or subscribers. In India, that job belongs mainly to Prasar Bharati, the statutory autonomous body that runs Doordarshan for television and All India Radio, also called Akashvani, for radio. Parliament passed the Prasar Bharati Act in 1990, and Prasar Bharati came into existence in 1997, after Doordarshan and All India Radio had long operated as government departments. Doordarshan began as an experimental telecast in Delhi on 15 September 1959, while All India Radio dates to 1936. The stated purpose was to inform, educate, and entertain, and to reach audiences that commercial media might ignore.

That reach remains the strongest argument for public broadcasting. All India Radio broadcasts in many languages and dialects, and its local stations can carry farming advice, health information, education, and disaster warnings in the language people actually speak. Doordarshan's free-to-air service, DD Free Dish, gives households without a paid cable or satellite subscription access to television channels, including educational channels. Supporters argue that this matters most in rural, remote, and low-income areas, where a commercial channel may see little profit in local-language or public-service programming. In emergencies, a public network with wide transmitter coverage can also be a reliable channel for official warnings.

The main criticism is about autonomy in practice. Prasar Bharati is legally autonomous, but it is publicly funded, its leadership and governance are connected to the state, and its news coverage is often judged by whether it gives the government of the day fair scrutiny. Critics argue that public broadcasting can become too close to government messaging, especially on politically sensitive stories. Defenders argue that private channels have their own commercial and political pressures, and that the test should be the quality and balance of public output over time, not any single bulletin. A second challenge is relevance: younger audiences increasingly get news and entertainment on phones, so public broadcasters must compete for attention while keeping a public-service mission that commercial rivals do not have to carry.

Key takeaways

  • Prasar Bharati, created under the 1990 Act and in existence from 1997, runs Doordarshan (from 1959) and All India Radio (from 1936).
  • Its public-service case rests on wide, multilingual reach, including free television and local radio for audiences commercial media may ignore.
  • Its central tension is autonomy: legal independence must be tested against funding, governance, and the balance of its news coverage.

Why it matters for civil studies: Public broadcasting shows how a state can fund information for all citizens without turning that information into government publicity — a difficult balance to design and to judge.

Discussion question: How should the success of a public broadcaster be measured — by audience size, by reach to underserved areas, by trust, or by something else?
