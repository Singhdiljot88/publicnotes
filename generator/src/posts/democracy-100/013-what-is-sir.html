What is SIR? Special Intensive Revision explained simply

Note 013 · Theme 2: Election Commission & voter rolls · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, is a thorough, time-bound review of electoral rolls ordered by the Election Commission. It is more searching than a routine summary revision. Instead of mainly correcting the existing list, SIR involves fresh enumeration: Booth Level Officers visit households, distribute enumeration forms, collect them back, verify entries, publish a draft roll, invite claims and objections, and then publish a final roll.

The legal basis cited by the Commission is Article 324 of the Constitution together with Section 21(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1950, which allows special revision of rolls. The Commission has said the purpose is to remove entries for people who have died, shifted permanently, are enrolled more than once, or are otherwise not eligible, and to include eligible voters who are missing. It has also said the aim is that no eligible voter is left out.

The current cycle began with Bihar. On 24 June 2025, the Commission ordered SIR in Bihar, noting that the previous intensive revision there dated back to 2003 and citing urbanisation, migration, unreported deaths and duplicate entries. The exercise was later extended in phases to other States and Union Territories, each with its own calendar, so there has been no single national deadline. A separate Special Summary Revision was ordered on 15 October 2026 for 17 States and Union Territories, with 1 January 2027 as the qualifying date; schedules for States and Union Territories where SIR was ongoing or had recently concluded were to be announced separately.

Supporters of intensive revision argue that rolls decay over time: people move, die, or enrol twice, and only house-to-house checking can restore accuracy. Critics argue that a fast, large-scale exercise risks excluding people who are away, lack documents, live at an old address, or do not return a form in time. The Supreme Court's role is covered in Notes 4 and 5. For now, the key point is definitional: SIR is not a new kind of election, and it is not a citizenship test in itself. It is a roll-cleaning exercise whose fairness depends on how enumeration, notice, and correction actually work for each voter.

Key takeaways

  • SIR is a deep, door-to-door re-verification of rolls, ending in a draft, claims and objections, and a final roll.
  • The Commission cites Article 324 and Section 21(3) of the 1950 Act as its authority.
  • Its stated aim is accuracy and inclusion, but critics warn that speed and document demands can create exclusion risks.

Why it matters for civil studies: SIR shows how an administrative procedure — forms, visits and deadlines — can have constitutional consequences for the right to vote.

Discussion question: When is a thorough roll revision worth its disruption, and who should bear the cost if a voter is missed through no fault of their own?
