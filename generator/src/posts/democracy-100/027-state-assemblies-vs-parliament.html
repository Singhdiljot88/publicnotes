State Assemblies vs Parliament: Where Different Politics Live

Note 027 · Theme 3: Parliament & federalism · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

India has two layers of elected legislature that often feel like different political worlds. Parliament in New Delhi makes laws for the whole country on Union and Concurrent subjects, approves the Union Budget, and holds the central government to account. State legislative assemblies — Vidhan Sabhas — do the equivalent job for their state: they make laws on State and Concurrent subjects, approve the state budget, and hold the state government, led by the Chief Minister, to account. Members of Legislative Assemblies (MLAs) are directly elected, and the assembly normally serves for five years.

The two layers are not simply a big and a small version of each other. Parliament alone can amend the Constitution, legislate on defence, foreign affairs and currency, and decide national finance. Assemblies, by contrast, control police, public order, agriculture and many day-to-day services that citizens encounter most directly. Only Parliament can create or abolish a state legislative council, and only by law after the state assembly passes a resolution to that effect by a special majority (a majority of its total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting), and only some states have one: a Vidhan Parishad, or upper house, whose members are chosen through a mix of election by MLAs, local bodies, graduates and teachers, and nomination. Where it exists, the council can delay and revise bills but cannot ultimately block the assembly, and it has only 14 days to recommend changes to a money bill.

That separation is why voters can — and often do — choose differently at state and national level. A party that wins nationally may lose in a state because voters judge it on local services, language, caste and regional identity, or because a regional party speaks more directly to state concerns. State assemblies also elect members of the Rajya Sabha and vote in presidential elections, so state politics feeds back into national institutions. Conversely, central laws on Concurrent subjects, central funding, and national party campaigns shape what state governments can do. The result is a layered politics in which a citizen is governed, taxed and represented twice over — and in which the most important question is often not which party wins, but which level of government is responsible for the problem in front of them.

Key takeaways

  • Parliament handles national subjects and constitutional amendment; assemblies handle State subjects and day-to-day services.
  • Assemblies are directly elected for five years; a few states also have a legislative council that can delay, but not veto, assembly bills.
  • Voters often choose differently at state and national level, because the issues, parties and responsibilities differ.

Why it matters for civil studies: Understanding which legislature is responsible for an issue is the first step to holding the right government to account for it.

Discussion question: When state and national governments are run by rival parties, does that improve checks and balances — or make it harder to get things done?
