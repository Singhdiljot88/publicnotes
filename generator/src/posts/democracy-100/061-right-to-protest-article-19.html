The right to protest: Article 19 and its limits

Note 061 · Theme 7: Civil society & protest · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

India's Constitution does not use the exact phrase "right to protest." Instead, peaceful protest is generally understood to flow from the freedoms in Article 19: freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), the right to assemble peaceably and without arms under Article 19(1)(b), and the freedom to form associations or unions under Article 19(1)(c). Together, these provisions protect marches, sit-ins, meetings and other peaceful ways of expressing dissent, criticism or support for a cause.

That protection is not absolute. Article 19(2) allows reasonable restrictions on speech in interests including the sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. Article 19(3) allows reasonable restrictions on peaceful assembly in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India or public order. The key word is "reasonable": restrictions are meant to be linked to a legitimate aim, imposed by law, and proportionate, not arbitrary or so broad that they extinguish the right itself.

The Supreme Court has tried to balance competing rights. In Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan v. Union of India (2018), concerning protests at Jantar Mantar and in central Delhi, the Court recognised peaceful protest as a fundamental right while also recognising the rights of residents and commuters, and the need for regulation. In Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police (2020), arising from the Shaheen Bagh protest, the Court held that public roads and public spaces cannot be occupied indefinitely and that protests should ordinarily take place in designated places. Protesters' supporters argue that designated sites far from decision-makers can weaken a protest's visibility. Authorities argue that time, place and manner rules are needed so that one group's protest does not indefinitely block movement, emergency access or daily life for others.

In practice, permission systems, designated protest sites, police conditions and court orders shape where and how protest happens. A protest may lose protection if it turns violent, damages property, carries arms, or incites an offence. At the same time, criticism of a policy, even sharp criticism, is not by itself unlawful. For civil studies readers, the useful question is rarely whether protest is allowed in the abstract, but how a particular restriction is justified, who decides, and whether a less restrictive alternative was available.

Key takeaways

  • Peaceful protest is protected mainly through Articles 19(1)(a), (b) and (c), not by a separate "right to protest" clause.
  • Restrictions must be reasonable, lawful and proportionate, and linked to grounds listed in Articles 19(2) and 19(3).
  • Courts have balanced protesters' rights against the rights of residents, commuters and the wider public.

Why it matters for civil studies: Article 19 shows how a democracy protects dissent while also protecting public order and other people's rights.

Discussion question: If a peaceful protest blocks a main road for a week, what factors should decide whether the restriction on it is reasonable?
