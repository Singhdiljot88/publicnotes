Question Hour, Debates and Disruptions: Is Disruption a Democratic Tool?

Note 022 · Theme 3: Parliament & federalism · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Question Hour is usually the first hour of a sitting, when members put questions to ministers and can ask follow-up, or supplementary, questions. In the Lok Sabha it generally runs from 11 a.m. to noon, and in the Rajya Sabha from noon to 1 p.m. Members give advance notice, questions are checked for admissibility, and some are answered orally while others receive written replies. Alongside it, Zero Hour — an Indian innovation — lets members raise urgent matters in the Lok Sabha after Question Hour, without the long advance notice required for questions (members give same-day notice for the ballot); in the Rajya Sabha, Zero Hour comes before Question Hour, at around 11 a.m. Together with debates, adjournment motions, calling-attention motions and short-duration discussions, these devices are meant to force the government to explain itself in public.

Disruption happens when proceedings are repeatedly interrupted, slogans are raised, or members enter the well of the House, leading to adjournments and lost time. Analysts writing in 2026 pointed to a sharp example: in the Monsoon Session of 2026, the Lok Sabha was reported to have functioned for only 15% of scheduled time and the Rajya Sabha for 33%, with Lok Sabha Question Hour lasting only nine minutes. Over the longer 17th Lok Sabha, Question Hour was reported to have functioned for about 60% of scheduled time in the Lok Sabha and 52% in the Rajya Sabha. These figures come from secondary analyses and should be read as reported estimates, not as official session records reproduced here.

Defenders of disruption argue that it is sometimes the only way for a smaller opposition to force an issue onto the agenda when debate is refused, and that protest inside the House mirrors protest outside it. Critics argue that it defeats its own purpose: when Question Hour collapses, ministers escape oral questioning, bills can be passed with little debate, and voters lose the record of argument they need to judge both government and opposition. Reform proposals commonly discussed include a fixed parliamentary calendar, protected time for questions, and stronger negotiation between government and opposition before sittings begin.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Question Hour and Zero Hour are the main daily tools for questioning ministers and raising urgent issues.
  • Disruption can draw attention to an issue, but it also removes the questioning and debate it claims to demand.
  • Reported functioning rates for recent sessions have been low, which is why reform of sitting time is widely debated.

Why it matters for civil studies: Accountability depends not only on the right to ask questions, but on whether the House actually runs long enough for answers and follow-ups to happen.

Discussion question: If a government refuses a debate, is disruption a legitimate last resort, or does it damage the very accountability it seeks?
