Adivasi and Dalit movements: rights, land, and dignity

Note 067 · Theme 7: Civil society & protest · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Adivasi and Dalit movements address a shared democratic question from different histories: whether constitutional equality is real in daily life, not only on paper. Adivasi communities, also called Scheduled Tribes, have long claimed rights over jal, jangal, zameen — water, forest and land — and self-governance in their homelands. Dalit communities, historically subjected to untouchability and caste discrimination, have claimed dignity, equal access to public spaces, education, land and political power. Both movements use protest, litigation, electoral politics and cultural assertion, and both point to gaps between legal safeguards and implementation.

The Constitution provides several safeguards. Article 17 abolishes untouchability. Article 46 directs the State to promote the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and protect them from exploitation. The Fifth Schedule governs Scheduled Areas, and the Sixth Schedule provides for autonomous districts in parts of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Two later laws are central to study: the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA), which extends panchayat self-governance to Scheduled Areas and gives gram sabhas a role in decisions affecting their communities, and the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, which recognises individual and community rights over forest land. Activists argue that gram sabha consent is sometimes bypassed or recorded without genuine consultation, especially for mining and infrastructure projects. Governments and companies involved in such projects often argue that they have followed legal procedures and that projects bring jobs and development; those competing claims are frequently tested in courts, official inquiries and public debate.

Dalit assertion has a distinct history linked to B. R. Ambedkar, who chaired the Drafting Committee of the Constitution and campaigned against untouchability and caste discrimination. Dalit movements have pressed for implementation of laws against atrocities, access to land and education, and representation. Adivasi movements have included struggles over forest rights, displacement and cultural recognition, including the well-known Niyamgiri case, in which gram sabhas of Dongria Kondh communities rejected a mining project in 2013 after a Supreme Court direction for gram sabha consultation. Supporters cite that case as local consent working; others note such outcomes are rare.

For civil studies, the point is not to treat Adivasi and Dalit movements as identical. Their histories, legal tools and demands differ, but both test whether democracy delivers land, dignity and voice to people who have historically been excluded from power.

Key takeaways

  • Adivasi movements centre land, forest, water and self-governance; Dalit movements centre dignity, equality and access.
  • PESA (1996) and the Forest Rights Act (2006) give gram sabhas and forest dwellers important legal tools.
  • Implementation — consent, land records and protection from discrimination — is where many disputes arise.

Why it matters for civil studies: These movements test whether constitutional promises survive contact with mining, infrastructure, caste and local power.

Discussion question: If a gram sabha rejects a project that a state government says will create jobs, whose decision should prevail, and what safeguards should apply?
