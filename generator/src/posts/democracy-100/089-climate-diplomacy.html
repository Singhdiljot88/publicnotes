Climate diplomacy: India's voice in global talks

Note 089 · Theme 9: Foreign policy & democracy · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Climate negotiations are, for India, negotiations about development as much as about the environment. India is a large emitter in total terms, yet its per-person emissions remain well below those of the United States or China, and its historical contribution to carbon already in the atmosphere is much smaller than that of countries that industrialised earlier. That combination shapes India's central argument: climate justice.

The principle India invokes is Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC), from the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, adopted in 1992. In plain terms, all countries share responsibility, but developed countries, which emitted most historically and have greater capacity, should do more and should support developing countries with finance and technology. India worked with the BASIC group — Brazil, South Africa, India and China — and with the wider G77 developing-country bloc to defend that principle from the Kyoto Protocol of 1997, which set binding targets only for developed countries, to the Paris Agreement of 2015, which set a universal goal of holding warming well below 2°C, preferably to 1.5°C, while retaining differentiation in capability and support.

India's own pledges under Paris, known as Nationally Determined Contributions, include reducing the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 compared with 2005, reaching 50% of installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030, and achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 — a target announced at COP26 in Glasgow in 2021. Critics note that 2070 is later than China's 2060 target and the European Union's 2050 target; supporters reply that India's development needs and lower per-person emissions justify a later date, provided the path to it is credible.

India has also tried to lead, not only to negotiate. The International Solar Alliance, co-founded with France at COP21 in Paris in 2015 and headquartered in Gurugram, now has more than 120 member countries and aims to mobilise solar investment in developing countries. The Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure, launched in 2019, focuses on climate-proofing infrastructure. At the same time, India has pushed at COP meetings for climate finance — developed countries' long-standing $100 billion a year pledge was not fully delivered by 2020 — and for a Loss and Damage Fund, agreed in principle at COP27 in 2022, to help countries facing climate harm they did not mainly cause.

Key takeaways

  • India's climate argument rests on CBDR-RC: shared responsibility, differentiated by history and capability.
  • Its Paris pledges include a 45% emissions-intensity cut by 2030 and net zero by 2070.
  • The International Solar Alliance and the push for finance and loss-and-damage funding show India as agenda-setter, not only as a negotiator.

Why it matters for civil studies: Climate questions link international law, economics and equity, and they reward answers that explain why a developing country can demand finance while still cutting its own emissions intensity.

Discussion question: Is India's 2070 net-zero date a fair reflection of climate justice, or does it place too much burden on future generations in India and elsewhere?
