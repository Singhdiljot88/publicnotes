State elections vs national elections: why results differ

Note 050 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

It surprises many beginners that a party can win a state election and lose the national election in the same state a few months later — or the reverse. The surprise fades once the two elections are seen as different contests, even when the same voters take part. They choose different governments, judge different records, and often face different candidates and alliances.

A Lok Sabha election chooses members of Parliament and, in practice, decides who forms the central government. Voters may weigh national leadership, foreign policy, national security, the national economy, and the prime minister's record. A state assembly election chooses members of the Legislative Assembly and decides who forms the state government. There, voters are more likely to judge roads, water, schools, health services, policing, local jobs, and the chief minister's record — although national issues can still spill over, and state leaders often try to nationalise or localise a campaign depending on what helps them.

Timing also matters. Until 1967, Lok Sabha and most assembly elections were usually held together. After dissolutions in the late 1960s and the mid-term Lok Sabha election of 1971, the cycles separated, so most states now vote on their own timetables, with five-year terms ending at different times. That separation gives voters a chance to send different messages at different moments. A state result midway through a central government's term is sometimes read as a verdict on Delhi, but it may also be a verdict on the state government, on local candidates, or on an issue that barely featured nationally.

Alliances can differ, too. Parties that fight together nationally may compete in a state, and parties that are rivals nationally may cooperate locally. Candidate quality matters more in smaller assembly constituencies, where a familiar local worker, a community network, or a record of solving neighbourhood problems can outweigh a national slogan.

The proposal for simultaneous elections — often called "One Nation, One Election" — is part of this debate. Supporters argue it would cut costs, reduce repeated campaign disruption, and let governments focus on governing. Critics argue it could blur state and national issues, disadvantage regional parties, and create practical problems if a government falls early. It had not become the settled system as of late 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Lok Sabha and assembly elections choose different governments and invite different judgements.
  • Separate cycles, different alliances, and local candidates all help explain split results.
  • Simultaneous elections are a debated proposal, not an established fact.

Why it matters for civil studies: federalism is tested at the ballot box, because voters can reward one level of government while punishing another.

Discussion question: If state and national elections were held on the same day, would voters make clearer choices, or would state issues be crowded out by national campaigns?
