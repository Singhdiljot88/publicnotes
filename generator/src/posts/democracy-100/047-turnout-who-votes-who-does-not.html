Turnout: who votes, who does not, and why it matters

Note 047 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Turnout is the share of registered electors who actually vote. It is not the same as enthusiasm in a crowd, and it is not the same as a party's vote share. A party can win with modest turnout if its supporters turn out more reliably than others, and high turnout does not automatically favour any one side. That is why analysts study who votes, not just how many.

In the 2024 Lok Sabha election, Election Commission data pointed to turnout of roughly two-thirds of electors — about 66%, compared with about 67.4% in 2019. The precise final figure varies slightly between initial and later ECI releases, because postal ballots and final verification are added over time, so small differences alone are not evidence of a problem. What is clearer from the ECI's December 2024 dataset is the scale: more than 64 crore votes were cast, and women's turnout (65.78%) slightly exceeded men's (65.55%), continuing a trend in which the historic gap has narrowed and, in recent elections, reversed in aggregate terms.

Turnout also varies by place and group. Assam recorded among the highest state turnouts in 2024, while Bihar recorded among the lowest, according to ECI figures reported at the time. Urban voters, migrants, young first-time voters, and people who have moved for work often face practical barriers: registration at a new address, travel home on polling day, work schedules, or unfamiliarity with the process. Weather, a multi-phase election, and local security conditions can also matter. Surveys of non-voters find a mix of practical reasons, disinterest, and the belief that voting will not change outcomes — not a single explanation.

Why should a civil studies reader care? First, low or uneven turnout can mean the elected body reflects some groups more than others. Second, parties may target reliable voters rather than persuading new ones, which can narrow the agenda. Third, turnout debates are often used politically: a fall is sometimes presented as disillusionment, and a rise as endorsement, when either could reflect weather, migration, or better registration. The careful question is always: turnout of whom, where, and compared with what?

Key takeaways

  • Turnout measures registered electors who vote, not total population or crowd size.
  • In 2024, overall turnout was about 66%, with women's turnout slightly above men's.
  • Place, migration, work, and registration barriers help explain uneven turnout.

Why it matters for civil studies: uneven turnout can skew representation, even in a free election, because the voters who stay home are not randomly distributed.

Discussion question: Should making voting easier for internal migrants be a higher priority than raising overall turnout — and what safeguards would remote or postal voting need?
