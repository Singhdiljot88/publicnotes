Television news and the TRP economy: what drives the debate

Note 052 · Theme 6: Media & information · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Television Rating Points, or TRPs, are estimates of how many people watch a programme or channel. In India, the Broadcast Audience Research Council, known as BARC, measures television viewing through a panel of metered homes and converts that sample into ratings. Advertisers use those ratings to decide where to spend money, and channels use them to set advertising rates, plan programmes, and argue that they are popular. In that sense, TRPs are the television industry's common currency: they connect audiences, broadcasters, and advertisers.

Because ratings affect revenue, they also affect editorial choices. A debate show, a breaking-news banner, or a dramatic headline may attract viewers in the short term, even if it adds little new information. Critics argue that this creates an incentive for loud, fast, and conflict-driven coverage, especially in prime time, when competition for attention is highest. They also argue that ratings reward repetition of a few dramatic stories over slower reporting on courts, budgets, health, or local government. Broadcasters and their supporters reply that ratings reflect what viewers choose to watch, that popular does not automatically mean false, and that serious programmes also need an audience to survive commercially.

Measurement itself is contested. A panel is a sample, not a census of every television home, so its design matters: which homes are included, how rural and regional viewers are covered, and how viewing on a landing page or through other routes is counted can change the result. In 2020, allegations of TRP manipulation led to a major controversy and to scrutiny of the measurement system, and news ratings were suspended in October 2020 before resuming on 17 March 2022 with revised methods, including a four-week rolling average for news. The episode is a reminder that a number can look precise while still depending on methods, audits, and trust. Students should therefore ask who measures, who audits, and whether the sample represents the audience being claimed.

There is also a difference between popularity and public value. A channel can top a ratings table in a particular week and still miss an important story, while a smaller channel can break a story that later becomes central. Ratings are useful for understanding the business of television, but they are a weak test of accuracy, fairness, or importance on their own.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • TRPs estimate viewing from a sample of metered homes and strongly influence advertising revenue and programming decisions.
  • The chase for ratings can favour dramatic, fast coverage, though broadcasters argue that ratings also reflect genuine viewer choice.
  • A rating is only as trustworthy as its sample, method, and audit; past manipulation allegations show why method matters.

Why it matters for civil studies: Television still shapes what many voters notice first, so understanding the TRP economy helps explain why some issues become loud debates and others stay quiet.

Discussion question: If ratings were published with more detail about the sample and method, would that change how viewers, advertisers, or channels behaved — and why?
