Social media and politics: WhatsApp, YouTube, and short video

Note 053 · Theme 6: Media & information · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

For many Indians, politics now arrives first on a phone rather than in a newspaper or on a television bulletin. WhatsApp groups connect families, neighbourhoods, workplaces, and party supporters. YouTube offers long explainers, interviews, and live streams in many languages. Short-video platforms offer clips that can be watched in seconds, shared quickly, and understood without high literacy in English. Together, they have lowered the cost of publishing: a local activist, a candidate, or a creator can reach an audience without owning a printing press or a television channel.

That openness has both democratic strengths and risks. Supporters point to wider participation, faster local reporting, and a voice for people and languages that national studios may ignore. A video about a broken road, a school, or a local market can travel beyond the district where it was filmed. Critics point to the opposite effect: closed WhatsApp groups can circulate claims without a public correction, short clips can remove context, and recommendation systems can keep showing a user more of what they already agree with. The same tool can therefore inform, mobilise, entertain, or mislead, depending on who made the content and how it spreads.

Political parties and candidates use these platforms in different ways. Some use them for direct messaging, volunteer coordination, and fundraising appeals. Others use creators and influencers to explain a policy, respond to an opponent, or build a personal image. This raises questions that older media rules did not have to answer in the same form: when is a creator's video journalism, advertising, or campaign material? Should it be labelled? Who is responsible if it is false? Platforms have introduced steps such as forwarding limits, labels, and reporting tools, but their effectiveness is debated, and enforcement can vary by language and region.

For a civil studies reader, the key shift is from a small number of gatekeepers to many gatekeepers, including algorithms. A claim may go viral because it is true, because it is emotional, because a large network shared it, or because a platform promoted it. Asking who created it, what evidence it shows, and whether it appears in an independent source is therefore more important than asking how many times it was shared.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • WhatsApp, YouTube, and short video have widened who can publish politics and in which languages, beyond traditional newsrooms.
  • Closed groups, short clips, and recommendations can spread claims quickly while stripping away context or correction.
  • Virality is not proof of truth; it can reflect emotion, network size, or platform design as much as accuracy.

Why it matters for civil studies: Campaigns, public opinion, and even protests are now organised partly online, so citizens need to understand platform power as well as party power.

Discussion question: Should a political video by an independent creator be treated like journalism, advertising, or something else — and what label would help a viewer decide?
