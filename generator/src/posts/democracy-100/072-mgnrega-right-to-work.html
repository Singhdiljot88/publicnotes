MGNREGA and the right to work in practice

Note 072 · Theme 8: Economy & welfare · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005, made an unusual promise: any rural household whose adult members volunteer for unskilled manual work can demand wage employment, originally for up to 100 days in a financial year. The aim was to turn work from a favour into a legal entitlement, especially in lean agricultural seasons and crises. The design also envisaged an unemployment allowance if work was not provided in time, although that safeguard's implementation has long been debated.

A Press Information Bureau release of 26 August 2025 reported 290.60 crore person-days generated in 2024–25, with women accounting for 58.15% of participation. It put the 2025–26 budget allocation at Rs 86,000 crore, described as the highest since the scheme began. Supporters point to this scale, to assets such as ponds and water conservation works, and to income for women and poorer households. Critics point to a different problem: many households do not receive anything close to 100 days, and delays in wage payment, measurement of work, and local planning can weaken the guarantee in practice.

In December 2025, Parliament passed the Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 (VB–G RAM G), replacing MGNREGA. The Act received the President's assent on 21 December 2025 and took effect nationwide from 1 July 2026. Government summaries describe a higher guarantee of 125 days. Critics argue the new framework moves from a demand-driven, rights-based model toward advance allocations, greater state cost-sharing, and seasonal pauses, and question whether funding and delivery will match the headline number. Supporters of reform argue that better planning and convergence with other rural programmes can improve assets and reduce waste. Readers should therefore separate the number of days from the rules deciding who gets work, when, and who pays.

Statistics can also be read in opposite ways. Falling demand for scheme work can mean better farm or non-farm jobs are available, or that workers are discouraged or unable to register. Rising demand can mean distress, or that the scheme is finally reaching people who need it. Local evidence on registration, days actually worked, wage timeliness, and asset quality is therefore more informative than a single national total.

Key takeaways

  • MGNREGA was designed as a demand-driven legal entitlement, not only as relief.
  • Official 2024–25 data show large scale and high women's participation, alongside a gap to 100 days.
  • The 2025 replacement raises the headline to 125 days, while changing funding and planning rules.

Why it matters for civil studies: It tests whether a legal right can survive budget limits, federal cost-sharing, and weak local administration.

Discussion question: Is a guaranteed number of days meaningful if funding, registration, and wage payment are not guaranteed with the same force?
