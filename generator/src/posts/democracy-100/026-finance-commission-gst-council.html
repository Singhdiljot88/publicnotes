Finance Commission and GST Council: Sharing Money, Sharing Power

Note 026 · Theme 3: Parliament & federalism · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Two institutions sit at the heart of how money moves between the Centre and the states: the Finance Commission and the GST Council. They work differently, and that difference tells you a lot about Indian federalism. The Finance Commission is a temporary, expert body; the GST Council is a permanent, political forum. One recommends how a shared pool of taxes should be divided; the other decides, jointly, how a major shared tax should work.

Under Article 280, the President constitutes a Finance Commission every five years, consisting of a chairperson and four other members. Its core job is to recommend how the net proceeds of taxes that are to be shared should be divided between the Union and the states, and then among the states themselves — the vertical and horizontal shares — as well as the principles for grants-in-aid to states. Its recommendations are advisory, not binding, but governments have generally accepted the broad devolution formula it proposes, and its report is laid before Parliament. Because states differ hugely in population, income and needs, the formula always involves judgement: should a state get more because it has more people, because it is poorer, or because it has controlled population growth or raised its own revenue well? Different answers favour different states, which is why each Commission's terms of reference are watched so closely.

The GST Council, created by the 101st Constitutional Amendment of 2016 under Article 279A, works by shared decision-making. It is chaired by the Union Finance Minister and includes the minister in charge of finance or taxation, or another minister nominated by the government, of every state. Decisions require at least three-fourths of weighted votes, with the Centre holding one-third of the weight and all states together holding two-thirds — so neither side can decide alone, but the Centre cannot be outvoted by a small group of states either. The Council recommends rates, exemptions and other GST matters. Supporters present it as cooperative federalism in action; critics note that states gave up much of their independent indirect-tax power when GST was introduced, and argue about whether compensation and rate decisions have fairly reflected that bargain.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • The Finance Commission (Article 280) recommends tax-sharing between the Centre and states, and among states, every five years.
  • The GST Council (Article 279A) is chaired by the Union Finance Minister, includes all state finance ministers (or ministers nominated by state governments), and takes decisions by a three-fourths weighted majority, in the form of recommendations to the Union and the states.
  • Both bodies force a political judgement: how to balance population, need, performance and autonomy in sharing money.

Why it matters for civil studies: Fiscal federalism decides what states can actually afford to do — a power on paper means little without the revenue to exercise it.

Discussion question: Should tax-sharing reward states that perform well, support states that need most, or try to do both — and who should decide the balance?
