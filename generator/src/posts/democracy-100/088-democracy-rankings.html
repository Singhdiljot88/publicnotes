Democracy rankings: how India is rated, and why rankings are debated

Note 088 · Theme 9: Foreign policy & democracy · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Every year, several international projects rate how democratic countries are. They often reach different conclusions about India, and that disagreement itself is worth studying. Rankings are not elections, and they are not court judgments; they are expert assessments built from indicators, surveys and coding rules. Learning how they work helps you use them without over-claiming what they prove.

Three projects are most cited. Freedom House, in its Freedom in the World reports, downgraded India from "Free" to "Partly Free" in 2021, a category India has remained in since, with scores in the 60s out of 100 in recent editions — the exact score varies by edition, so a single year's number should be quoted with its year. The Sweden-based Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) Institute classifies India in its recent reports as an "electoral autocracy": a country that holds elections, but where, in V-Dem's assessment, civil liberties, media freedom and civil-society space are sufficiently constrained to remove it from the "electoral democracy" category. The Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index, by contrast, classifies India as a "flawed democracy", with a score of 7.29 out of 10 in its 2024 index, published in 2025, reflecting strong electoral processes alongside weaker civil liberties and political culture scores. Different labels do not mean one project is lying; they mean the projects measure democracy differently.

The Indian government and its supporters contest these assessments. They point to regular, competitive elections, high voter turnout, judicial decisions against government measures, and opposition parties winning state elections. They argue, and a 2022 working paper by members of the Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister also argued, that perception-based indices rely on small groups of experts, use subjective questions, apply the same questions to very different systems, and omit factors the critics consider relevant. V-Dem and other index producers respond that expert assessment is necessary because many democratic qualities, such as judicial independence or media pressure, cannot be captured by headcounts alone.

For a civil studies reader, the disciplined approach is to cite the project, year and indicator, separate electoral democracy (free and fair elections) from liberal democracy (rights, rule of law and checks), and ask what evidence would change your mind. A ranking can start a debate; it cannot settle it by itself.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Freedom House rates India "Partly Free" since 2021; V-Dem calls it an "electoral autocracy"; the Economist Intelligence Unit calls it a "flawed democracy".
  • The labels differ because the projects define and weight democracy differently, not only because they disagree on facts.
  • Indian critics challenge methodology and expert subjectivity; index producers defend expert coding as necessary for qualities that statistics alone miss.

Why it matters for civil studies: Ranking questions test source criticism — you must name the index, its year and its definition before you draw a conclusion from it.

Discussion question: Should a democracy be judged mainly by its elections, or mainly by the rights and checks that operate between elections — and can one ranking capture both?
