Sports, culture, and national identity in politics

Note 097 · Theme 10: Youth & future · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Few things unite a diverse country as quickly as a sporting moment. A cricket final, a hockey medal, a football derby, or a young athlete from a small town reaching a global stage can create a shared language across language, region, and religion. Politicians know this, which is why victories are celebrated in Parliament, in assemblies, and on social media, and why stadiums, academies, and sports budgets become political announcements. Culture works similarly. Films, music, festivals, and food can express a national story, a regional pride, or a local identity, sometimes all at once.

That power is also why sport and culture become contested. One view treats national identity as broad and inclusive: cheering for India does not require agreeing on language, faith, or politics. Another view worries that identity can be narrowed, so only one sport, language, festival, or version of history is treated as truly national. Both concerns appear in debates about women's sport. The International Cricket Council reported that nearly 300,000 fans attended matches across India and Sri Lanka during the 2025 Women's Cricket World Cup, which ended with India lifting the trophy. Supporters read that as growing recognition for women's cricket. Others caution that tournament attendance alone does not prove equal pay, facilities, media coverage, or grassroots investment. Celebration and structural change are not the same thing.

Young people are central as players, fans, creators, and critics. A young fan can make a chant go viral, question a selection, or call out abuse of an athlete. A young artist can remix a folk form for a new audience or challenge an old stereotype. At the same time, young athletes and artists can be used as symbols by parties, sponsors, and governments, praised when they win and ignored when they ask for better training, safety, or contracts. The test for a democratic culture is whether it protects the person behind the symbol, including the right to speak, dissent, and be safe from harassment. Stadiums tell part of the story: the Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad has a seating capacity of around 132,000 according to the ICC, showing how sport, city pride, and public investment intersect. Size, however, is not a measure of fairness in who gets to play.

Key takeaways

  • Sport and culture can build shared identity, but they can also be used to narrow who counts as national.
  • Celebration of a win does not by itself prove equal investment in women's sport or grassroots facilities.
  • Young players, fans, and artists are both makers of culture and symbols used by others; protecting their voice matters.

Why it matters for civil studies: National identity is made in stadiums, screens, and festivals as well as in laws, so cultural politics is part of democratic politics.

Discussion question: When a politician celebrates a sporting victory, how can voters tell whether it is genuine pride, a policy announcement, or an election message?
