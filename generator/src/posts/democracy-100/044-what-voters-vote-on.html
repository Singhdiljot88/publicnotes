What voters vote on: identity, development, welfare, or leadership?

Note 044 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Voters rarely vote on one thing. Surveys and election studies in India usually find a mix: everyday economic concerns, welfare benefits, local development, leadership, party loyalty, and social identity. The hard question is not which single factor matters, but how the mix changes from election to election and from place to place.

The Centre for the Study of Developing Societies (CSDS)–Lokniti pre-poll survey before the 2024 general election is a useful example. Unemployment was named as the most important issue by 27% of respondents, up from 11% in the 2019 post-poll survey, while price rise rose from 4% to 23%. Development was also prominent. In the post-poll survey, some voters praised cultural and religious initiatives, while others cited inflation, unemployment, and poverty as dislikes. The same election can therefore contain different voter stories at the same time.

Welfare is a second part of the mix. Schemes delivering food, housing, bank accounts, health cover, or cash support can affect how households judge a government, especially where incomes are uncertain. Supporters of incumbents often point to delivery as evidence of performance; critics argue welfare can be used to buy support or distract from jobs and prices. Researchers try to separate these claims by asking whether voters reward delivery, punish failure, or simply vote for the party they already identify with — and the answers are rarely neat.

Leadership is a third factor. In campaigns centred on a prime-ministerial or chief-ministerial face, voters may judge a party by its leader's image. But leadership effects are hard to isolate: a popular leader may also benefit from a strong organisation, a favourable economy, or a weak opposition. Identity — including caste, religion, language, and region — is a fourth factor, explored in a later note. It can shape trust and candidate choice, but it does not mechanically decide every vote, as the same social group often splits between parties.

For civil studies readers, the lesson is methodological as much as political. A single exit poll, viral video, or rally crowd is weak evidence about why millions voted as they did. Better questions ask: who was surveyed, when, what question was asked, and whether the finding holds across states, age groups, and income levels?

Key takeaways

  • Economic concerns such as jobs and prices were prominent in CSDS–Lokniti's 2024 surveys.
  • Welfare delivery, leadership, and identity interact; none explains voting on its own.
  • Survey wording, timing, and sample all affect what "the voter" appears to want.

Why it matters for civil studies: policy debate improves when claims about "what voters want" are tested against survey evidence rather than assumed from one result.

Discussion question: If jobs, prices, and welfare all matter, how would you design a survey question that separates a voter's long-term party loyalty from their judgement of the current government?
