Free speech cases: where courts draw the line

Note 036 · Theme 4: Judiciary · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression to citizens, but it is not absolute. Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions on specified grounds, including sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, public order, decency and morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offence. Much of free-speech law is therefore about drawing lines: what counts as a reasonable restriction, how precise the law must be, and who decides.

The leading internet-era example is Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, decided on 24 March 2015. Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, inserted in 2008, criminalised sending information through a computer or communication device that was grossly offensive, menacing, or caused annoyance or inconvenience, with imprisonment of up to three years. The Supreme Court struck down Section 66A in its entirety, holding that it violated Article 19(1)(a) and was not saved by Article 19(2). The Court found the terms vague and overbroad, creating a chilling effect: people might avoid lawful speech for fear of prosecution. It distinguished discussion and advocacy, which are protected, from incitement, which can be restricted. In the same judgment, the Court upheld the blocking provision in Section 69A and read down intermediary liability in Section 79.

That distinction still frames debates today. Supporters of stronger regulation argue that online speech can spread misinformation, hate, and threats at speed, and that platforms and the state need effective tools. Supporters of stronger protection argue that vague offences, broad takedown powers, and criminal defamation or contempt can silence criticism, journalism, and satire. Courts are often asked to decide not whether speech is agreeable, but whether the law restricting it is clear, proportionate, and tied to a ground in Article 19(2).

Students should note what courts do not do in these cases. They do not declare that all speech is protected, nor do they approve every restriction the state proposes. They test the law, not just the speaker.

Key takeaways

  • Article 19(1)(a) protects speech; Article 19(2) allows only reasonable restrictions on listed grounds.
  • In Shreya Singhal (2015), the Supreme Court struck down Section 66A as vague, overbroad, and chilling.
  • Courts distinguish protected discussion and advocacy from incitement that may lawfully be restricted.

Why it matters for civil studies: Free-speech cases show how Fundamental Rights are balanced against public order, reputation, and state security in a democracy.

Discussion question: When a law uses words like "offensive" or "annoying", who should decide what they mean — the police, a court, or Parliament in clearer language?
