Inequality: growth, wealth, and who benefits

Note 076 · Theme 8: Economy & welfare · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Economic growth can raise average incomes while leaving distribution almost unchanged, or it can narrow gaps if wages, assets, and public services spread more widely. That distinction is why inequality is a democratic question, not only an economic one: it affects who can afford good schooling and health care, who can take risks such as starting a business, and whose voice carries weight in politics.

The World Inequality Report 2026, from the World Inequality Lab, estimates that in 2025 the top 10% in India captured 58% of national income, the top 1% captured 22.6%, and the bottom 50% received 15%. For wealth, it estimates that the top 10% owned 65% of household wealth and the top 1% owned about 40%. These are modelled estimates, not a census of every household, and their methods are debated, including over tax data, survey coverage, and informal income and assets. Readers should treat them as one influential estimate, to be read alongside official surveys and other studies, rather than as an undisputed count.

Other evidence points in a different direction on poverty. A World Bank summary cited in recent reporting estimated that extreme poverty in India fell from 27.1% in 2011–12 to 5.3% in 2022–23. Supporters of this reading argue that growth, connectivity, digital access, and welfare transfers have improved living standards for many poorer households. Critics reply that escaping extreme poverty is not the same as secure prosperity, and that gaps in income, wealth, land, education, and health can persist even when poverty falls. Both claims can be partly true: a society can become less extremely poor and more unequal at the top at the same time.

Inequality in India also runs through caste, gender, region, and rural-urban location. Access to land, credit, formal jobs, and quality services is uneven, and women's labour income share remains low in inequality research. Policy proposals therefore differ. Some economists and activists propose wealth or inheritance taxes, stronger public services, and better enforcement of labour and land rights. Others argue that faster investment, job creation, and ease of doing business will broaden opportunity more sustainably, and warn that high taxes on capital could reduce investment. The debate is ultimately about sequence and fairness: whether redistribution should lead, follow, or accompany growth.

Key takeaways

  • World Inequality Report 2026 estimates very high income and wealth concentration, with methods that remain debated.
  • Poverty reduction and rising top-end concentration can occur together and must not be confused.
  • Caste, gender, region, and access to assets shape inequality beyond income alone.

Why it matters for civil studies: Inequality links growth models, tax choices, social justice, and the equal political voice democracy promises.

Discussion question: If growth lifts many people out of extreme poverty but the top share keeps rising, should that be judged a success, a warning, or both?
