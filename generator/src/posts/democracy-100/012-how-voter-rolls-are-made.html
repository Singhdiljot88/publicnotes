How voter rolls are made: enrolment, revision, and deletion rules

Note 012 · Theme 2: Election Commission & voter rolls · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

An electoral roll, or voter list, is the official list of people entitled to vote in a constituency. Without a correct roll, even a well-run polling day cannot guarantee that every eligible person can vote, or that no ineligible entry distorts the result. That is why roll preparation is treated as a core electoral task, not paperwork.

The basic qualifications come from the Constitution and the Representation of the People Act, 1950. In general, a person must be an Indian citizen, aged 18 or above, and ordinarily resident in the constituency. Article 325 says no one may be excluded only on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex, and Article 326 provides for adult suffrage. There are four qualifying dates in a year — 1 January, 1 April, 1 July and 1 October — so a person turning 18 can enrol at the next qualifying date.

Rolls are kept up to date in several ways. In continuous updating, individuals apply during the year. In summary revision, the existing roll is published as a draft, checked, and corrected. In intensive or special revision, officials go further, often with door-to-door enumeration, to rebuild or thoroughly verify the roll. At each stage, draft rolls are published and a claims-and-objections period follows, so citizens and parties can check entries.

Three forms matter most. Form 6 seeks inclusion of a new elector, Form 7 objects to an inclusion or seeks deletion of an entry, and Form 8 seeks correction or shifting of residence. The Electoral Registration Officer decides applications after verification. Deletion is not supposed to be automatic: the usual grounds are death, permanent shifting, duplicate enrolment, or loss of eligibility, and the rules provide for notice and an opportunity to be heard before an entry is removed. In practice, how fully that hearing happens in a large, fast revision is one of the issues debated in 2025–2026.

Key takeaways

  • Eligibility turns mainly on citizenship, age 18 or above, and ordinary residence.
  • Rolls are revised continuously and through summary or intensive exercises, with a draft and a claims period.
  • Inclusion, objection or deletion, and correction use Forms 6, 7 and 8, decided by the ERO after verification.

Why it matters for civil studies: The roll is the gateway to voting, so its rules decide who can actually exercise a right that looks universal on paper.

Discussion question: What safeguards would you design so a roll can be cleaned of errors without wrongly removing a person who has simply moved house or missed a visit?
