Farmers' movements: how agrarian protest shapes policy

Note 062 · Theme 7: Civil society & protest · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Farmers' movements in India have repeatedly turned questions about prices, markets, debt and land into national political questions. Farming is not only an economic activity for many households; it is tied to food security, rural livelihoods, caste and regional identity, and state procurement systems. When farmers organise through unions, cooperatives and village networks, they can sustain protests far longer than a single demonstration, and they can bring rural concerns into urban and parliamentary debate.

The 2020–21 farmers' protest is the best-known recent example. Parliament passed three farm laws in September 2020. The government presented them as a way to widen farmers' choices, encourage private investment and open more routes to market outside state-regulated wholesale markets, or mandis. Many farmer unions, led in the Delhi-border camps by the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, argued that the laws would weaken procurement and the minimum support price (MSP) system over time and leave small farmers exposed to large buyers. The government disputed that interpretation and said MSP would continue. After months of camps at Singhu, Tikri and Ghazipur, a Supreme Court stay on 12 January 2021, and failed talks, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced on 19 November 2021 that the laws would be repealed. Parliament repealed them later that month, and the protest was suspended in December 2021. A legal guarantee for MSP, a central demand of the unions, was not enacted as part of that repeal, and the issue has remained contested.

Earlier agrarian politics worked differently. State-level movements have pressed for loan waivers, higher MSPs, irrigation, electricity and relief after drought or crop failure. Some have used long marches, such as marches to state capitals, to make rural distress visible. Others have worked through electoral pressure, asking parties to adopt farm demands in manifestos. Critics of large farm protests argue that prolonged blockades impose costs on commuters, traders and other farmers. Supporters argue that without sustained pressure, rural issues are easily ignored between elections.

For young readers, the lesson is not that protest always wins. It is that organisation, a clear demand, broad alliances and timing — including elections and parliamentary sessions — affect whether a movement changes law, changes implementation, or only changes public debate.

Key takeaways

  • Agrarian protest links prices, markets, debt and livelihoods to wider questions of food and rural policy.
  • The 2020–21 protest ended with repeal of the three farm laws, but without a statutory MSP guarantee at that time.
  • Duration, organisation and alliances often matter as much as the size of a single rally.

Why it matters for civil studies: Farmers' movements show how organised groups outside Parliament can set the agenda, force negotiation, and test whether a reform has democratic consent.

Discussion question: Why might a government repeal a law after sustained protest even if it still believes the reform was economically sound?
