One Nation, One Election: The Idea, the Arguments For and Against

Note 030 · Theme 3: Parliament & federalism · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

"One Nation, One Election" is the proposal to hold elections to the Lok Sabha and all state legislative assemblies at the same time, rather than on staggered calendars that produce elections somewhere in India almost every year. India did in fact hold simultaneous elections in its early decades, until 1967; the cycle broke when some assemblies were dissolved in 1968 and 1969 and the Lok Sabha was dissolved in 1970, after which national and state polls drifted apart. The idea has returned repeatedly since, and a high-level committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind, set up in September 2023, submitted its report in March 2024 recommending a phased return to simultaneous elections, with local-body polls to follow within a fixed window.

Supporters make a governance and efficiency case. They argue that repeated elections mean repeated enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct, which can pause new policy announcements and development work; that teachers, officials and security forces are repeatedly diverted to election duty; and that governments spend much of their time in campaign mode rather than governing. Simultaneous polls, on this view, would free up administrative capacity, reduce overall election costs and give governments a clearer five-year run to implement policy.

Critics make a federalism and accountability case. They argue that tying state calendars to the national calendar would cut short assembly terms, limit a state's freedom to change its government mid-term, and let national issues and larger national parties overshadow state issues and regional parties. They also raise practical questions: what happens when a government loses its majority early, how many voting machines and security personnel would be needed at once, and whether voters would treat a combined ballot as one national choice rather than two distinct ones. The constitutional route is also demanding: bills introduced in December 2024, including the Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024, were referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, and as of mid-2026 reporting they had not been passed, with the committee's term extended to the Winter Session of 2026. The proposal therefore remains a live debate, not a settled change.

Key takeaways

  • One Nation, One Election would synchronise Lok Sabha and state assembly polls, as India did until 1967.
  • Supporters cite less disruption from the Model Code of Conduct, lower costs and longer governing runs; critics cite federalism, state issues being overshadowed, and mid-term government collapse.
  • The Kovind Committee reported in March 2024, and amendment bills introduced in December 2024 were still before a Joint Parliamentary Committee in 2026 reporting.

Why it matters for civil studies: The proposal forces a choice between two democratic goods — stable, efficient government and frequent, separate accountability at each level.

Discussion question: If simultaneous elections were adopted, what safeguard would matter most — fixed terms, fresh polls only for the remaining term, or something else?
