Money in elections: bonds, donations, and transparency debates

Note 046 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Elections cost money: rallies, travel, advertising, staff, and materials all need funding. The democratic question is not whether campaigns cost money, but where the money comes from, whether it is disclosed, and whether large donations create influence that ordinary voters cannot match. India has debated all three questions for decades, and no settlement has ended the argument.

The main legal channels have included individual donations, corporate donations, and electoral trusts, which act as intermediaries that collect and pass on donations. Parties must file contribution reports with the Election Commission, and candidates face spending limits, but party-level spending and indirect campaigning are harder to track. Critics argue this leaves gaps; supporters argue disclosure rules and audits already provide checks.

Electoral bonds became the centre of the debate. Introduced in 2018, they allowed donors to buy bonds from the State Bank of India and give them to political parties, while the donor's identity was not disclosed to the public. The government of the day presented the scheme as a way to move donations into formal banking channels and reduce cash. Critics, including the Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR), argued that anonymity prevented voters from knowing who funded a party and created risks of quid pro quo — an exchange of favours — even though such exchanges are difficult to prove in any individual case.

On 15 February 2024, a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court struck down the electoral bonds scheme as unconstitutional, holding that it violated citizens' right to information about political funding. The Court directed the State Bank of India to disclose purchaser and encashment data to the Election Commission, which was to publish it. ADR analyses of the disclosed data reported that the BJP had received a large share of bond donations, a finding its critics cite and its supporters contextualise by pointing to its electoral size and to donations received by other parties.

Since then, debate has moved to what should replace bonds: fuller real-time disclosure, state funding of elections, tighter caps, electoral trusts with clearer rules, or stronger transparency obligations for parties. Each proposal has trade-offs. State funding could reduce dependence on private donors but would use public money and raise questions about which parties qualify. Full disclosure could inform voters but might, some donors argue, expose them to pressure.

Key takeaways

  • Campaign finance is about sources and disclosure, not only about total spending.
  • Electoral bonds (2018–2024) allowed anonymous donations until the Supreme Court struck the scheme down.
  • Reform proposals disagree on whether transparency, state funding, or caps best protect fairness.

Why it matters for civil studies: who pays for politics can shape which issues are raised, which candidates can compete, and how much voters trust the result.

Discussion question: If you had to choose one reform — full public disclosure, a cap on donations, or public funding — which would you choose first, and what problem would it leave unsolved?
