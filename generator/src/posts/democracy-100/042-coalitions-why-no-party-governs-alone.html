Coalitions: why no party governs alone anymore

Note 042 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

This title is shorthand and needs a qualification. Parties have governed alone in seats at times — the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) won a Lok Sabha majority on its own in 2014 and 2019, while leading the wider National Democratic Alliance (NDA). The deeper point is that since the late 1980s, no single party has been able to take India's diversity for granted, and governments have usually depended on alliances, partners, or outside support.

The turning point most textbooks cite is 1989. After decades of Congress dominance, the 1989 election produced no single-party majority, and a National Front government took office with support from parties that did not join it. The 1990s then brought the United Front governments of 1996–1998, the BJP-led NDA from 1998, and, after 2004, the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance (UPA), which governed until 2014. Each combined national parties with state and regional parties that brought votes, seats, and local knowledge — and their own demands.

Why did this happen? India's first-past-the-post system rewards the largest vote in each constituency, not necessarily a majority of votes. At the same time, voters in different states care about different languages, economies, and histories, and regional parties have grown to represent those concerns. A national party strong in one region may be weak in another, so alliances become a practical way to reach the 272 seats needed for a Lok Sabha majority out of 543.

Coalitions have strengths and tensions. Supporters argue they force negotiation and give smaller states a voice in Delhi. Critics argue they can slow decisions, encourage bargaining over ministries, and blur responsibility for a decision. Both effects can be true at different times, depending on how disciplined partners are and how clear their shared programme is.

The 2024 election brought the point back into focus. The BJP won 240 seats, short of a majority alone, while the NDA won 293 and formed the government, with the Telugu Desam Party and Janata Dal (United) as its two main allies. The opposition INDIA alliance won 234 seats. That did not end single-party politics, but it showed again that arithmetic, federal diversity, and alliance management remain central to forming a government.

Key takeaways

  • A Lok Sabha majority needs 272 of 543 seats, which often requires partners.
  • Coalitions became routine after 1989 as regional parties grew stronger.
  • Coalitions can broaden representation, but they can also blur responsibility and slow decisions.

Why it matters for civil studies: coalition arithmetic explains cabinet formation, policy compromises, and why a party's vote share and its power in government are not the same thing.

Discussion question: Is a coalition government more democratic because it represents more voters, or less accountable because responsibility is shared — or can it be both?
