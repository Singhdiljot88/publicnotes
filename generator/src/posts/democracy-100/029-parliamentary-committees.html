Parliamentary Committees: The Quiet Work Behind the Noise

Note 029 · Theme 3: Parliament & federalism · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Television coverage of Parliament tends to show the noise: shouting, adjournments and walkouts. Much of Parliament's real work happens elsewhere, in committees — smaller groups of members, drawn from different parties, who examine bills, budgets and government performance in detail. A full House of hundreds of members cannot go through a bill clause by clause, question officials for hours, or audit spending line by line. Committees can, and that is why they are often called mini-parliaments or the workshops of Parliament.

There are two broad kinds. Standing committees are permanent and reconstituted regularly; ad hoc committees are set up for a specific purpose, such as a Joint Parliamentary Committee to investigate a particular issue. Among standing committees, the Department-related Standing Committees — 24 committees covering groups of ministries — examine bills referred to them, demands for grants and the working of ministries. The three financial committees are especially important: the Public Accounts Committee (22 members, 15 from the Lok Sabha and 7 from the Rajya Sabha, chaired by convention by an opposition member) examines the Comptroller and Auditor General's audit reports and whether money was spent as Parliament authorised; the Estimates Committee (30 members, all from the Lok Sabha) looks for economy and efficiency in proposed spending; and the Committee on Public Undertakings examines public sector enterprises.

Committees work differently from the floor of the House. They usually meet away from cameras, take evidence from officials and experts, and try to reach conclusions across party lines. Their recommendations are not legally binding — the government can reject them — but a detailed, cross-party committee report is politically hard to ignore and often improves legislation. Their limits are real too: referral of bills to committees is not mandatory, and analysts noted that only about 16% of bills in the 17th Lok Sabha were referred to committees, compared with about 71% in the 15th Lok Sabha, a reported comparison that illustrates how much this practice has varied. Strengthening committees — by referring more bills, publishing more evidence and following up recommendations — is one of the most widely suggested, and least partisan, parliamentary reforms.

## Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Committees let a smaller, cross-party group examine bills, budgets and audits in detail that the full House cannot.
  • The Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings anchor financial scrutiny.
  • Committee recommendations are advisory, but detailed cross-party reports carry political weight and often improve laws.

Why it matters for civil studies: If you want to judge how seriously a legislature scrutinises government, look at its committees — not just its debates.

Discussion question: Should referring major bills to a committee be made mandatory, or should the government keep discretion over when detailed scrutiny happens?
