How Indian parties are organised: national, state, and regional

Note 041 · Theme 5: Parties & elections · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

India has a multi-party system: hundreds of parties are registered, but only a smaller number are recognised by the Election Commission of India (ECI) as national or state parties. Recognition matters because it brings a reserved election symbol, which helps voters identify a party on the ballot, especially where literacy or language differences make names alone less useful.

A party must first register with the ECI under Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Recognition is separate, governed mainly by the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968. In broad terms, a national party must show strength across several states — for example, a set vote share in four or more states plus a minimum number of Lok Sabha seats, or state-party recognition in at least four states. A state party must show strength in a particular state, through its votes and seats in that state's assembly or Lok Sabha elections. The ECI reviews recognition over time, so status can be gained or lost.

As reported in 2025, there were six recognised national parties: the Aam Aadmi Party, Bahujan Samaj Party, Bharatiya Janata Party, Communist Party of India (Marxist), Indian National Congress, and National People's Party. Alongside them were dozens of recognised state parties and thousands of registered but unrecognised parties. The exact state-party count changes with ECI updates, so readers should check the current ECI list rather than treating any single count as permanent.

"State" and "regional" are often used interchangeably, but they differ. "State party" is a formal ECI category. "Regional party" is a looser description of a party whose base, language, or main issues are concentrated in one region. National parties also have state units, and their fortunes can differ sharply from state to state. Parties also vary in how centralised or democratic their internal life is — a point worth remembering before assuming every party works the same way inside.

Key takeaways

  • National-party status depends on performance across several states, not on size alone.
  • State-party status is formal ECI recognition in a state; "regional" is a broader, informal label.
  • Recognition affects symbols and visibility, and it can be gained or lost after elections.

Why it matters for civil studies: party organisation shapes who gets selected, which issues reach the agenda, and how federal India is governed.

Discussion question: Should recognition depend mainly on votes, on seats, or on a mix of both — and what would each rule do to small or new parties?
