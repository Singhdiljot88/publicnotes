Fundamental Rights vs Directive Principles: why both matter

Note 003 · Theme 1: Constitution & institutions · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12 to 35) sets out Fundamental Rights. Part IV (Articles 36 to 51) sets out Directive Principles of State Policy. Together, scholars have described them as the conscience of the Constitution, but they work in opposite directions, and that difference is the point.

Fundamental Rights are mainly enforceable limits on the state. They protect equality, freedom, protection against exploitation, religious freedom, and cultural and educational rights, and Article 32 provides the right to move the Supreme Court for their enforcement. They are not absolute: the Constitution allows reasonable restrictions, and courts examine whether a law that limits a right is justified.

Directive Principles are goals for governance, not rights a person can enforce in court. Article 37 says they are not enforceable by any court, but are fundamental in the governance of the country. They urge the state to secure a just social order, fair distribution of resources, a living wage, free legal aid, protection of the environment, separation of the judiciary from the executive, and other welfare aims. Governments are expected to keep them in mind when making laws, and courts may use them when interpreting laws and rights.

The relationship between the two has changed over time. Early case law tended to give Fundamental Rights priority in a conflict. Later judgments, notably in Minerva Mills (1980), treated harmony and balance between the two as part of the basic structure. The tension is deliberate: political democracy without social and economic justice, the framers feared, would be incomplete.

Key takeaways

  • Fundamental Rights are justiciable; Directive Principles are not, but guide law-making.
  • Rights restrain the state; Directive Principles direct it toward welfare and social justice.
  • Courts today seek harmony and balance between the two, rather than treating one as irrelevant.

Why it matters for civil studies: Many policy debates — welfare, land, education, environment — are really arguments about how to balance an enforceable right with a constitutional goal.

Discussion question: Should a welfare goal ever justify limiting a Fundamental Right, and who should decide where that line falls?
