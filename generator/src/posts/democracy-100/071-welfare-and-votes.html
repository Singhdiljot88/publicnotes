Welfare and votes: freebies, rights, or investment?

Note 071 · Theme 8: Economy & welfare · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

Every election brings promises of cheaper electricity, free bus travel, cash support, or subsidised food. Supporters call these measures a way to reduce hardship and widen opportunity. Critics call them "freebies" and warn that they strain public finances and distort voter choice. The argument is about more than money: it asks what democracy owes citizens, and how voters should judge a promise.

The Constitution points in two directions. The Directive Principles in Part IV guide the state toward social and economic welfare, although they are not enforceable like Fundamental Rights. At the same time, public money must be budgeted and audited, and governments face competing demands for schools, hospitals, roads, and debt repayment. A scheme helping one group today may therefore reduce funds for another need tomorrow, which is why cost, targeting, and duration matter.

The Supreme Court has wrestled with the line. In S. Subramaniam Balaji (2013), it held that manifesto promises are not, by themselves, corrupt practices under the Representation of the People Act, 1951. In August 2022, the bench referred the petitions to a three-judge bench for an extensive hearing, noting that free education or minimum essential power raises different questions from free consumer electronics. The legal debate remains open, as the petitions were still to be heard in full by the larger bench.

Voters can compare promises with four questions. Who benefits — people facing clear hardship, or everyone? Is it a one-time transfer, a continuing entitlement, or an investment in schooling or health? What will it cost over several years, and what spending might be crowded out? And how will delivery and outcomes be checked? Critics warn of pre-election bidding wars and unsustainable state bills. Supporters reply that, in an unequal society, subsidised basics can be a precondition for dignity, learning, and work, and that "freebie" is sometimes used selectively against help for poorer voters.

Key takeaways:

Key takeaways

  • Welfare, rights, and investment are different justifications, and one promise can combine them.
  • Manifesto promises are not automatically corrupt practices, but the wider freebies question is unresolved.
  • Cost, targeting, duration, and accountability are practical tests voters can apply.

Why it matters for civil studies: Welfare debates link constitutional values, elections, public finance, and state capacity in one question.

Discussion question: Should election promises using public money be required to publish a cost estimate before polling, and who should check it?
