Jobs: the employment debate young voters face

Note 075 · Theme 8: Economy & welfare · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

For young voters, the jobs debate is rarely about a single unemployment number. It is about whether work is available, secure, fairly paid, and matched to education and aspiration. A graduate who cannot find suitable work, a gig worker without paid leave, and a farm worker moving between seasonal jobs may all be counted differently, yet all face insecurity. Understanding the measure is therefore the first step to understanding the politics.

The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Annual Report 2025, as summarised in official and press reporting, put unemployment for people aged 15 and above at 3.1% in 2025. Youth unemployment for ages 15–29 was 9.9%, down from 10.3% in 2024, while urban youth unemployment was higher, at 13.6%. The same survey estimated that 25% of people aged 15–29 were not in employment, education, or training, and that unemployment among people with secondary education or above was 6.5%. These figures show improvement on the headline youth rate, but also a persistent gap between young people and the overall workforce, and between urban and rural youth.

The India Employment Report 2024, by the Institute for Human Development with the International Labour Organization, highlighted a related paradox: educated young people often face higher unemployment, suggesting a mismatch between qualifications, aspirations, and available jobs. Some analysts argue India needs more formal, productive jobs in manufacturing and services, better vocational training, and stronger links between colleges and employers. Others argue that informal and self-employment — agriculture, small trade, and platform work — should be counted and improved rather than dismissed, because they already absorb large numbers of workers.

Policy responses are debated on similar lines. Apprenticeships, skill programmes, public employment, support for small enterprises, and labour protections for gig workers are each presented as part of the answer, but their scale, funding, and job quality are contested. A programme creating short-term placements is not the same as one building lasting employability, and a rise in employment can coincide with low pay or irregular hours. Young readers should therefore ask not only how many jobs are reported, but what kind of jobs, for whom, in which places, and with what security.

Key takeaways

  • PLFS 2025 shows lower overall unemployment than youth unemployment, with urban youth facing the highest rate.
  • One in four people aged 15–29 were estimated to be outside employment, education, and training.
  • Educated youth unemployment points to a mismatch between qualifications and available work.

Why it matters for civil studies: Employment shapes migration, family decisions, protest, and voting, and exposes the limits of any single survey measure.

Discussion question: Should policy prioritise more jobs of any kind, better-paid formal jobs, or skills matching future work — and what trade-offs does each involve?
