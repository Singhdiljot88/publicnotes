Education and democracy: what schools and colleges teach about citizenship

Note 093 · Theme 10: Youth & future · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not published as legal advice

For most young Indians, the first theory of democracy comes from a classroom. NCERT's Social and Political Life textbooks, used widely in CBSE-affiliated schools and many state-board schools, introduce the Constitution, rights, government, and social justice in stages, from local government to Parliament. The National Curriculum Framework of 2005 set an early ambition for this teaching: education should help students think and act independently, and democracy should be understood not only as institutions but as something that depends on ordinary people. That ambition remains a useful yardstick, whatever the textbook edition in hand.

In practice, teaching varies a great deal. Research on teachers in CBSE schools in Punjab found lessons were often teacher-led and closely tied to the prescribed NCERT text, while teachers also drew on their own experience to teach ideas about good citizenship, nationalism, secularism, democracy, and national identity. Teachers in that study reported trying to stay objective on sensitive topics such as reservation, religion and caste, gender violence, and human rights. Other educators argue that memorising rights and duties is not the same as practising democracy, and that schools should also offer student councils, debates, mock parliaments, and real decisions about school life. Supporters of a textbook-centred approach reply that a shared factual base matters first, especially in a large and diverse country.

Colleges add a second layer. Student elections, clubs, hostels, and campus newspapers can teach negotiation, representation, and accountability, or they can teach the opposite: that power belongs to the loudest group. The same tension appears in how history and current affairs are taught. One view stresses national unity, constitutional values, and shared heritage. Another stresses critical inquiry, dissent, and minority rights. A balanced civic education does not have to choose between pride and criticism. It can admire the freedom movement and the Constitution while asking how far equality and justice have reached in practice. The practical test is simple: after a lesson, can a student explain a right, check a government claim, and say where to take a grievance?

Key takeaways

  • NCERT's Social and Political Life series is a central, though not universal, route into citizenship education.
  • Research suggests much teaching remains teacher-led and textbook-based, even when teachers aim for objectivity.
  • Student councils, debates, and campus life can practise democracy, but can also reproduce exclusion if only some voices are heard.

Why it matters for civil studies: How citizenship is taught shapes whether young citizens see democracy as a subject to pass or a skill to use.

Discussion question: Should a school be judged on debating a controversial policy well, or on reciting constitutional facts well — and can it do both?
