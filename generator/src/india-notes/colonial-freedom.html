Colonial Rule and the Freedom Struggle — 1757 to 1947

From Company rule to independence, through revolt, reform and mass movements.

← Medieval India · Post-Independence → · India Notes hub

The timeline

Date / periodEvent / person / milestone — why it matters
1757Battle of Plassey — East India Company defeats Siraj-ud-daulah in Bengal.
A trading company begins to become a territorial power; revenue, not just trade, becomes its prize.
1764Battle of Buxar — Company defeats the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-daulah and Shah Alam II.
Leads to the Diwani of Bengal (1765): the Company collects revenue in the Mughal emperor's name.
1857–1858Revolt of 1857 — Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi; Rani Lakshmibai, Bahadur Shah Zafar, Tantia Tope.
Called the First War of Independence by some and a sepoy mutiny by others — the naming itself is part of the debate.
1858Crown takes direct control — Government of India Act 1858; East India Company rule ends.
India is now ruled directly by the British Crown, with a Viceroy in Calcutta (later Delhi).
1885Indian National Congress founded in Bombay.
A forum that grows from moderate petitions into the main vehicle of the national movement.
1905–1911Partition of Bengal (1905) and Swadeshi movement; partition annulled 1911, capital moves to Delhi.
Swadeshi boycott and national education become mass tactics; the annulment shows protest could force a reversal.
1915Gandhi returns to India from South Africa.
Satyagraha — non-violent resistance — becomes the movement's central method.
1919Jallianwala Bagh massacre, Amritsar (13 April 1919); Rowlatt Act protests.
Turns many moderates towards non-cooperation; a turning point in British legitimacy.
1920–1922Non-Cooperation Movement; Chauri Chaura (1922) leads Gandhi to call it off.
Mass mobilisation — and its limits when protest turns violent.
1930Dandi March (12 March–6 April 1930) and Civil Disobedience — Gandhi breaks the salt law at Dandi.
A simple, symbolic act makes a colonial monopoly a national issue.
1930–1932First Round Table Conference (1930); Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931); Poona Pact (1932, Ambedkar–Gandhi).
Negotiation, representation of depressed classes, and separate electorates are fought over, not settled by marches alone.
1942Quit India Movement — 'Do or Die'; leaders arrested, widespread protest.
The demand is now immediate independence, not gradual reform.
1945–1947INA trials, naval mutiny (1946), Cabinet Mission, Partition plans.
War, demobilisation and communal violence compress the endgame into two years.
15 August 1947Independence — India and Pakistan created; Partition displacement and violence.
Freedom and Partition arrive together; the human cost is part of the story, not an afterthought.

Key takeaways

  • British rule grew through revenue and law, not by one battle.
  • The freedom movement used many methods — petitions, boycott, satyagraha, negotiation, and armed resistance.
  • Partition is inseparable from independence.

Why it matters: It explains why the Constitution, the army, the railways and the borders look the way they do.

Discussion / revision question: Was 1857 a war of independence, a mutiny, or something in between — and who decides that label?
