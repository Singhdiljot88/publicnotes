Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 📰 Theme 6: Media & information

Digital regulation: IT Rules, takedowns, and free speech

Note 057 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Online speech in India sits between two constitutional ideas: freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), and the reasonable restrictions allowed under Article 19(2). The Information Technology Act, 2000, gives that balance a practical shape. Section 79 gives intermediaries — platforms that host other people's content — protection from liability for user content, provided they follow certain conditions. Section 69A allows the government to direct blocking of online information in specified circumstances, such as in the interests of sovereignty, security, or public order. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), the Supreme Court struck down Section 66A, which had criminalised sending grossly offensive online messages, on the ground that it was vague and chilled speech, while also clarifying how intermediary liability should work.

The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, added detailed duties for platforms and for digital news and streaming services. Larger platforms, described as significant social media intermediaries, face extra obligations, including appointing grievance and compliance officers in India, publishing reports, and acting on certain orders within set timelines. Supporters argue that these rules make large, powerful platforms answerable in India, help victims of harassment and unlawful content get faster action, and bring digital news under a basic ethics framework. Critics argue that short takedown timelines, traceability requirements, and the risk of losing legal protection can push platforms to remove lawful but controversial content first and ask questions later — a practice sometimes called over-removal.

The debate is not only about platforms. It is about who decides what is unlawful, how quickly, and with what appeal. A court order is public and can be challenged in a court. A government direction may be less visible to the user whose post disappears. A platform's own policy decision may be based on its terms of service rather than Indian law. Each route has different transparency and accountability, and litigation over the 2021 Rules and related blocking powers has continued, so students should treat the legal position as evolving rather than settled. The central civil studies question remains constant: how can a democracy remove genuinely harmful or unlawful content without giving any single actor unchecked power over public conversation?

Key takeaways

  • Section 79 protects intermediaries conditionally, Section 69A allows blocking in specified circumstances, and Shreya Singhal (2015) struck down the vague Section 66A.
  • The IT Rules, 2021, impose extra duties on larger platforms, including officers, reports, and action on orders within set timelines.
  • The key dispute is over-removal, transparency, and appeal: who decides, how fast, and how a user can challenge a takedown.

Why it matters for civil studies: Digital rules decide what millions of citizens can publish, read, and debate, so they are now as important to free speech as traditional press law.

Discussion question: If a post is removed on government direction, what information should the user and the public receive to make that decision accountable?
