Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 🌾 Theme 8: Economy & welfare

Health and education as democratic promises

Note 074 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Health and education are among the most personal promises a democracy makes. A hospital bill or a school admission can decide whether a family falls into debt, whether a child stays in learning, and whether a young person can compete for work. India has built both rights-based and insurance-based approaches, but access on paper and quality in practice often diverge, and that gap shapes political debate.

In health, Ayushman Bharat–Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, launched in 2018, offers cashless cover of Rs 5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary hospitalisation. Government summaries describe coverage for about 12 crore poor and vulnerable families, or roughly 55 crore people, identified mainly through Socio-Economic Caste Census 2011 data, with cost-sharing between Centre and states. In October 2024, it was expanded to senior citizens aged 70 and above regardless of income. Supporters argue it reduces catastrophic hospital costs and gives portability to migrants. Critics point to the "missing middle" — people neither poor enough for the scheme nor covered by employer insurance — plus uneven hospital empanelment and the weaker state of primary care, where everyday illness is first treated.

In education, the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, provides free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14. Enrolment has expanded greatly, but learning outcomes, teacher availability, classroom conditions, and dropout at higher levels remain central concerns. Digital learning widened reach for some students while exposing gaps in devices, connectivity, and language for others. As with health, the question is not only whether a school or clinic exists, but whether staffing, supplies, transport, and accountability keep it functioning.

State variation matters greatly. Health and education delivery is substantially a state responsibility, even when the Centre funds major schemes. Two citizens with the same formal entitlement can therefore face very different waiting times, fees, and quality, and national averages can hide both strong state models and neglected districts. A useful test is to follow one entitlement from announcement to experience: who is eligible, how is eligibility proved, what happens on refusal or delay, and where can a complaint be heard?

Key takeaways

  • PM-JAY covers hospitalisation for a large vulnerable population, but primary care and the missing middle remain gaps.
  • Free compulsory schooling to age 14 is a legal promise, while learning quality and retention are separate challenges.
  • State delivery explains much of the difference between a national entitlement and lived experience.

Why it matters for civil studies: Health and education show how rights, insurance, federalism, and frontline administration interact in everyday democracy.

Discussion question: Should the next priority be wider insurance coverage, stronger primary schools and clinics, or both — and how would you fund the choice?
