Women's movements: from safety to representation

Note 065 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Women's movements in India have worked on two connected fronts: safety from violence and discrimination in daily life, and equal representation in public decision-making. The first concerns laws, policing, workplaces, homes and public spaces. The second concerns whether women are present — and heard — in panchayats, legislatures, parties, courts and other institutions. A movement can win a legal change on one front while representation on the other remains uneven, which is why many women's organisations pursue both at once.

Protests after the December 2012 gang rape and murder in Delhi, widely known as the Nirbhaya case, became a turning point in public debate. Large demonstrations in Delhi and other cities demanded stronger laws, faster trials and safer public transport. Supporters of the protests argued that the case exposed routine harassment and institutional failure. Officials and commentators also debated whether harsher punishment alone would prevent violence, or whether policing, prosecution, education and social attitudes mattered more. Legal and institutional changes followed, but women's groups continue to argue that implementation — reporting, investigation, support for survivors and conviction in practice — matters as much as the text of a law.

Representation has a longer institutional history. Reservations for women in panchayats and municipalities, introduced through the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments in the early 1990s, brought large numbers of women into local elected office, although scholars and activists debate how much independent decision-making power elected women representatives exercise in practice, given family, party and social pressures. At the national and state level, the Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023, known as the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, including one-third of seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The Act was brought into force on 16 April 2026, but the reservation itself will take effect only after a census and delimitation exercise. Supporters welcome the constitutional commitment; critics argue that the delay, the absence of a separate sub-quota for women from Other Backward Classes, and party nomination practices limit its immediate effect.

Women's movements are not a single movement. They include organisations focused on labour, health, caste, religion, disability, rural livelihoods and LGBTQ+ rights, and they sometimes disagree about priorities and strategies. That diversity is a strength for democratic debate, even when it makes a single "women's demand" hard to define.

Key takeaways

  • Women's movements link safety, equality at work and home, and representation in elected bodies.
  • The 2012 Delhi protests sharpened debate on sexual violence, but implementation remains contested.
  • The 2023 women's reservation law reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats, operative only after census and delimitation.

Why it matters for civil studies: Representation without safety, and safety without representation, are both incomplete — women's movements show why democracies must pursue both.

Discussion question: Does reserving seats guarantee that women's concerns will shape policy, or does it depend on parties, resources and local power as well?
