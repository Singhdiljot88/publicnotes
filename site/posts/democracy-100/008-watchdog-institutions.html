Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 📜 Theme 1: Constitution & institutions

Comptroller and Auditor General, UPSC and other watchdog institutions

Note 008 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Democracy needs referees that are not players in the daily contest for power. The Constitution creates several such bodies, with different jobs, but a shared purpose: to check that public power and public money are used lawfully and fairly.

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG), under Article 148, audits the accounts of the Union and state governments and related bodies. The CAG's reports go to the President or Governor, who place them before Parliament or the state legislature, where committees such as the Public Accounts Committee examine them. The CAG does not punish officials or cancel spending; its power is to reveal, document, and trigger legislative and public scrutiny.

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC), under Article 315, conducts examinations for appointments to Union services and advises the government on personnel matters, including recruitment rules and disciplinary cases. State Public Service Commissions perform a similar role for state services. Their independence is meant to protect merit-based recruitment from patronage. The Finance Commission, under Article 280, recommends how tax revenues should be shared between the Union and states, while National Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Backward Classes monitor safeguards for those groups.

These bodies are often called watchdogs, but they are not identical. Some audit, some recruit, some advise, and some recommend. When reports are ignored, or appointments are questioned, critics allege weakening of oversight; governments may respond that oversight must respect elected policy choices and due process.

Key takeaways

  • The CAG audits public accounts and reports to legislatures; it does not itself prosecute or punish.
  • The UPSC protects merit in Union recruitment and advises on service matters.
  • Watchdog strength depends on independence, tenure, resources, and follow-up by Parliament and legislatures.

Why it matters for civil studies: These institutions are where constitutional accountability becomes concrete — in an audit paragraph, an exam, or a revenue recommendation.

Discussion question: If a watchdog report is published but never debated, has accountability happened — and who is responsible for the gap?
