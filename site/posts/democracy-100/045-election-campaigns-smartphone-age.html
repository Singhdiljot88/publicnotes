Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 📣 Theme 5: Parties & elections

Election campaigns in the smartphone age

Note 045 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Indian campaigns once ran mainly through rallies, posters, newspapers, and television. Those tools have not disappeared — a large rally still signals strength, and door-to-door visits still matter, especially where personal contact and local reputation count. But a second campaign now runs alongside them, on phones: short videos, WhatsApp groups, YouTube speeches, Instagram reels, and targeted advertisements in regional languages.

The shift has been gradual. The 2014 general election is often called India's first "social media election", and 2019 is often described as a "WhatsApp election" because of how widely that platform was used for voter outreach. By 2024, researchers described a more multi-platform campaign, with parties using data, influencers, regional-language video, and rapid rebuttals to shape opinion in real time. Precise figures for spending or reach vary by source and should be treated with care, but the direction is clear: parties now campaign where voters already spend time.

Smartphone campaigning changes who can speak and who can be reached. Smaller parties and independent voices can publish video without buying television time, and voters in rural areas can watch a leader's speech without travelling to a rally. At the same time, new problems have grown. Closed groups make false claims harder to check, deepfakes and edited clips can spread before a correction catches up, and micro-targeting can show different voters different promises without a shared public record. Pew reporting in May 2025 found 65% of Indian respondents viewed misinformation as a major issue, which shows why verification is now part of campaigning itself. (Note: other Pew summaries combine 'big or moderately big' concern at a higher share; the 65% figure is for 'major issue' wording.)

The Election Commission has tried to keep rules in step, including social-media monitoring and requirements around political advertising, but regulation of encrypted messaging and fast-moving video remains contested. Parties, platforms, fact-checkers, and voters disagree about where responsibility lies, and about how much intervention risks restricting legitimate speech.

For a young reader, the practical skill is not to avoid digital campaigning, but to read it critically: check the source, look for the full clip rather than a cut, compare the same claim across outlets, and ask who paid for an advertisement and who benefits if it is believed.

Key takeaways

  • Campaigns now combine rallies and door-to-door work with video, messaging apps, and targeted ads.
  • Phones lower the cost of reaching voters, but also speed up misinformation.
  • Rules for digital campaigning are still catching up with the technology.

Why it matters for civil studies: campaign technology affects agenda-setting, accountability, and whether voters share a common set of facts before they vote.

Discussion question: Should a political advertisement on a messaging app carry the same disclosure rules as one on television — and how could that be enforced without reading private messages?
