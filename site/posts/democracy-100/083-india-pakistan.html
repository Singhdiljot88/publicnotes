India-Pakistan: why dialogue stops and starts

Note 083 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

India-Pakistan relations follow a repeated pattern: talks begin, a crisis interrupts them, a limited understanding restores calm, and then the cycle repeats. Understanding that pattern matters more than memorising any single summit, because the same structural obstacles return each time.

The main issues are Kashmir, cross-border terrorism as alleged by India, water, trade and the legacy of Partition. Pakistan disputes India's position on Jammu and Kashmir; India maintains that Kashmir is an integral part of India and that terrorism must stop before normal relations can proceed. Pakistan, in turn, has argued that dialogue must include Kashmir and has linked talks to India's August 2019 changes to Jammu and Kashmir's constitutional status. These positions are presented here as the two sides' stated positions, not as settled fact.

Dialogue has worked in limited ways. The Composite Dialogue from 2004 to 2008 addressed a basket of issues, including Kashmir, terrorism, trade and people-to-people contact, but it was paused after the November 2008 Mumbai terrorist attacks. A ceasefire along the Line of Control agreed in 2003 reduced firing for a time, though violations rose again from 2007 and sharply after 2013. In February 2021, the two Directors General of Military Operations reaffirmed strict observance of the ceasefire from the night of 24–25 February 2021, and firing fell. That military understanding, however, did not by itself restart a political dialogue.

The most recent sharp crisis came in 2025. After a terrorist attack at Pahalgam in Jammu and Kashmir on 22 April 2025, in which 26 people were killed, India launched Operation Sindoor on 7 May 2025, striking what it described as terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan and Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Four days of strikes, drone activity and shelling followed before both sides agreed on 10 May 2025 to stop military action from 1700 hours Indian Standard Time. India has consistently said the halt was reached through direct military communication between the two sides; the then United States President claimed a mediating role, a claim India rejected. Many battlefield claims from that period remain disputed and cannot be independently verified from public sources alone.

For a civil studies reader, the useful distinction is between ceasefire management, which armies can agree, and political settlement, which needs governments willing to spend political capital at home.

Key takeaways

  • Dialogue has repeatedly been interrupted by terrorist attacks and by political changes on either side.
  • The 2003 and 2021 ceasefire understandings show that military channels can reduce violence without solving the dispute.
  • The 2025 crisis shows how quickly a local attack can escalate between two nuclear-armed neighbours.

Why it matters for civil studies: India-Pakistan questions reward answers that separate crisis management from conflict resolution, and that attribute contested claims to their sources.

Discussion question: Should India treat a ceasefire as a first step towards talks, or as a sufficient goal in itself when trust is low?
