The Budget Session and Why the Budget Is a Political Document

Note 023 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

The Union Budget is formally called the Annual Financial Statement, laid before both Houses under Article 112 of the Constitution. It sets out the government's estimated receipts and expenditure for the coming financial year, which in India begins on 1 April. The Budget Session usually begins in late January and runs in two phases until around April, with a recess in between so that departmental committees can examine ministries' spending plans. The Finance Minister presents the Budget in the Lok Sabha, normally on 1 February, and the Economic Survey is tabled shortly before as background analysis.

Passing the Budget is a sequence, not a single vote. After a general discussion in both Houses, the Lok Sabha discusses and votes on Demands for Grants — the money each ministry seeks. The Rajya Sabha can discuss the Budget but cannot vote on these demands. Once the demands are approved, an Appropriation Bill authorises money to be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of India. A Finance Bill then gives legal effect to tax proposals. If the full Budget cannot be passed before the financial year starts, Parliament can approve a Vote on Account to keep government running in the meantime. No money can be spent, and no tax levied, without this parliamentary authorisation.

That is why the Budget is political, not merely technical. Its choices — how much to spend on health, education, defence, infrastructure or welfare, where revenue comes from, and which regions or groups benefit — reveal a government's priorities and its theory of fairness and growth. Opposition parties use the Budget debate to challenge those priorities and to press for different ones, while state governments watch closely because central transfers and shared taxes shape what they can do. A Budget speech can therefore be read on two levels: as an accounting exercise, and as a statement of what the government of the day believes the state is for.

Key takeaways

  • The Budget is the Annual Financial Statement under Article 112, presented in the Lok Sabha, usually on 1 February.
  • The Lok Sabha votes on Demands for Grants; the Appropriation Bill authorises spending and the Finance Bill enacts tax changes.
  • Spending and tax choices make the Budget a statement of political priorities, not just a set of numbers.

Why it matters for civil studies: The Budget links constitutional finance rules to real policy — follow the money and you can follow what a government actually values.

Discussion question: Should Budget debates focus more on past outcomes — what earlier spending achieved — than on new announcements?
