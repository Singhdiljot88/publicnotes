Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 🌾 Theme 8: Economy & welfare

Climate, economy, and just transition

Note 080 of 100 · 3 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Climate change is an economic issue as much as an environmental one. Heat, erratic monsoons, floods, and droughts affect crops, health, working hours, and infrastructure, while the shift away from fossil fuels affects jobs, energy prices, and public revenue. India faces both sides at once: it must protect people from climate harm already under way, and change how energy is produced and used, without slowing the growth that supports livelihoods.

India's international position rests on equity and development. Under the Paris Agreement, countries submit Nationally Determined Contributions. India's updated contribution in 2022 included reducing the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005 levels, alongside expanded non-fossil capacity goals. At COP26 in Glasgow in 2021, India announced a net-zero target for 2070. Supporters of this timeline argue that it balances responsibility for past emissions, largely from richer countries, with India's need to expand energy access. Critics, including some climate advocates, argue for faster action, while others warn that too rapid a shift could raise costs and disrupt employment.

A "just transition" asks who bears the cost of change. NITI Aayog's February 2026 reports on pathways to Viksit Bharat by 2047 and net zero by 2070, as summarised in reporting, project GDP rising from about USD 4.2 trillion in 2025 to nearly USD 30 trillion by 2047, with clean energy and related sectors generating about 7 million additional jobs by 2050. The same summaries warn that more than 150 districts depend heavily on coal and fossil-fuel activities, and that fossil-fuel-linked industries employ an estimated 17 million workers. These are modelling projections, not guaranteed outcomes, and they underline why reskilling, diversification, pensions, health support, and local investment matter alongside new solar or wind capacity.

The politics is therefore regional as well as national. Coal states such as Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and Odisha face different risks from states gaining renewable investment. Farmers face changing rainfall and groundwater stress, coastal communities face sea-level and cyclone risks, and cities face heat and flooding. Adaptation — resilient crops, water management, early warning, and cool housing — can protect incomes even before emissions fall. Finance is the binding constraint in many proposals: India argues for international climate finance and technology transfer, while domestic debates concern subsidies, electricity pricing, and who pays for grid upgrades, storage, and stranded assets.

Key takeaways

  • India's stated goals are a 45% emissions-intensity cut by 2030 from 2005 levels and net zero by 2070.
  • NITI Aayog modelling projects large green job gains, but also deep adjustment risks in coal-dependent districts.
  • Just transition means pairing clean energy with reskilling, diversification, and social protection, not only closing plants.

Why it matters for civil studies: Climate policy joins global equity, federal distribution, labour rights, and long-term public investment in one debate.

Discussion question: Should coal regions receive transition support before, during, or after new clean-energy jobs arrive — and who should decide the timetable?
