Agriculture policy: MSP, markets, and farmer income

Note 079 of 100 · 3 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Agriculture still supports a large share of India's workforce, even as its share of national output has fallen. That gap helps explain why farm prices, credit, irrigation, and markets are politically explosive: a small change in the price of paddy, wheat, cotton, or onions can affect household income, food prices for urban consumers, and government procurement bills at the same time.

The Minimum Support Price (MSP) is a price floor announced by the government on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, covering a set of major crops before sowing seasons. It is meant to insure farmers against sharp price falls and guide sowing decisions. In practice, procurement at MSP is concentrated in wheat and rice and in certain states, so many farmers sell in open markets below MSP, particularly for other crops or where procurement infrastructure is weak. The formula for MSP is itself contested. The National Commission on Farmers, chaired by M. S. Swaminathan and reporting in 2006, recommended that MSP be at least 50% more than the weighted average cost of production, often described as C2 plus 50%. The government announced in the 2018–19 Budget that MSP would be kept at 1.5 times the cost of production, but farmer organisations and analysts dispute which cost concept is used, arguing that a narrower cost base produces a lower price than the Swaminathan formula would. Others argue that a legal MSP guarantee for all crops would be fiscally heavy, distort cropping toward water-intensive cereals, and weaken market signals. Supporters of a legal guarantee reply that without enforceable purchase, an announced price is only a promise.

Markets add a second layer. Agricultural Produce Market Committee mandis, private trade, contract farming, and the electronic National Agriculture Market (eNAM) offer different routes to buyers, with different fees, transport costs, and bargaining power. Smallholders often lack storage, transport, and price information, so even a good harvest can mean distress sales. The farm laws passed in 2020, which sought to expand trade outside mandis and enable contract farming, were repealed in 2021 after prolonged farmer protests, showing how deeply market reform touches federal power, state revenues, and trust. Income support, crop insurance, irrigation, and diversification are therefore debated alongside price policy, because price alone cannot stabilise income exposed to weather, pests, and debt.

Key takeaways

  • MSP is a government-announced floor based on CACP advice, but procurement and benefit are uneven across crops and regions.
  • The C2 plus 50% Swaminathan recommendation and the government's 1.5-times cost announcement use contested cost concepts.
  • Markets, storage, credit, and insurance shape farmer income as much as the announced price does.

Why it matters for civil studies: Farm policy tests federalism, price stability, food security, and the power of organised protest in a democracy.

Discussion question: Would a legal MSP guarantee, better markets, or direct income support do most for small farmers — and what would each cost consumers and the exchequer?
