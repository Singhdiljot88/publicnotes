Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 🌏 Theme 9: Foreign policy & democracy

Defence, security, and democratic control of the armed forces

Note 090 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

India has never had a military coup, unlike Pakistan, where the army has taken direct power several times. That is not an accident of culture alone. It is the result of constitutional design: at every link in the chain of command, the armed forces answer to elected civilians.

The Constitution vests supreme command of the Defence Forces in the President under Article 53(2), but adds that its exercise shall be regulated by law. Under Article 74, the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, who is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. In practice, therefore, supreme command is exercised through elected ministers, not by the military itself. The Cabinet Committee on Security, chaired by the Prime Minister and including the ministers for defence, home affairs, external affairs and finance, is the highest decision-making body on national security and defence spending.

Below that level, the Ministry of Defence, headed by a civilian minister (the Raksha Mantri) and staffed largely by civilian officials, exercises administrative control over the Army, Navy and Air Force, including budgets, procurement and personnel policy. Early decisions reinforced this pattern: the Commander-in-Chief was kept out of the Cabinet in the interim government, and in 1955 the Commander-in-Chief designation was replaced by three Service Chiefs, each heading one service. The services were treated as attached offices of the Ministry of Defence, so that military policy remained with the civilian minister.

Reforms since have tried to improve joint working without weakening civilian control. After the Kargil War of 1999, a Group of Ministers recommended a Chief of Defence Staff (CDS). The post was approved by the Cabinet on 24 December 2019, with the CDS heading the new Department of Military Affairs within the Ministry of Defence and serving as Principal Military Adviser to the Defence Minister and Permanent Chairman of the Chiefs of Staff Committee. Crucially, the CDS does not exercise military command over the three Service Chiefs. Critics of the civilian-heavy structure argue it can slow procurement and joint planning; defenders argue that friction is the price of democratic control, and that Parliament, the Comptroller and Auditor General, and the courts provide further oversight of defence spending and conduct.

Key takeaways

  • Supreme command rests with the President, exercised on ministerial advice — so real control sits with elected civilians.
  • The civilian Ministry of Defence, not the services, controls budgets and policy.
  • The CDS, created in 2019, advises and coordinates; it does not command the Service Chiefs.

Why it matters for civil studies: Civil-military questions test whether you can trace the constitutional chain from Article 53 through the Cabinet Committee on Security to the Service Chiefs, rather than simply naming ranks.

Discussion question: Does India's strong civilian control make defence decision-making slower but safer — and where, if anywhere, should military advice be given more weight?
