Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 📰 Theme 6: Media & information

Government advertising and media independence

Note 056 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Governments are among the largest advertisers in India. Central and state governments publicise health campaigns, welfare schemes, examination dates, disaster warnings, and job notices, and they pay newspapers, television channels, radio stations, and digital platforms to carry those messages. That spending has a legitimate public purpose: citizens need to know what services exist and how to use them. The question for media independence is whether the way advertising is allocated also creates pressure on what news outlets report.

Central government advertising has historically been routed through the Directorate of Advertising and Visual Publicity, known as DAVP, which was merged into the Central Bureau of Communication (CBC) on 8 December 2017. Outlets are generally empanelled, or listed as eligible, and rates are set under government policy rather than by open market negotiation alone. Reported government policy states that advertisements should not be allocated on the basis of a newspaper or channel's political affiliation or editorial policy. Supporters of the system argue that published eligibility criteria, circulation figures, and rate cards make allocation a routine administrative task, not a reward for praise or a punishment for criticism.

Critics raise a different concern: even a formally neutral system can create dependence. If government advertising is a large share of a small or regional outlet's revenue, losing it can threaten jobs and survival. That dependence, critics argue, may encourage softer coverage of the government of the day, even without an explicit threat. They also point to the difficulty of checking allocation in practice: published totals do not always show, outlet by outlet, why one publication received more than another, or whether public-information messages have blurred into promotion of a government's record. Defenders reply that governments of all parties have used advertising, that private advertising also shapes media incentives, and that the remedy is transparency — clear criteria, published data, and independent audit — rather than ending public advertising altogether.

For students, the useful test is not whether a government advertises, but how the system is designed. Are criteria public? Is allocation data available? Is there an independent check? And can an outlet that criticises a government still survive without that revenue? The answers, outlet by outlet, tell more about independence than any single advertising total.

Key takeaways

  • Government advertising serves a real public-information purpose, but it is also a large revenue source for many outlets.
  • Reported policy says allocation should not depend on editorial policy, yet critics argue dependence can still encourage softer coverage.
  • Transparency — public criteria, outlet-level data, and independent audit — is the main proposed check on misuse.

Why it matters for civil studies: When the state is both a subject of news and a major funder of news, citizens need to know whether coverage is shaped by evidence or by the need to keep advertising.

Discussion question: What would a fair government advertising system look like — and who should be trusted to audit it?
