Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 📣 Theme 5: Parties & elections

Caste, religion, and class in voting behaviour

Note 049 of 100 · 3 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

No honest introduction to Indian voting can avoid caste, religion, and class. No honest introduction can reduce voting to them, either. These identities shape networks, grievances, opportunities, and trust — and yet voters from the same background often vote differently, and the same voter may weigh identity differently in a local, state, and national election.

Caste has been central to party formation and mobilisation since independence. Parties have appealed to particular caste groups, formed social coalitions across groups, and promised representation, reservations, and development. The Mandal Commission debate of the late 1980s and early 1990s, which expanded reservations for Other Backward Classes in central government jobs, is a landmark example of how caste became a national political question. Election studies, including CSDS–Lokniti's analysis of Uttar Pradesh in 2024, illustrate variation without proving a simple rule: in that state, upper-caste voters leaned heavily towards the NDA (79% in the CSDS–Lokniti post-poll analysis), while Yadav voters (82%) and Muslim voters (92%) leaned heavily towards the INDIA coalition. OBC and Scheduled Caste voters did not lean uniformly one way — for example, non-Yadav OBC voters leaned towards the NDA (59% to 34% for INDIA), and Dalit voters split, including between Jatav and non-Jatav voters.

Religion also matters, but in contested ways. Parties and commentators disagree about whether religious identity drives voting, whether it is activated mainly during campaigns, and how it interacts with development and welfare. In 2024, CSDS–Lokniti's post-poll analysis noted that some voters praised the construction of the Ram temple in Ayodhya as a government achievement, while others cited rising communalism as a dislike. That split is a reminder to avoid treating any religious community as a single voting bloc. Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs, Christians, and other communities are internally diverse by region, language, class, and preference.

Class cuts across both. A wealthy urban voter and a poor rural voter may share a caste or religion but face different concerns about jobs, prices, housing, and services. Researchers therefore distinguish between identity as a social location, identity as a network that delivers information and help, and identity as a campaign appeal. A party may win support because it is seen as "our party", because its local workers helped a family, or because its policies seem more credible — and voters may give more than one reason.

For civil studies, the key discipline is to ask for evidence at the right level: a constituency, a social group, or a survey subgroup, rather than a national stereotype.

Key takeaways

  • Caste, religion, and class shape networks and grievances, but they do not determine every vote.
  • Broad labels hide internal differences, as caste and community subgroups show.
  • Identity can work as belonging, as practical help, or as a campaign appeal — sometimes all three.

Why it matters for civil studies: without disaggregated evidence, debates about identity politics become slogans rather than explanations of how representation actually works.

Discussion question: When a party appeals to a social group, how can a voter tell whether it is offering representation, delivering services, or simply using identity as an election slogan?
