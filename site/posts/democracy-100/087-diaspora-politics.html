Indian Democracy in 100 Notes · 🌏 Theme 9: Foreign policy & democracy

Diaspora politics: Indians abroad and Indian democracy

Note 087 of 100 · 3 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

More than 35 million people of Indian origin live outside India, according to India's Ministry of External Affairs figures reported in May 2024: about 15.85 million Non-Resident Indians (Indian citizens living abroad) and about 19.57 million Persons of Indian Origin and Overseas Citizens of India. They are not a single community. Gulf workers, students, professionals in North America and Europe, and long-settled communities in Fiji, Malaysia, Mauritius and the Caribbean have different histories, legal statuses and political concerns.

Legally, the distinction matters. India does not allow dual citizenship: under Article 9 of the Constitution, a person who voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship generally ceases to be an Indian citizen. The Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI) card, created in 2005 and merged with the earlier Person of Indian Origin card in 2015, gives lifelong visa-free travel and near-equal economic rights, such as property and work rights, but not political rights. OCI holders cannot vote in Indian elections, stand for Parliament, or hold constitutional posts. Non-Resident Indians who remain Indian citizens can vote, but in practice they must generally be physically present in India to do so, because India has not introduced general postal or online voting for them. That limitation is a recurring subject of debate.

Politically, the diaspora matters in two directions. Abroad, people of Indian origin vote, campaign, fund and lobby in their host countries, and their views can affect how India is seen there. At home, remittances, investment, cultural influence and diaspora advocacy can affect Indian politics, especially during elections and crises such as evacuations. Governments engage the diaspora through Pravasi Bharatiya Divas, observed on 9 January to mark Mahatma Gandhi's return from South Africa in 1915, and through awards and outreach by the Ministry of External Affairs.

This creates tensions that students should notice. A diaspora community may support a party or cause in India that it does not have to live under, while workers in the Gulf may face labour conditions that Indian diplomacy must address without interfering in a host country's politics. Diaspora politics is therefore not only about pride or remittances; it is about who counts as a member of the democratic community, and on what terms.

Key takeaways

  • The diaspora is large and diverse: about 35 million people, split between citizens abroad and people of Indian origin.
  • OCI is not dual citizenship: it gives travel and economic rights, but no vote or constitutional office.
  • Non-Resident Indian citizens can vote in principle, but the absence of general remote voting limits participation in practice.

Why it matters for civil studies: Diaspora questions link citizenship, voting rights and foreign policy, and they test whether you can separate legal status from cultural belonging.

Discussion question: Should India introduce remote voting for citizens abroad, and what risks — fraud, cost, or unequal influence — would need to be managed first?
