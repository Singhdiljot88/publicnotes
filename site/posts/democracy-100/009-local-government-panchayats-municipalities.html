Local government: panchayats and municipalities after 73rd/74th Amendments

Note 009 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

For most citizens, government is not Parliament in New Delhi but the panchayat, municipal council, or corporation that deals with water, roads, sanitation, and local planning. Before 1992, local bodies existed in many states, but their powers, elections, and finances depended largely on state governments, and elections could be delayed. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments, passed in 1992 and in force in 1993, gave local government constitutional status.

The 73rd Amendment added Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects) for rural panchayats. It requires a three-tier system — village, intermediate (block), and district — in most states, direct elections every five years, reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and women, a Gram Sabha of voters at village level, a State Election Commission to conduct local elections, and a State Finance Commission to recommend sharing of revenues. The 74th Amendment added Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects) for municipalities, with three types — Nagar Panchayat for transitional areas, Municipal Council for smaller urban areas, and Municipal Corporation for larger urban areas — and similar provisions on elections, reservation, and finance.

In practice, the record is mixed, and that is a fair summary rather than a partisan judgement. Elections are now far more regular, and reservation has brought many first-time representatives, including women, into office.

Key takeaways

  • The 73rd Amendment constitutionalised panchayats; the 74th did the same for municipalities.
  • Both require regular elections, reservation, State Election Commissions, and State Finance Commissions.
  • Real power still depends on how much functions, funds, and staff states actually devolve.

Why it matters for civil studies: Local government is where democratic participation, service delivery, and federalism meet in everyday life — and where constitutional promises are tested.

Discussion question: What would make your local body more accountable: more money, more powers, better elections, or more citizen participation — and why?
