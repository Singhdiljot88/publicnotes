Opposition in Parliament: Role, Rights, and Limits

Note 024 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

A parliamentary democracy needs an opposition, not merely a losing side. The opposition's job is to scrutinise government proposals, offer alternatives, expose mistakes, and give voters a credible alternative government. It does this through questions, debates, amendments, motions — including no-confidence motions in the Lok Sabha — committee work, and public argument. Without that challenge, a majority could pass laws and spend money with little testing of its reasoning, and voters would have less information with which to judge it at the next election.

The Leader of the Opposition is the formal head of this role. The post is not named in the Constitution itself, but it received statutory recognition under the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977, which treats the holder on par with a Cabinet Minister. By convention, the leader must head the largest party in opposition, and Speakers have applied a further convention that the party should hold at least 10% of the House's seats. That convention mattered in practice: after the 2014 and 2019 elections, the largest opposition party in the Lok Sabha, the Congress, won 44 and 52 seats respectively and was not recognised as holding the post; after the 2024 election, with 99 seats, Rahul Gandhi was recognised as Leader of the Opposition.

The opposition also has concrete institutional rights. Its leader sits on selection committees for key appointments, including those for the Central Vigilance Commissioner, the Director of the CBI and the Lokpal, and, by convention, an opposition member (not necessarily the Leader of the Opposition personally) chairs the Public Accounts Committee. But its limits are real. It cannot block a determined majority in the Lok Sabha, it cannot pass its own bills without government support in practice, and its time for debates and private members' business depends on the business decided for the House. Its power is therefore mainly persuasive and supervisory: to delay, amend, expose and build a public case — not to govern from the opposition benches.

Key takeaways

  • The opposition scrutinises, amends and offers alternatives; it does not govern, but it shapes what governing must answer for.
  • The Leader of the Opposition is recognised by statute (1977), with a 10% seat convention applied by Speakers.
  • Opposition influence runs through questions, committees and selection panels, not through a veto over a Lok Sabha majority.

Why it matters for civil studies: The health of a democracy can often be judged by how much room its opposition has to question, delay and dissent without being shut out.

Discussion question: Should the 10% rule for recognising a Leader of the Opposition be kept, lowered, or replaced by recognising the largest opposition party regardless of size?
