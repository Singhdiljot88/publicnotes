Climate anxiety and youth politics

Note 095 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

Climate anxiety is a phrase young people use for worry, grief, anger, or helplessness about a warming planet and its effects on their future. It is not a single medical diagnosis, and researchers debate how to measure it, but the feeling is widely reported among students and young activists. In India, that worry is often concrete: heat, floods, air pollution, water stress, and crop loss already affect health, schooling, work, and family budgets in different regions. A young person does not need a global model to notice a hotter summer or a smoggy winter morning.

Indian youth climate politics takes several forms. Some join school strikes and campaigns linked to Fridays for Future, which began with weekly school strikes in Sweden in 2018 and spread to many countries, including India. Others work through clean-up drives, tree planting, campus projects, legal petitions, and engagement with local bodies on waste, air, and water. Still others focus on jobs and justice, asking who pays for cleaner energy and who gets the new work. These approaches pull in different directions. One stresses personal action. Another stresses government policy, regulation, and investment. A third stresses fairness between richer and poorer countries, and between richer and poorer Indians.

The politics is contested. Supporters of youth climate action argue that young people will live longest with the consequences, so they have a special stake in long-term decisions. Critics argue that protests alone do not design power grids, manage water, or protect livelihoods, and that climate policy must balance environmental goals with energy access, jobs, and development needs. Both points can be true, which is why climate debates are rarely settled by slogans. They are settled, slowly, through budgets, laws, courts, and implementation.

One episode shows the stakes without settling the argument. Disha Ravi, a young activist associated with Fridays for Future in India, was arrested in 2021 in connection with a toolkit related to the farmers' protests, and was later granted bail, with the judge describing police evidence as scanty and sketchy. Supporters of the arrest argued law enforcement must investigate alleged conspiracies; critics argued the case chilled activism. Note the distinction: the bail order was a decision in that case, not a general ruling on climate protest.

Key takeaways

  • Climate anxiety describes worry about future climate effects; it is a widely used term, not a single uniform diagnosis.
  • Youth climate action in India ranges from strikes and clean-ups to petitions, campus projects, and policy advocacy.
  • Climate policy involves trade-offs between environment, energy access, jobs, and fairness, so it is debated, not simply declared.

Why it matters for civil studies: Climate is a long-term area where today's young voters will bear tomorrow's costs, so intergenerational fairness is central.

Discussion question: How should a government weigh a young person's demand for faster climate action against a worker's demand for secure energy and jobs today?
