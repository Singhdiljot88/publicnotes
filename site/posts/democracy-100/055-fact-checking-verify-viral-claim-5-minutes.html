Fact-checking: how to verify a viral claim in 5 minutes

Note 055 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 1 November 2026 · Not legal advice

You do not need special software to do a useful first check of a viral claim. You need a short routine, and the discipline to pause before sharing. The routine below takes about five minutes for many claims, and its main purpose is not to prove everything true or false. It is to separate claims that are well supported from claims that should not be shared yet.

First, read past the headline and the caption. Headlines and captions are often written to provoke a reaction, and a video can be real while its caption is false. Ask: who is making the claim, what exactly is being claimed, and when and where is it supposed to have happened? If the post gives no date, place, or named source, that is a warning sign, not proof of falsehood, but a reason to check further.

Second, look for the original. For an image or video, a reverse image search can show whether the same material appeared earlier, in a different place, or in a different context. For a quote, search for the exact words on the speaker's official account or in a reputable news report. For a statistic, look for the original report, survey, or government release, and check whether the number has been rounded, taken out of context, or attached to the wrong year.

Third, compare across independent sources. One outlet repeating another outlet is not independent confirmation. Two or three outlets with their own reporting, or a primary document such as a court order, an official notice, or a data table, carry more weight. Established fact-checking organisations in India, such as BOOM, Alt News, and The Quint's WebQoof, publish their method and evidence, but they should still be read critically: check what evidence they cite, not only their verdict.

Fourth, watch for the usual tricks: old footage reused as if new, a cropped screenshot, a logo added to a fake graphic, a real event given a false cause, and satire presented as news. AI-detection tools can help, but they are not definitive, and a tool's score is not a verdict. If you cannot verify a claim in five minutes, the honest answer is "unverified", not "probably true". Sharing with a warning does not make an unverified claim safe, because many readers will only remember the claim.

Key takeaways

  • Pause first: check the exact claim, date, place, and source behind the headline or caption.
  • Find the original material and compare it with independent sources or primary documents.
  • If a claim cannot be verified quickly, treat it as unverified and do not share it as fact.

Why it matters for civil studies: Citizens who can check a claim quickly are harder to mislead, and their choices are more likely to rest on evidence than on emotion or repetition.

Discussion question: A friend shares a dramatic video with no source. What is the most helpful reply you could send in under a minute — and what should you avoid saying?
