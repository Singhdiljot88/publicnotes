Space sector reforms and private companies

Note 224 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

For decades, India's space activities were carried out almost entirely by ISRO, with private companies acting only as suppliers of parts. Reforms since 2020 aim to open the sector to private players, to expand India's small share of the global space economy and free ISRO to focus on research and advanced missions.

The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre (IN-SPACe), set up in 2020, authorises and supervises private space activities and gives companies access to ISRO's facilities. NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), formed in 2019, is ISRO's commercial arm; it owns and operates satellites built for customers and markets launch services. The Indian Space Policy 2023 defined the roles of ISRO, IN-SPACe, NSIL and private companies, allowing private firms to undertake end-to-end activities, including building and launching rockets and satellites and operating ground stations. In 2024 the government eased foreign direct investment rules, allowing up to 74% FDI in satellite manufacturing and operations and up to 49% in launch vehicles through the automatic route, and up to 100% in components.

Indian start-ups have responded. Skyroot Aerospace launched Vikram-S, India's first privately built rocket, on a suborbital flight in November 2022. Agnikul Cosmos launched a rocket with a 3D-printed semi-cryogenic engine from its own launch pad at Sriharikota in May 2024. Companies such as Pixxel are building earth-imaging satellites. Private industry is also building PSLV rockets under a technology transfer arrangement with Hindustan Aeronautics and Larsen & Toubro.

India's space economy was estimated at about 8 billion US dollars, roughly 2% of the global space economy, and the government's target is 44 billion dollars by 2033. A national space activities law to provide legal certainty on licensing, liability and debris has been under discussion.

Key takeaways

  • IN-SPACe (2020) authorises private space activities; NSIL (2019) is ISRO's commercial arm.
  • The Space Policy 2023 and 2024 FDI changes opened the sector to private and foreign investment.
  • Skyroot and Agnikul have launched private rockets; the target is a 44 billion dollar space economy by 2033.

Why it matters for UPSC: Space sector reforms and private participation are frequent recent GS III questions.

Practice question: How do the space sector reforms change ISRO's role? What risks should be managed?
