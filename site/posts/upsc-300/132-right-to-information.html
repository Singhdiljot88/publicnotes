The Right to Information Act

Note 132 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The Right to Information Act, 2005 gives every citizen the right to request information from public authorities. It grew out of grassroots campaigns, notably the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan's public hearings in Rajasthan in the 1990s, which exposed corruption by comparing official records with what villagers had actually received. The Supreme Court had already recognised the right to information as part of free speech under Article 19(1)(a).

Under the Act, public authorities must appoint public information officers and proactively publish key information about their functioning. A request must normally be answered within 30 days, or within 48 hours if it concerns the life or liberty of a person. Applicants need not give reasons. First appeals go to a senior officer within the authority, and second appeals to the Central or State Information Commission, which can impose penalties of up to Rs 25,000 on officers who fail to provide information without reasonable cause.

Section 8 lists exemptions, including information that would harm sovereignty or security, cabinet papers until a decision is taken, and personal information unrelated to public activity. Intelligence and security organisations listed in the Second Schedule are exempt except for information about corruption and human rights violations.

The Act has been widely used to expose corruption and improve service delivery, but it faces challenges. The RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019 allowed the Union government to set the tenure and salaries of information commissioners, which critics said reduced their independence. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 amended Section 8(1)(j) to exempt all personal information, removing the earlier public interest test, which transparency activists have strongly criticised. Vacancies in information commissions have caused large backlogs, and dozens of RTI users have been killed over the years.

Key takeaways

  • The RTI Act (2005) requires replies within 30 days, or 48 hours for life and liberty.
  • Information commissions hear appeals and can penalise officers.
  • The 2019 amendment and the 2023 data protection law's change to Section 8(1)(j) are criticised as weakening the Act.

Why it matters for UPSC: RTI is a core governance topic and a frequent Prelims and Mains question, with recent amendments very likely to be asked.

Practice question: Has the RTI Act improved accountability in India? What threats does it face today?
