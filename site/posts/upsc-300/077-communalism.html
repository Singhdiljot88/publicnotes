Communalism

Note 077 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In the Indian context, communalism means the belief that people who follow the same religion share common social, political and economic interests that are different from, and opposed to, those of other religious communities. It turns religious identity into a political identity, and it can lead to prejudice, discrimination and violence.

Historians trace modern communalism to the colonial period. The British census categorised people by religion, separate electorates (introduced in 1909) gave political weight to religious identity, and economic competition among the educated middle classes was sometimes expressed in religious terms. Communal organisations of different communities grew in the early twentieth century. Partition in 1947, with its massive violence, was the most catastrophic result.

After independence, India has seen several major episodes of communal violence, including in 1984 against Sikhs in Delhi, the riots after the Babri Masjid demolition in 1992–93, Gujarat in 2002, Muzaffarnagar in 2013 and north-east Delhi in 2020. Commissions of inquiry have often found political mobilisation, rumours and failures of policing behind such violence. Social media has made the spread of rumours and hate speech faster.

Responses include constitutional secularism, laws against hate speech and promoting enmity between groups (now in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the corresponding sections of the Indian Penal Code), police reform, and civil society efforts at inter-faith dialogue. The Supreme Court has directed police to act against hate speech on their own without waiting for complaints. Scholars stress that everyday coexistence, shared neighbourhoods and civic associations are among the strongest protections against communal violence.

Key takeaways

  • Communalism turns religious identity into opposed political interests.
  • Colonial policies such as separate electorates contributed to its growth, with Partition as the worst outcome.
  • Laws, police reform, secular institutions and everyday civic ties are key responses.

Why it matters for UPSC: Communalism is a named topic in the GS I syllabus and appears regularly in Mains questions.

Practice question: What role can civil society play in preventing communal violence? Illustrate with examples.
