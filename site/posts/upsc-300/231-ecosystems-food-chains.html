Ecosystems: structure, energy flow and food chains

Note 231 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their physical surroundings. It has two broad parts. The biotic part is made up of producers (green plants and algae that make food through photosynthesis), consumers (herbivores, carnivores and omnivores) and decomposers (bacteria and fungi that break down dead matter). The abiotic part includes sunlight, water, soil, temperature and nutrients. Ecosystems range from a small pond to a forest, a grassland or the open ocean.

Energy enters almost every ecosystem as sunlight and flows in one direction, from producers to herbivores to carnivores. At each step most of the energy is lost as heat through respiration. Raymond Lindeman's "ten per cent law" (1942) captures this: on average only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next. This is why food chains rarely have more than four or five links, and why a plant-based diet supports more people per hectare than a meat-based one.

A food chain is a single path of eating, such as grass to grasshopper to frog to snake to hawk. In nature these chains interlock into food webs, which make ecosystems more stable, because a predator can switch prey if one species declines. Ecological pyramids show numbers, biomass or energy at each level. The pyramid of energy is always upright. The pyramid of numbers can be inverted (many insects on one tree), and the pyramid of biomass can be inverted in the sea, where a small mass of fast-growing phytoplankton supports a larger mass of zooplankton.

Some pollutants do not break down and build up along the chain. This is biomagnification. DDT, mercury and some other persistent chemicals reach their highest concentrations in top predators, as shown by the thinning of eggshells in birds of prey that led to DDT bans in many countries.

Key takeaways

  • Energy flows one way and only about 10% passes between trophic levels (Lindeman's law), limiting the length of food chains.
  • Food webs are more stable than single chains; the pyramid of energy is always upright, while numbers and biomass pyramids can be inverted.
  • Persistent pollutants such as DDT and mercury biomagnify, concentrating in top predators.

Why it matters for UPSC: Ecosystem basics underpin prelims questions on trophic levels, pyramids and biomagnification, and frame mains answers on conservation.

Practice question: Explain why the pyramid of energy can never be inverted, while pyramids of numbers and biomass sometimes are.
