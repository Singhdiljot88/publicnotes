Air pollution and the National Clean Air Programme

Note 233 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Air pollution is among India's most serious public health problems. The main pollutants are particulate matter (PM10 and the finer PM2.5, which enters deep into the lungs and bloodstream), nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, ozone, carbon monoxide and ammonia. Sources include vehicles, power plants, industry, construction dust, burning of solid fuels in homes, and the seasonal burning of crop residue in north-west India. In winter, low temperatures and still air trap pollution close to the ground across the Indo-Gangetic plain, giving Delhi and other northern cities some of the worst air in the world.

India's legal base is the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, and the National Ambient Air Quality Standards, revised in 2009 to cover 12 pollutants. The National Air Quality Index, launched in 2014, reports air quality in six categories from good to severe. The National Clean Air Programme (NCAP), launched in 2019, was India's first national plan with a time-bound target. It covers about 130 "non-attainment" cities that failed to meet standards. Its revised goal is a reduction of up to 40% in PM10 levels by 2025-26 compared with 2017-18, and funding to cities is linked to performance.

For Delhi and the National Capital Region, the Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM), set up under a 2021 law, coordinates action across states. The Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) triggers stricter steps, from dust control to stopping construction and restricting vehicles, as the air quality index worsens. Other measures include the leap to BS-VI fuel norms in 2020, the Ujjwala scheme for cooking gas, and support for machines that manage crop stubble.

Critics note that progress across NCAP cities has been uneven, that funds have mostly gone to dust control rather than to industry, vehicles or burning, and that pollution crosses city and state lines.

Key takeaways

  • PM2.5 is the most harmful pollutant; winter inversion traps pollution across the Indo-Gangetic plain.
  • NCAP (2019) covers about 130 non-attainment cities, targeting up to 40% lower PM10 by 2025-26 against 2017-18.
  • CAQM (2021) and GRAP manage Delhi-NCR's air with graded, airshed-wide actions.

Why it matters for UPSC: Air pollution combines health, federalism and agriculture, and appears every year in GS III and essay papers.

Practice question: Why has air pollution control in India needed an airshed approach rather than a city-by-city one?
