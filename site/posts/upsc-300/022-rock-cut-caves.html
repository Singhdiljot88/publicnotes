Rock-cut caves: Barabar to Ellora

Note 022 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India has more than a thousand rock-cut caves, carved over about 1,500 years. The earliest are the Barabar caves in Bihar, carved in the third century BCE under Ashoka and his grandson Dasharatha for the Ajivika sect. Their polished granite walls show the Mauryan mastery of stone.

Buddhist caves in the western Deccan, carved from the second century BCE onwards, follow two types. The chaitya was a prayer hall with a stupa at one end and a horseshoe-shaped window, as at Karla and Bhaja. The vihara was a monastery with cells for monks around a central hall.

Ajanta, in Maharashtra, has about thirty caves carved in two phases: an early phase around the second and first centuries BCE and a later phase in the fifth century CE under the Vakatakas. Its murals, painted on prepared plaster, show scenes from the Jatakas and the Buddha's life and are among the finest surviving ancient paintings anywhere. The image of the Bodhisattva Padmapani is the best known.

Ellora, also in Maharashtra, has thirty-four caves made between about the sixth and tenth centuries: Buddhist, Hindu and Jain caves side by side, a striking example of religious coexistence. Cave 16, the Kailasa temple, was carved downward from a single rock under the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I in the eighth century. The Elephanta caves near Mumbai have a giant three-headed Shiva (Maheshmurti). Ajanta, Ellora and Elephanta are all UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Key takeaways

  • The Barabar caves (third century BCE) are the earliest rock-cut caves.
  • Chaityas were prayer halls and viharas were monasteries.
  • Ajanta is famous for murals, Ellora for its multi-faith caves and the Kailasa temple.

Why it matters for UPSC: Cave sites, their religions and dynasties are frequently tested in Prelims.

Practice question: How do the caves at Ellora reflect the religious pluralism of early medieval India?
