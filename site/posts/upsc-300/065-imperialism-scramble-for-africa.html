Imperialism and the scramble for Africa

Note 065 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Between about 1870 and 1914, European powers expanded their empires dramatically, a period often called the age of new imperialism. Industrial economies needed raw materials and markets, investors sought profitable outlets, and nationalism made colonies symbols of prestige. Ideas of racial superiority and a "civilising mission" provided justification. Technologies such as steamships, the telegraph, quinine against malaria and the machine gun made conquest easier.

Africa saw the most rapid partition. In 1870, Europeans controlled about a tenth of the continent, mostly along the coasts. At the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, convened by Bismarck, European powers set rules for claiming African territory without consulting any African. By 1914, only Ethiopia, which defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remained independent. Borders were drawn with little regard for ethnic, linguistic or economic realities, creating problems that persist. King Leopold II of Belgium ruled the Congo Free State as personal property, and forced labour there caused millions of deaths.

Asia was also divided. Britain controlled India, Burma and Malaya; France took Indochina; the Dutch held Indonesia. China was forced to open treaty ports after the Opium Wars (1839–42 and 1856–60) and divided into spheres of influence. Japan modernised rapidly after the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and became an imperial power itself, defeating China in 1895 and Russia in 1905.

Imperialism left deep legacies: arbitrary borders, economies built around exporting raw materials, and the anti-colonial nationalism that would produce dozens of new nations after 1945.

Key takeaways

  • Economic interests, nationalism and racist ideology drove new imperialism after 1870.
  • The Berlin Conference (1884–85) set rules for dividing Africa; only Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent.
  • China was weakened by the Opium Wars, while Japan became an imperial power after 1868.

Why it matters for UPSC: Imperialism links world history to India's own colonial experience and to modern global inequality.

Practice question: How did the partition of Africa by European powers shape the continent's political problems after independence?
