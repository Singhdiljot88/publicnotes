Capital account convertibility and the international rupee

Note 190 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Capital account convertibility means the freedom to convert local financial assets into foreign ones and back at market-determined rates, without restrictions. It allows residents to invest abroad and foreigners to invest freely in a country. India has gradually liberalised its capital account but kept controls, for example on how much residents can send abroad each year (250,000 dollars under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme), on foreign borrowing by companies and on foreign holdings of government bonds.

The RBI's Tarapore committees examined full convertibility in 1997 and 2006. They recommended moving towards it only after meeting preconditions: a low fiscal deficit, low inflation, a strong banking system with low bad loans, and adequate foreign exchange reserves. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, when sudden capital outflows devastated economies such as Thailand and Indonesia, reinforced India's caution. India's partial controls are often credited with protecting it from the worst of that crisis and the 2008 global financial crisis.

Internationalisation of the rupee means wider use of the rupee in trade, investment and reserves outside India. In July 2022 the RBI allowed international trade to be invoiced and settled in rupees, through special rupee vostro accounts that foreign banks open with Indian banks. Russia, the UAE and several other countries have used the arrangement to varying degrees. India has also signed local currency settlement agreements with the UAE, Indonesia and the Maldives, and UPI is being linked abroad. An RBI working group in 2023 laid out a roadmap for rupee internationalisation.

Benefits include lower exchange risk for Indian traders, less dependence on the dollar and lower reserve needs. Obstacles include the rupee's limited convertibility, India's trade deficits (which leave trading partners holding rupees they cannot easily use), and the depth of India's financial markets.

Key takeaways

  • India has partial capital account convertibility, with limits such as 250,000 dollars a year under the LRS.
  • The Tarapore committees (1997, 2006) set preconditions for full convertibility.
  • Since 2022, trade can be settled in rupees through special vostro accounts.

Why it matters for UPSC: Convertibility and rupee internationalisation are recurring GS III topics in the external sector.

Practice question: What are the preconditions for full capital account convertibility, and has India met them?
