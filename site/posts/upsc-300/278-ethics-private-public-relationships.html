Ethics in private and public relationships

Note 278 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The syllabus includes ethics in private and public relationships. Private relationships, with family, friends and neighbours, are based on personal bonds, emotion and reciprocity. Public relationships, between officials and citizens, or between public institutions, are based on roles, rules and the public interest.

The values that suit each sphere differ. In private life, loyalty, partiality towards loved ones and forgiveness are often virtues. In public life, the same impulses can become nepotism, favouritism and cover-ups. A parent should favour their own child; a recruiting officer must not. The ethical challenge for a public servant is to keep personal loyalties from distorting public duties, while remaining humane.

The two spheres also influence each other. A person who is honest and respectful at home is more likely to be so at work, and vice versa. Private conduct can become a public matter when it affects trust in an office: a public servant's lavish lifestyle beyond known income, harassment of colleagues, or domestic violence by a police officer raise questions about fitness for public duty. At the same time, public officials are entitled to privacy in matters that do not affect their work, and the line must be drawn carefully.

Gender justice is an important dimension. Ethical relationships require respect for the dignity and autonomy of women at home and at work. The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013, building on the Supreme Court's Vishaka guidelines of 1997, makes this a legal duty for employers.

Accountability in public relationships also means treating every citizen with equal respect, regardless of status, caste, gender or religion, which is the essence of fraternity.

Key takeaways

  • Private relationships allow partiality; public roles demand impartiality.
  • Private conduct becomes a public concern when it affects trust in an office.
  • Respect for dignity, especially gender justice, applies in both spheres.

Why it matters for UPSC: This is a named syllabus topic, often tested through questions on nepotism, conflict of interest and workplace ethics.

Practice question: Where should the line be drawn between a public servant's private life and public accountability?
