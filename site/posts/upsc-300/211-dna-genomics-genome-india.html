DNA, genomics and Genome India

Note 211 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) carries the genetic instructions of living things. It is a double helix, a structure described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953 using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. Each strand is a chain of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C), with A pairing with T and G with C. A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein. DNA is first copied into messenger RNA (transcription), which is then read to build a protein (translation). The human genome has about 3 billion base pairs and around 20,000 protein-coding genes.

The Human Genome Project, an international effort, produced a nearly complete sequence of the human genome in 2003. Since then the cost of sequencing a genome has fallen from billions of dollars to a few hundred, making genomics a practical tool in medicine, agriculture and forensics.

Most genetic research has been done on people of European ancestry, so it may not reflect the genetic diversity of other populations. India, with thousands of endogamous communities, is genetically very diverse, and some communities carry high rates of particular inherited disorders, such as sickle cell disease among several tribal groups. The Genome India Project, led by the Department of Biotechnology and launched in 2020, sequenced the genomes of 10,000 healthy Indians from 99 communities; the data were released to researchers in January 2025 through the Indian Biological Data Centre. The data can help identify disease-causing variants, design genetic tests, and develop medicines suited to Indian populations.

Genomics raises ethical questions: consent, privacy of genetic data, the risk of discrimination by employers or insurers, and misuse of genetic information to support ideas of caste or racial purity. India does not yet have a specific law on genetic data.

Key takeaways

  • DNA is a double helix of four bases; genes code for proteins via RNA.
  • The Human Genome Project was completed in 2003; sequencing costs have collapsed since.
  • The Genome India Project sequenced 10,000 Indian genomes, released in January 2025.

Why it matters for UPSC: Genetics basics and the Genome India Project are frequent Prelims topics, and genomic ethics suits GS III and IV.

Practice question: What are the benefits and risks of building a national genomic database? Suggest safeguards.
