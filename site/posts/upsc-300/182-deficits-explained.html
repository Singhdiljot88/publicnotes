Fiscal, revenue and primary deficits

Note 182 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Government finances are judged by several deficit measures, each telling a different story.

Government receipts are divided into revenue receipts, such as taxes and non-tax income (dividends, fees, interest), which do not create liabilities, and capital receipts, such as borrowing, loan recoveries and disinvestment proceeds. Expenditure is divided into revenue expenditure, which does not create assets (salaries, pensions, interest, subsidies), and capital expenditure, which creates assets or reduces liabilities (roads, railways, defence equipment, loans to states).

The revenue deficit is revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts. A revenue deficit means the government is borrowing to pay for day-to-day expenses, which is considered poor practice. The effective revenue deficit subtracts grants given to states for creating capital assets, because those grants are revenue spending for the Union but build assets in the states.

The fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus total receipts other than borrowing. It equals the government's total borrowing requirement in a year and is the most closely watched measure. Persistently high fiscal deficits raise public debt, push up interest rates, can "crowd out" private investment, and may fuel inflation. The primary deficit is the fiscal deficit minus interest payments. It shows whether current policies, apart from the burden of past debt, are adding to borrowing. India's interest payments take up a large share of revenue receipts, around two-fifths in recent Union budgets, which is why the primary deficit is much smaller than the fiscal deficit.

Monetised deficit refers to the part of the deficit financed by the central bank creating money. India ended automatic monetisation through ad hoc treasury bills in 1997.

The Union government's fiscal deficit rose to 9.2% of GDP in the pandemic year 2020-21 and has been reduced each year since, to a target of 4.3% in 2026-27. States' combined fiscal deficits add roughly another 3% of GDP.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue deficit shows borrowing for day-to-day spending; fiscal deficit is total borrowing.
  • Primary deficit removes interest payments to show the effect of current policy.
  • The Union fiscal deficit fell from 9.2% of GDP in 2020-21 to a 4.3% target in 2026-27.

Why it matters for UPSC: Deficit definitions are among the most frequently asked Prelims economy questions.

Practice question: Why is the quality of fiscal deficit, revenue versus capital spending, as important as its size?
