The exchange rate and the rupee

Note 187 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Under a fixed exchange rate, the central bank sets the rate and defends it. Under a floating rate, it is set by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. Most countries, including India, use a managed float: the market largely determines the rate, but the central bank intervenes to smooth excessive volatility, not to defend a particular level.

India's rupee was pegged first to the British pound and then to a basket of currencies. After the 1991 crisis, the rupee was devalued and then moved to a market-determined rate in 1993. The rupee became fully convertible on the current account in 1994, meaning it can be freely exchanged for trade and other current transactions, while capital account convertibility remains partial.

When the rupee depreciates, Indian exports become cheaper abroad and imports more expensive. This can help exporters but raises the cost of imported oil, electronics and gold, adding to inflation, and increases the rupee cost of repaying foreign debt. When it appreciates, the reverse happens. The rupee has tended to depreciate gradually over the long run, because India's inflation has generally been higher than that of its trading partners and because it runs a current account deficit. In 2026 the rupee came under pressure amid high oil prices linked to the West Asia conflict and capital outflows, and the RBI sold dollars from its reserves to limit volatility.

Economists look at the real effective exchange rate (REER), which adjusts the rupee's value against a basket of trading partners' currencies for differences in inflation. It is a better guide to competitiveness than the dollar-rupee rate alone. The nominal effective exchange rate (NEER) uses the same basket without inflation adjustment.

The "impossible trinity" says a country cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and an independent monetary policy; it must choose two. India has chosen a flexible exchange rate with partial capital controls to preserve monetary independence.

Key takeaways

  • India follows a managed float, with the RBI smoothing volatility rather than targeting a level.
  • The rupee has been fully convertible on the current account since 1994, but only partially on the capital account.
  • REER adjusts for inflation and is a better measure of competitiveness than the dollar rate.

Why it matters for UPSC: Exchange rate regimes, convertibility and the impossible trinity are regular Prelims and Mains topics.

Practice question: Is a weaker rupee good or bad for the Indian economy? Discuss.
