OBCs, the creamy layer and sub-categorisation

Note 143 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Other Backward Classes (OBCs) are socially and educationally backward classes recognised under Articles 15(4) and 16(4). After the Mandal Commission's report was implemented in 1990, the Union government reserves 27% of central jobs and, since 2008, seats in central educational institutions for OBCs. The Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney (1992) upheld this but excluded the "creamy layer", the better-off among OBCs, to ensure benefits reach those who need them. The income limit for the creamy layer was last raised to Rs 8 lakh a year in 2017; salary and agricultural income of parents are not counted, and children of senior officials are excluded on the basis of their parents' posts.

The National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) was first created by statute in 1993. The 102nd Amendment in 2018 gave it constitutional status under Article 338B. The Supreme Court in the Maratha reservation case (2021) read that amendment as removing states' power to identify backward classes, and Parliament quickly passed the 105th Amendment in 2021 to restore that power.

The benefits of OBC reservation have been unevenly shared. The Justice G. Rohini Commission was set up in 2017 to examine sub-categorisation of OBCs. Its analysis, as reported, found that a small number of the more than 2,600 castes in the central OBC list had received a large share of jobs and admissions, while about a thousand had received none. Its report was submitted in 2023 but has not been made public or implemented. Several states, such as Bihar, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, already sub-categorise OBCs.

Demands by groups such as the Marathas, Jats and Patidars for OBC status, and the 50% ceiling, have led to legal and political battles, and the upcoming caste census is expected to intensify them.

Key takeaways

  • OBCs get 27% reservation in central jobs and institutions, excluding the creamy layer (Rs 8 lakh limit).
  • The NCBC got constitutional status in 2018; the 105th Amendment (2021) restored states' power to list OBCs.
  • The Rohini Commission on sub-categorisation reported in 2023, but its report is not public.

Why it matters for UPSC: OBC reservation, the creamy layer and sub-categorisation are frequent GS II topics, made more relevant by the caste census.

Practice question: Is sub-categorisation of OBCs necessary for social justice? Discuss the arguments and challenges.
