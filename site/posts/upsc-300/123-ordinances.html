Ordinances: law-making by the executive

Note 123 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Article 123 allows the President to issue an ordinance when either house of Parliament is not in session and circumstances require immediate action. Article 213 gives governors the same power in states. An ordinance has the same force as an act of Parliament, but it must be laid before both houses when they reassemble and ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reconvenes, unless approved earlier. So an ordinance can last up to six months and six weeks. It can also be withdrawn at any time. The President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, and an ordinance can be issued only on subjects on which Parliament can legislate. It cannot amend the Constitution.

The framers saw ordinances as an emergency power, to be used rarely. In practice they have been used frequently, sometimes to push through laws that a government could not pass in the Rajya Sabha. The most striking abuse was in Bihar, where some ordinances were repromulgated repeatedly for years without ever being placed before the legislature.

The courts have set limits. In R. C. Cooper (1970), the Supreme Court held that the President's satisfaction could be challenged if it was based on mala fide reasons; the 38th Amendment tried to bar review, but the 44th Amendment removed that bar. In D. C. Wadhwa (1987), the Court held that repeated repromulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature was a fraud on the Constitution. In Krishna Kumar Singh (2017), a seven-judge bench held that repromulgation is unconstitutional, that the failure to place an ordinance before the legislature is a constitutional violation, and that ordinances are subject to judicial review.

Ordinances remain useful in genuine emergencies, but frequent use weakens parliamentary scrutiny and the deliberative role of the legislature.

Key takeaways

  • Ordinances under Articles 123 and 213 can last up to six weeks after the legislature reassembles.
  • They are issued on ministerial advice and cannot amend the Constitution.
  • D. C. Wadhwa (1987) and Krishna Kumar Singh (2017) condemned repromulgation and confirmed judicial review.

Why it matters for UPSC: Ordinance powers and the related judgments are frequently tested and relevant to separation-of-powers questions.

Practice question: Has the ordinance power been used as intended by the framers? Suggest safeguards against its misuse.
