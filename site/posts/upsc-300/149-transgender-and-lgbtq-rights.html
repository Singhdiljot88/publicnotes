Transgender and LGBTQ+ rights

Note 149 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In NALSA v. Union of India (2014), the Supreme Court recognised transgender persons as a "third gender", affirmed their right to self-identify their gender, and directed governments to treat them as socially and educationally backward classes for reservations and to provide welfare measures. The judgment drew on Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21.

The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 prohibits discrimination against transgender persons in education, employment, health care and access to public facilities, and created a National Council for Transgender Persons. But it requires a certificate of identity from the district magistrate, and a revised certificate after gender-affirming surgery to identify as male or female, which activists argue contradicts NALSA's principle of self-identification. It also prescribes lighter punishments for offences against transgender persons than for similar offences against others. The government launched the SMILE scheme for welfare and the National Portal for Transgender Persons.

In Navtej Singh Johar (2018), a five-judge bench decriminalised consensual same-sex relations between adults by reading down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, overruling Suresh Kumar Koushal (2013). The Court held that sexual orientation is an essential attribute of privacy and dignity. Section 377's remaining parts were not carried into the new Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita in 2024, leaving a gap in protection for non-consensual acts against men and transgender persons, which has been criticised.

In Supriyo Chakraborty (2023), the Supreme Court unanimously declined to recognise same-sex marriage, holding that this was for Parliament to decide, though it affirmed that queer persons have the right to form relationships free from harassment. The government set up a committee under the Cabinet Secretary to examine the rights of queer couples in matters such as joint bank accounts and medical decisions.

Key takeaways

  • NALSA (2014) recognised the third gender and the right to self-identify.
  • The Transgender Persons Act (2019) bans discrimination but requires certification, which is criticised.
  • Navtej (2018) decriminalised same-sex relations; Supriyo (2023) left same-sex marriage to Parliament.

Why it matters for UPSC: LGBTQ+ rights cover GS II social justice, polity and GS IV ethics, and the key judgments are often asked.

Practice question: Should courts or legislatures lead on extending rights to LGBTQ+ persons? Discuss with reference to Indian judgments.
