E-waste, batteries and the circular economy

Note 239 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

A circular economy keeps materials in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair, refurbishment and recycling, instead of the linear model of take, make and throw away. For India, which imports much of its critical minerals and generates fast-growing waste, circularity is both an environmental and an economic strategy. NITI Aayog and the Environment Ministry have identified priority areas such as electronics, batteries, vehicles, tyres, plastics and metals.

India is among the largest generators of electronic waste in the world. E-waste contains valuable metals such as copper, gold and rare elements, but also toxic substances such as lead, mercury and cadmium. Most of it is handled by the informal sector, where workers dismantle devices and burn or acid-wash parts without protection, exposing themselves and harming soil and water. The E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022, in force from April 2023, put Extended Producer Responsibility at the centre. Producers must meet yearly recycling targets, and registered recyclers generate EPR certificates on a central online portal that producers can buy. The rules widened the list of covered products, including solar panels.

The Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 apply EPR to all kinds of batteries, including those of electric vehicles, and set targets for recovering materials and using recycled content in new batteries. As the number of electric vehicles grows, recovering lithium, cobalt and nickel from old batteries could reduce India's reliance on imports. Similar EPR rules now cover tyres and used oil, and a vehicle scrappage policy aims to retire old, polluting vehicles.

The challenges are bringing the informal sector into the formal system, preventing fake certificates, and building enough recycling capacity.

Key takeaways

  • A circular economy keeps materials in use; India's priority sectors include electronics, batteries, vehicles and plastics.
  • E-Waste Rules 2022 (from April 2023) and Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 rely on EPR with tradable certificates.
  • Recycling EV batteries can reduce import dependence for critical minerals; formalising the informal sector is the key challenge.

Why it matters for UPSC: The circular economy links environment, resource security and jobs, and is a growing theme in GS III.

Practice question: How can Extended Producer Responsibility help India manage its growing e-waste problem?
