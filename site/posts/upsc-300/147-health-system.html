India's health system

Note 147 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Health is a state subject, but the Union plays a large role through national programmes and funding. India's public health spending has historically been low. According to the National Health Accounts estimates for 2021–22, government health expenditure was about 1.8% of GDP, while out-of-pocket spending by households fell to about 39% of total health expenditure, from over 64% in 2013–14. The National Health Policy 2017 set a goal of raising government health spending to 2.5% of GDP by 2025, which was not met.

India's public system has three tiers: sub-centres and primary health centres for primary care, community health centres for secondary care, and district and medical college hospitals for tertiary care. The National Health Mission, combining the rural (2005) and urban (2013) missions, strengthened this system, including through ASHA workers, community health volunteers in every village.

Ayushman Bharat, launched in 2018, has two pillars. Health and wellness centres, renamed Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, upgrade sub-centres and primary health centres to provide comprehensive primary care, including screening for diabetes, hypertension and cancers. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY) provides cashless hospital cover of Rs 5 lakh per family per year for poor and vulnerable families, and since 2024 for all citizens aged 70 and above regardless of income. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission creates digital health IDs and records.

Challenges include shortages of doctors, nurses and specialists, especially in rural areas; uneven quality; the growing burden of non-communicable diseases, which cause most deaths; and dependence on an unregulated private sector. India's achievements include eliminating polio (certified in 2014) and maternal and neonatal tetanus, and large gains in institutional deliveries.

Key takeaways

  • Government health spending is about 1.8% of GDP; out-of-pocket spending has fallen to about 39%.
  • Ayushman Bharat combines Arogya Mandirs for primary care with PM-JAY's Rs 5 lakh hospital cover.
  • Workforce shortages and non-communicable diseases are major challenges.

Why it matters for UPSC: Health policy is a recurring GS II question, often requiring data on spending and schemes.

Practice question: Can insurance-based schemes like PM-JAY achieve universal health coverage without stronger public primary care?
