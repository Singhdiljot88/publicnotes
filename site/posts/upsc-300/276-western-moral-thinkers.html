UPSC 300 · 🧠 Theme 28: Ethics: foundations · GS IV

Western moral thinkers

Note 276 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The syllabus asks for the contributions of moral thinkers and philosophers from India and the world. Several Western thinkers are essential.

Socrates held that virtue is knowledge and that "the unexamined life is not worth living". Plato, in The Republic, imagined a just state ruled by philosopher-kings and described justice as each part of society doing its proper role. Aristotle developed virtue ethics: the goal of life is eudaimonia, flourishing, achieved by developing virtues through habit. Each virtue is a golden mean between extremes, so courage lies between cowardice and recklessness.

Immanuel Kant founded the leading duty-based (deontological) ethics. Morality rests on reason, not consequences. His categorical imperative says act only on principles you could will to be universal laws, and treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means. On this view lying is wrong even to achieve a good result.

Utilitarianism, developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, judges actions by their consequences: the right act produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Bentham treated all pleasures as comparable; Mill distinguished higher and lower pleasures and, in On Liberty (1859), defended individual freedom through the harm principle: the state may restrict liberty only to prevent harm to others. Utilitarian reasoning is common in policy, as in cost-benefit analysis, but critics say it can justify sacrificing minorities.

John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), asked what principles people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance", not knowing their own position in society. He argued they would choose equal basic liberties and allow inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (the difference principle). Other thinkers often cited include Thomas Hobbes and John Locke on the social contract, and Amartya Sen on capabilities and justice.

Key takeaways

  • Aristotle's virtue ethics seeks flourishing through habits of virtue and the golden mean.
  • Kant's categorical imperative grounds morality in duty and respect for persons.
  • Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) judges by consequences; Rawls's veil of ignorance supports fairness to the least advantaged.

Why it matters for UPSC: Thinkers and their ideas are a recurring GS IV question, often asking for relevance to administration today.

Practice question: How can Rawls's difference principle guide the design of welfare policy in India?
