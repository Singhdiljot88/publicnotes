The Indian model of secularism

Note 079 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Secularism in the Western sense often means a strict separation of religion and state, as in France's principle of laïcité, where the state keeps religion out of public life. Indian secularism developed differently. It is often described as "principled distance": the state is not aligned with any religion, but it can engage with religions to protect equality and freedom, for example by reforming practices such as untouchability or by supporting minority educational institutions.

The word "secular" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, but the principle was present in the original Constitution. Articles 25 to 28 guarantee freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health; the freedom of religious denominations to manage their affairs; freedom from taxes for promoting a religion; and freedom from compulsory religious instruction in state-funded schools. Articles 29 and 30 protect the cultural and educational rights of minorities. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held secularism to be part of the basic structure.

Indian secularism faces several debates. Critics argue that the state has intervened more in Hindu personal law (through the Hindu Code Bills of the 1950s) than in other communities' personal laws, and that a Uniform Civil Code, which Article 44 lists as a directive principle, is needed. Uttarakhand became the first state to implement a Uniform Civil Code, in January 2025. Others argue that a UCC must not be imposed in a way that targets minorities. There are also debates about state control of temples, the use of religion in elections, and anti-conversion laws in several states.

Key takeaways

  • Indian secularism means principled distance, not strict separation.
  • Articles 25–30 protect religious freedom and minority rights; secularism is part of the basic structure (Bommai, 1994).
  • The Uniform Civil Code and state control of religious institutions remain contested.

Why it matters for UPSC: Secularism is a GS I society topic and a GS II polity topic, and often appears in essay questions.

Practice question: How is Indian secularism different from the Western model? Is the difference a strength or a weakness?
