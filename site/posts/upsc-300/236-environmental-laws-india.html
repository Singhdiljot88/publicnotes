India's environmental laws: a framework

Note 236 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

India's environmental protection rests on the Constitution as well as statutes. The 42nd Amendment (1976) added Article 48A, a Directive Principle asking the state to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife, and Article 51A(g), a fundamental duty of every citizen to protect the natural environment. The Supreme Court has read the right to a clean and healthy environment into the right to life under Article 21, and in 2024 it recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change.

The main statutes came in stages. The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 created protected areas and schedules of protected species; its 2022 amendment rationalised the schedules and gave effect to CITES. The Water Act, 1974 and the Air Act, 1981 set up the pollution control boards. The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 required central approval to divert forest land; it was amended in 2023 and renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, which narrowed the land it covers and exempted some categories, such as strategic projects near borders. The amendment has been challenged in the Supreme Court.

After the Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984, Parliament passed the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, an umbrella law that lets the Centre set standards, regulate industry and issue rules. Most waste rules and the EIA notification are made under it. Other laws include the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (amended in 2023), the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, and the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.

Courts have shaped this field through doctrines such as the polluter pays principle, the precautionary principle, the public trust doctrine and absolute liability for hazardous industries (from the M.C. Mehta oleum gas case of 1986). The Jan Vishwas Act, 2023 replaced jail terms for many minor offences under environmental laws with monetary penalties, which supporters see as ease of business and critics as weaker deterrence.

Key takeaways

  • Articles 48A and 51A(g), added in 1976, and Article 21 as interpreted by courts form the constitutional base.
  • The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is the umbrella law under which most rules are made.
  • Judicial doctrines (polluter pays, precautionary principle, absolute liability) fill gaps; the 2023 forest law amendment is contested.

Why it matters for UPSC: GS III often asks about the adequacy of environmental laws, and prelims tests years, articles and doctrines.

Practice question: How has the judiciary strengthened environmental protection in India beyond what statutes provide?
