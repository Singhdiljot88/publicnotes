Foundational values for the civil service

Note 274 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The syllabus lists aptitude and foundational values for civil service: integrity, impartiality and non-partisanship, objectivity, dedication to public service, and empathy, tolerance and compassion towards the weaker sections. Aptitude is the capacity to learn and perform a role well; values decide whether that capacity is used for the public good.

Integrity means consistency between one's values, words and actions, and being honest when no one is watching. It covers financial honesty but also intellectual honesty, such as giving frank advice. Impartiality means treating everyone fairly without favour or prejudice, and non-partisanship means serving any elected government faithfully without political bias, while refusing to act illegally for any party. Objectivity means taking decisions on evidence and merit, not on personal preference or pressure.

Dedication to public service means putting the public interest above personal comfort or career, and persisting in difficult postings. Empathy is the ability to understand another person's situation and feelings; compassion adds the desire to help; tolerance means respecting differences of belief and way of life. These matter most for the weaker sections, who depend most on the state and have the least power to complain.

Britain's Committee on Standards in Public Life, chaired by Lord Nolan, set out seven principles of public life in 1995: selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty and leadership. India's Second Administrative Reforms Commission drew on them in its report on ethics in governance and recommended a code of ethics for civil servants. A draft Public Services Bill in India also proposed listing core values.

These values can conflict. Compassion may tempt an officer to bend a rule; impartiality may seem cold. Good judgement lies in balancing them within the law.

Key takeaways

  • Integrity, impartiality, non-partisanship, objectivity, dedication, empathy, tolerance and compassion are the syllabus values.
  • The Nolan Committee's seven principles (1995) are a widely cited model.
  • Values can conflict, and judgement is needed to balance them lawfully.

Why it matters for UPSC: These values are named in the syllabus and underpin almost every case study answer.

Practice question: Can a civil servant be both non-partisan and committed to the policies of the elected government? Discuss.
