The Ahom kingdom and the North-East

Note 020 of 300 · 1 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The Ahoms ruled the Brahmaputra valley in Assam for about six hundred years, from 1228, when their founder Sukaphaa (Chaolung Siu-Ka-Pha) arrived from the Shan region of present-day Myanmar, until the Burmese invasions and British annexation in the 1820s. This is one of the longest continuous dynasties in Indian history.

The Ahoms developed a distinctive administration. The paik system required every adult male to provide labour or military service to the state in turns, in return for land. This allowed the kingdom to raise armies and build roads, embankments and tanks without a large cash economy. The Ahoms also kept detailed court chronicles called buranjis, first in the Tai Ahom language and later in Assamese, a rare tradition of historical writing in medieval India.

The Ahoms are remembered for resisting Mughal expansion. In 1671, at the Battle of Saraighat on the Brahmaputra near Guwahati, the Ahom general Lachit Borphukan defeated a much larger Mughal force using river warfare and fortifications. The National Defence Academy awards the Lachit Borphukan gold medal to its best cadet.

Ahom royalty were buried in mound burials called moidams at Charaideo, their first capital. The Moidams were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024, the first cultural site in the North-East to be listed. Over time the Ahoms adopted Hinduism and the Assamese language, blending Tai and local traditions.

Key takeaways

  • The Ahoms ruled Assam from 1228 to the 1820s.
  • The paik labour system and buranji chronicles were distinctive features.
  • Lachit Borphukan's victory at Saraighat (1671) stopped Mughal expansion; the Charaideo Moidams are a UNESCO site.

Why it matters for UPSC: North-East history is increasingly tested, and the Moidams link medieval history to recent heritage news.

Practice question: How did geography shape the military and administrative systems of the Ahom kingdom?
