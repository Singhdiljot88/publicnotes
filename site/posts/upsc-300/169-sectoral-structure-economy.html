The structure of India's economy: agriculture, industry and services

Note 169 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Economies are usually divided into three sectors. The primary sector extracts natural resources (agriculture, forestry, fishing and mining). The secondary sector processes them (manufacturing, construction, electricity and water supply). The tertiary sector provides services (trade, transport, communication, finance, real estate, public administration and others). Some analysts add a quaternary sector for knowledge-based services.

The classic pattern of development, described by economists such as Colin Clark and Simon Kuznets, is that as countries grow richer, workers and output shift first from agriculture to industry, especially manufacturing, and later to services. India has followed an unusual path. The share of agriculture and allied activities in output has fallen to roughly a sixth of GVA, but it still employs around 45% of the workforce, according to recent PLFS data, which means farm incomes per worker are low. Manufacturing has stayed at around 15–17% of GVA for decades, far below East Asian economies at a comparable stage, and has not absorbed large numbers of workers. Services have grown fastest and now produce more than half of GVA, led by information technology, finance, trade and telecommunications.

Economists call this "premature deindustrialisation" or a services-led growth model. It has advantages: India is a major exporter of software and business services. But services that employ many people, such as retail and transport, are often informal and low-paid, while high-productivity services need skills that most workers lack. Construction has absorbed many workers leaving agriculture, but often in insecure jobs.

Government policy has tried to raise manufacturing through Make in India (2014), production-linked incentive (PLI) schemes for sectors such as electronics, pharmaceuticals and automobiles, industrial corridors and the National Manufacturing Mission announced in the 2025-26 Budget.

Key takeaways

  • Agriculture's share of output has fallen to around a sixth, but it still employs about 45% of workers.
  • Manufacturing has remained around 15–17% of GVA; services produce over half.
  • India's services-led path raises concerns about premature deindustrialisation and job quality.

Why it matters for UPSC: Structural change and the manufacturing gap are frequent GS III Mains questions.

Practice question: Why has manufacturing not become India's main source of jobs? Suggest a strategy to change this.
