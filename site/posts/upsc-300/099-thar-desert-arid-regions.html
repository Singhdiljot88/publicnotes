The Thar desert and India's arid regions

Note 099 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The Thar, or Great Indian Desert, lies in western Rajasthan and extends into Gujarat, Haryana, Punjab and Pakistan. Rainfall is usually below 25 cm a year in its western parts, and temperatures can exceed 45°C in summer. The landscape includes sand dunes (including crescent-shaped barchans), rocky plains, salt lakes such as Sambhar, and seasonal streams that disappear into the sand; the Luni is the only significant river. Much of the region lies west of the Aravallis, which run parallel to the monsoon winds and so do little to stop them, one reason for the low rainfall.

Despite harsh conditions, the Thar is one of the most densely populated deserts in the world. Traditional communities developed sophisticated water harvesting systems: kunds (covered underground tanks), johads (small earthen check dams), stepwells (baolis) and khadins (runoff farming). Livestock, including camels, sheep and goats, is central to the economy. The Bishnoi community is known for protecting trees and blackbuck.

The Indira Gandhi Canal, one of the world's longest irrigation canals, brings water from the Harike barrage in Punjab into western Rajasthan, transforming parts of the desert into farmland. But it has also brought waterlogging and salinity in some areas and changed the desert ecosystem.

The desert is home to unique wildlife, including the Great Indian Bustard, one of the world's most endangered birds; fewer than 150 survive in the wild, mainly in Rajasthan. Overhead power lines kill many, and the Supreme Court has heard cases on burying transmission lines in its habitat, which overlaps with major solar and wind energy projects. Desertification and land degradation affect nearly 30% of India's land area, according to ISRO's Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas.

Key takeaways

  • The Thar receives very low rainfall partly because the Aravallis run parallel to the monsoon winds.
  • Traditional water harvesting and the Indira Gandhi Canal sustain people in the desert.
  • The Great Indian Bustard is critically endangered; renewable energy projects overlap its habitat.

Why it matters for UPSC: Arid regions, desertification and the bustard-renewables conflict appear in geography and environment questions.

Practice question: How can renewable energy expansion in Rajasthan be reconciled with the conservation of the Great Indian Bustard?
