Family, marriage and ageing in a changing India

Note 080 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

The family has been central to Indian society, and the joint family, in which several generations live together and share property and resources, was long seen as typical. In reality, sociologists note that nuclear families have always been common, especially among poorer households. Urbanisation, migration for work, smaller family sizes and changing aspirations have increased the share of nuclear households, though many keep strong ties with the wider family and live in "functionally joint" arrangements.

Marriage is also changing. The legal age of marriage is 18 for women and 21 for men; a bill to raise women's age to 21 was introduced in 2021 but lapsed. According to NFHS-5 (2019–21), 23.3% of women aged 20–24 were married before 18, down from 47.4% in NFHS-3 (2005–06). Arranged marriage remains the norm, but individual choice within it has grown, and "love-cum-arranged" marriages are increasingly common. Divorce rates are rising from a low base. The Supreme Court has strengthened individual rights within the family, for example by recognising women's equal coparcenary rights in ancestral property (Vineeta Sharma, 2020) and striking down instant triple talaq (Shayara Bano, 2017).

Population ageing is a new challenge. There were about 13.8 crore people aged 60 and above in 2021, around 10% of the population, and this is projected to rise sharply. The Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) shows high rates of chronic disease, low pension coverage and many elderly people, especially widows, living alone or depending on children. The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 makes children legally responsible for supporting parents.

Key takeaways

  • Nuclear households are growing, but many families remain functionally joint.
  • Child marriage fell from 47.4% (NFHS-3) to 23.3% (NFHS-5) of women aged 20–24.
  • India's elderly population is growing fast, with weak pension and care systems.

Why it matters for UPSC: Changes in family and marriage, and population ageing, are frequent GS I society questions.

Practice question: How is the changing structure of the Indian family affecting the care of the elderly? Suggest policy responses.
