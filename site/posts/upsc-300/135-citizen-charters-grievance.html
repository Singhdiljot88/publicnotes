Citizens' charters, grievance redress and social audit

Note 135 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Good governance requires that citizens know what services they are entitled to, and have a way to complain when services fail. Citizens' charters, introduced in India in 1997 on the model of Britain's 1991 Citizen's Charter, are public statements by government departments of the services they provide, their standards and timelines, and how to seek redress. In practice, many charters were drafted without consulting citizens, were not publicised and had no consequences for failure.

The Sevottam model, developed by the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances, sought to improve service quality through charters, grievance redress and capacity building. The Centralised Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System (CPGRAMS) is an online platform where citizens can lodge complaints against any central ministry; it handles lakhs of grievances each year. Several states have passed Right to Services laws, starting with Madhya Pradesh in 2010, that make specified services time-bound and impose penalties on officials who delay them.

Social audit goes further, involving the community in checking government spending against what was delivered on the ground. It was made mandatory for MGNREGA works under Section 17 of the Act, and the Supreme Court has directed it for other schemes. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana built strong independent social audit units, and Meghalaya passed the first state law on social audit in 2017. Done well, social audits have recovered misused funds and deterred corruption; done poorly, they become a formality.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission recommended that charters be made enforceable, linked to grievance redress and reviewed regularly. A Citizens' Charter and Grievance Redressal Bill was introduced in 2011 but lapsed.

Key takeaways

  • Citizens' charters (since 1997) set service standards, but have often lacked enforcement.
  • CPGRAMS and state Right to Services laws give citizens channels for complaints and time-bound services.
  • Social audit, mandatory under MGNREGA, involves communities in checking public spending.

Why it matters for UPSC: Charters, grievance redress and social audit are standard GS II governance topics.

Practice question: How can social audits be made more effective in ensuring accountability in welfare schemes?
