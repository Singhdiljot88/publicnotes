The French Revolution

Note 063 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In 1789 France was an absolute monarchy under Louis XVI, deeply in debt partly because of its support for the American Revolution. Society was divided into three estates: the clergy, the nobility and everyone else. The third estate, the vast majority, bore most of the tax burden while the first two enjoyed privileges.

To solve the financial crisis, the king summoned the Estates-General in May 1789. The third estate declared itself the National Assembly and took the Tennis Court Oath not to disband until France had a constitution. On 14 July 1789 crowds stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison in Paris, now celebrated as France's national day. In August, the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed liberty, equality before the law and popular sovereignty.

The revolution grew more radical. France became a republic in 1792, and Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. Under the Jacobins, led by Maximilien Robespierre, the Reign of Terror (1793–94) saw thousands executed as enemies of the revolution. After Robespierre's fall, a more conservative Directory governed until Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in 1799. Napoleon crowned himself emperor in 1804, spread revolutionary legal reforms through the Napoleonic Code, and conquered much of Europe before his final defeat at Waterloo in 1815.

The revolution's slogan, "Liberty, equality, fraternity", spread across the world. It established the idea that sovereignty belongs to the nation, encouraged nationalism and inspired later democratic movements. The words liberty, equality and fraternity appear in the Preamble of India's Constitution.

Key takeaways

  • France's financial crisis and unequal estates system led to revolution in 1789.
  • The storming of the Bastille and the Declaration of the Rights of Man defined the revolution's ideals.
  • The revolution turned radical, then gave way to Napoleon, but its ideas spread worldwide.

Why it matters for UPSC: The French Revolution is central to world history questions and to the origins of the Preamble's ideals.

Practice question: How did the French Revolution contribute to the spread of nationalism and democracy in the nineteenth century?
