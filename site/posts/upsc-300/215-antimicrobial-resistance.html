Antimicrobial resistance: the silent pandemic

Note 215 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) happens when bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites evolve to survive the medicines meant to kill them. Infections then become harder or impossible to treat. Bacteria that resist many antibiotics are called "superbugs". A study published in The Lancet estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019 and contributed to nearly 5 million. The World Health Organization lists AMR among the top global public health threats.

AMR develops naturally, but human behaviour speeds it up. Major causes include overuse and misuse of antibiotics in people, often for viral infections such as colds where they do not work; easy over-the-counter sale without prescriptions; patients not completing courses; heavy use of antibiotics in poultry, livestock and fish farming to promote growth; poor infection control in hospitals; and pharmaceutical waste released into rivers. India is one of the world's largest consumers of antibiotics, and high resistance has been reported in common infections. The NDM-1 enzyme, which makes bacteria resistant to powerful carbapenem antibiotics, was first identified in a patient who had been treated in India.

India's response includes the National Action Plan on AMR (2017), based on the One Health approach linking human, animal and environmental health; Schedule H1 of the drug rules, which requires stricter record-keeping for the sale of certain antibiotics; the "Red Line" campaign to mark prescription-only antibiotics; a ban on colistin as a growth promoter in animal feed from 2019; and surveillance networks run by the Indian Council of Medical Research. Kerala was the first state to adopt its own AMR action plan, in 2018.

Few new antibiotics are being developed because they are less profitable for drug companies, so preserving existing ones matters greatly.

Key takeaways

  • AMR directly caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019.
  • Misuse in humans and animals, over-the-counter sales and pharmaceutical waste drive AMR.
  • India's National Action Plan (2017) uses a One Health approach; Kerala adopted the first state plan.

Why it matters for UPSC: AMR is a frequent GS II and GS III topic, linking health, agriculture and the environment.

Practice question: Why is antimicrobial resistance described as a "One Health" problem? What should India do about it?
