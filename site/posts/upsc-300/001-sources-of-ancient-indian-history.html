How we know about ancient India: the sources

Note 001 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Historians build the story of ancient India from two kinds of evidence. Literary sources include religious texts such as the Vedas, the epics and the Puranas, Buddhist texts such as the Jatakas and the Pali canon, Jain texts, and secular works such as Kautilya's Arthashastra, Panini's grammar and the poetry of the Sangam collections in Tamil. These texts were often composed and edited over centuries, so they are read for what they reveal about ideas and society, not as exact chronicles.

Archaeological sources are harder evidence. Excavated cities, pottery styles, seals, coins and monuments help date settlements and trace trade. Inscriptions are especially valuable because they are fixed in time and place. Ashoka's edicts, written mostly in Prakrit in the Brahmi script, were deciphered by James Prinsep in 1837 and opened up Mauryan history. Coins of the Indo-Greeks, Kushanas and Guptas reveal rulers' names, titles, religious symbols and the reach of trade.

Foreign accounts add an outside view. The Greek envoy Megasthenes described Chandragupta Maurya's court in Indica, which survives only in quotations by later writers. The Chinese pilgrims Faxian (Fa-Hien) in the Gupta period and Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang) in Harsha's time described monasteries, cities and customs. Each source has a bias: a royal inscription praises the king, a pilgrim notices what interests a pilgrim.

Good history cross-checks these sources against one another, which is exactly the habit UPSC answers reward.

Key takeaways

  • Literary sources show ideas and society; archaeology and inscriptions give firmer dates and places.
  • Prinsep's decipherment of Brahmi in 1837 unlocked Ashoka's edicts.
  • Foreign travellers such as Megasthenes, Faxian and Xuanzang are useful but partial witnesses.

Why it matters for UPSC: Prelims regularly tests which source belongs to which period, and Mains asks how historians reconstruct the past.

Practice question: Why are inscriptions and coins often considered more reliable than literary texts for writing ancient Indian history? Discuss with examples.
