Heatwaves: India's silent disaster

Note 259 of 300 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

Heatwaves are periods of abnormally high temperatures. The India Meteorological Department declares a heatwave in the plains when the maximum temperature reaches at least 40°C and is at least 4.5°C above normal, or when it reaches 45°C regardless of normal; in coastal areas and hills, lower thresholds apply. Heatwaves usually occur from March to June, mainly over north-western, central and eastern India, and they have become more frequent, longer and more intense with climate change. The summer of 2024 saw prolonged heat across north India, with temperatures near 50°C in parts of Rajasthan and Delhi.

Extreme heat is dangerous to health. It can cause heat exhaustion and heatstroke, and worsens heart, kidney and lung disease. The most vulnerable are outdoor workers, such as construction labourers, farm workers, street vendors and delivery workers, along with the elderly, young children, pregnant women and people in poorly ventilated homes. High humidity makes heat more dangerous because sweat cannot evaporate to cool the body; scientists measure this through the "wet-bulb" temperature. Heat also reduces labour productivity, damages crops such as wheat, strains electricity grids and dries water sources.

Heat deaths are under-counted, because many are recorded as heart attacks or other causes. Heatwaves were not among the disasters notified for central relief funding, though states can use part of their disaster funds for them.

Ahmedabad prepared India's first Heat Action Plan in 2013, after a 2010 heatwave killed over a thousand people. Its measures include early warnings, opening cooling centres, changing working hours, training health workers and painting roofs white to reflect sunlight (cool roofs). Many cities and states have since prepared plans, guided by NDMA guidelines. Studies have found that many plans lack funding, legal backing and attention to the most vulnerable, and that they focus on dry heat while ignoring humidity.

Key takeaways

  • IMD declares a heatwave at 40°C and 4.5°C above normal, or at 45°C, in the plains.
  • Outdoor workers, the elderly and the poor are most at risk; heat deaths are under-counted.
  • Ahmedabad's 2013 Heat Action Plan was the first; many later plans lack funding and legal backing.

Why it matters for UPSC: Heatwaves are a fast-rising GS III topic linking climate change, health, labour and disaster management.

Practice question: Should heatwaves be notified as a disaster under the Disaster Management Act? Discuss.
