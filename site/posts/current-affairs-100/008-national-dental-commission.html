The National Dental Commission replaces the Dental Council

Note 008 of 100 · 2 min read · Fact-checked 2 November 2026

In March 2026 the Union government brought into force a new framework for regulating dentistry. The National Dental Commission (NDC) replaced the Dental Council of India (DCI), which had regulated the profession since the Dentists Act of 1948. The 1948 Act has been repealed. The new framework took effect on 19 March 2026, under the National Dental Commission Act, 2023, which Parliament passed alongside a similar law for nursing and midwifery.

The change follows the model of the National Medical Commission, which replaced the Medical Council of India in 2020 after years of criticism of corruption and weak oversight in medical education. Like the NMC, the NDC works through three autonomous boards: an Undergraduate and Postgraduate Dental Education Board for courses and curriculum; a Dental Assessment and Rating Board for assessing and rating institutions; and an Ethics and Dental Registration Board for professional conduct and the national register of dentists. Dr Sanjay Tewari was appointed its first chairperson.

The commission will set standards for dental education, assess colleges, promote research and community dental care, and issue guidelines for fees in private dental colleges. The government said the reform would make regulation more transparent, quality-focused and accountable, and improve access to affordable dental care.

India has a large number of dental colleges, many of them private, and has faced a paradox of unemployed dental graduates in cities alongside poor access to dental care in rural areas. Critics of the new model, as with the NMC, say that replacing elected councils with government-appointed bodies reduces the profession's autonomy.

Key takeaways

  • The National Dental Commission replaced the Dental Council of India from 19 March 2026.
  • It works through three autonomous boards for education, assessment and ethics and registration.
  • It follows the National Medical Commission model, raising similar debates on autonomy.

Why it matters for UPSC: Regulatory reform of health professions is a GS II governance and health topic.

Discussion question: Have commission-based regulators improved the quality of professional education in health? Discuss.
