India Notes

Ancient India — to about 1200 CE

From the Harappan cities to the early medieval kingdoms, with traditional dates flagged.

The timeline

Date / periodEvent / person / milestone — why it matters
c. 2600–1900 BCEIndus Valley / Harappan civilisation — planned cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, drainage, standard weights, trade with Mesopotamia.
Shows urban planning and trade long before later kingdoms; its script remains undeciphered, so claims about it must stay cautious.
c. 1500–500 BCE *Vedic period — composition of the Rigveda and later Vedic texts, pastoral life giving way to settled agriculture and kingdoms.
The earliest textual window on early India; oral transmission means dates are approximate, not fixed.
c. 6th century BCEMahavira and the Buddha — Jain and Buddhist teachings challenge ritual-centred religion and stress conduct, non-violence and liberation.
Two traditions that reshape Indian ethics, art and philosophy, and spread far beyond India.
c. 563–483 BCE *Buddha (traditional dates vary).
Traditional dates differ by tradition; use them as traditional, not as an exact birth/death record.
c. 326 BCEAlexander's campaign reaches the Indus; Porus (Puru) resists at the Hydaspes (Jhelum).
Opens sustained contact between India and the Hellenistic world, without establishing lasting rule in India.
c. 321–185 BCEMauryan Empire — Chandragupta, Bindusara, Ashoka; Ashoka's edicts after Kalinga promote dhamma, tolerance and public welfare.
India's first large empire and the earliest readable royal inscriptions; Ashoka's edicts are a primary source, not legend.
c. 185 BCE–320 CEPost-Mauryan centuries — Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas (Kanishka, c. 127–150 CE); Gandhara art blends Greek and Indian styles.
Trade and art link India to Central Asia, China and Rome; a reminder that 'Indian' culture was always connected.
c. 320–550 CEGupta period — Chandragupta I, Samudragupta, Chandragupta II; Kalidasa's literature, Aryabhata's astronomy (499 CE).
A high point in literature, science and art — but 'golden age' should be weighed against inequality and regional variation.
c. 606–647 CEHarsha of Kannauj; the Chinese traveller Xuanzang visits India (c. 630s).
A traveller's account gives an outside check on Indian courts, monasteries and trade.
c. 8th–12th centuriesChola power in the south — Rajaraja I and Rajendra I, Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur, naval reach to Southeast Asia.
South Indian temple, irrigation and maritime history is central, not a footnote to the north.

Key takeaways

  • India's earliest cities were planned and trading, not isolated.
  • Religion, philosophy and statecraft change together, not in separate boxes.
  • Where a date is traditional, treat it as approximate — inscriptions and archaeology are the firmer anchors.

Why it matters: It gives the long roots — cities, texts, empires and ideas — that later periods keep returning to.

Discussion / revision question: Which tells us more about ancient India, a royal inscription or a later story about a king — and why?
