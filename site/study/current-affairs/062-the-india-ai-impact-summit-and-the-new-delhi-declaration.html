The India AI Impact Summit and the New Delhi Declaration

Science · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

From 16 to 20 February 2026, New Delhi hosted the India AI Impact Summit at Bharat Mandapam, the first summit in this series to be hosted by a country of the Global South. Delegations came from more than 100 countries, and the summit drew heads of state, ministers, and leaders of major technology companies. Its headline outcome was the New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact, initially endorsed by 89 countries and international organisations, including the United States and China, with the number later reported as rising to 91 as further signatories joined. The declaration is non-binding, but it was presented as the broadest multilateral consensus on artificial intelligence to date.

The declaration is organised around seven pillars, described as chakras: democratising AI resources, economic growth and social development, safe and trusted AI, AI for science, social empowerment, human capital, and resilient systems. Its guiding principle, in the Indian formulation, was “Sarvajan Hitaya, Sarvajan Sukhaya”, welfare for all and happiness for all, alongside the older idea of Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam, the world as one family. In practical terms, the summit launched several voluntary platforms: a Charter for the Democratic Diffusion of AI, a Global AI Impact Commons for sharing successful AI use cases, a Trusted AI Commons of benchmarks and safety tools, and an AI workforce development playbook.

India also used the summit to advance its own position. The government announced an expansion of public computing capacity, adding 20,000 graphics processing units to an existing 38,000, with a stated aim of crossing 100,000 by the end of 2026. It reported investment commitments of more than 250 billion dollars in infrastructure, alongside about 20 billion dollars for deep-tech venture capital, and it joined the United States-led Pax Silica initiative on semiconductor and critical-mineral supply chains. Company announcements included a partnership between OpenAI and the Tata Group and an agreement between Anthropic and Infosys.

The honest caveat matters as much as the headline. A non-binding declaration does not settle the hard questions: who pays for compute, who sets safety standards, and what happens when national rules conflict. Critics of such summits argue that voluntary outcomes are less durable than law. The test of New Delhi will therefore be follow-through, in national regulation, in the commons platforms, and in whether the promised compute and investment actually arrive.

Key takeaways

  • The New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact, endorsed by 89 countries and organisations at adoption, is built on seven pillars and is non-binding.
  • India framed the summit around democratic diffusion of AI: wider access to resources, tools, and skills, not only frontier-model development.
  • Announced commitments included 20,000 additional GPUs, large infrastructure pledges, and new company partnerships, all of which still need delivery.

Why it matters for civil studies

AI governance is becoming a subject of summit diplomacy like trade or climate, and declarations now shape the standards and supply chains that domestic regulation must work with.

Think it through

Is a broad but non-binding declaration better than a narrow binding agreement that fewer countries sign?
