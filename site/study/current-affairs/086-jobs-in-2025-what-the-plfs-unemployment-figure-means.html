Jobs in 2025: what the PLFS unemployment figure means

Society · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; role of women, population, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

In March 2026, the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation published the Periodic Labour Force Survey for the calendar year 2025. It found overall unemployment among those aged 15 and above at 3.1 per cent. That single figure is widely quoted, and widely misunderstood, so it is worth unpacking what it does and does not say. The PLFS is India's official household survey of employment, and it measures whether people who are in the labour force, that is, working or seeking work, found work in a reference period. A low unemployment rate in a country where most people cannot afford to be without work does not mean good jobs are plentiful.

The distinction that matters is between unemployment, informality, and underemployment. A person selling vegetables for a few hours a day counts as employed, as does a graduate doing unpaid family work or a delivery rider between orders. India's labour market is dominated by informal work, self-employment, and agriculture, so the headline unemployment rate mainly captures people who are actively seeking work and not finding any, not people in low-paid, insecure, or part-time work who want more. Youth unemployment, women's labour force participation, and the quality of work are therefore more informative than the headline rate, and they move differently from it.

The PLFS figure also sits beside the gig-economy numbers of the same year. Nearly one crore workers were engaged in gig and platform work as of May 2026, and the new labour codes now define those workers in law. Platform work shows up in the PLFS as employment, but it rarely carries the protections of a formal job: no paid leave, no employer pension, no guaranteed hours. Reading the two sources together gives a fuller picture than either alone: low measured unemployment alongside a large, growing workforce outside traditional employment protections.

For policy, the implication is to ask better questions of the data. What share of workers are in formal employment with social security? What do young graduates actually earn? How many women have left the labour force, and why? The PLFS can answer some of these when its detailed tables are used, but the headline rate alone cannot, and any debate that treats 3.1 per cent as a verdict on the jobs market is missing the structure of Indian work.

Key takeaways

  • PLFS calendar-year 2025, published March 2026, put unemployment among those aged 15 and above at 3.1 per cent.
  • The headline rate measures people seeking work and not finding it, not informality, underemployment, or job quality.
  • Low unemployment alongside nearly one crore gig and platform workers shows a labour market changing form faster than its protections.

Why it matters for civil studies

Labour statistics are among the most misread numbers in public debate, and knowing what a survey measures is a prerequisite to judging employment policy.

Think it through

If unemployment is low but most new work is informal, should policy aim to create more jobs, or to make existing work more secure?
