Artemis II: humans return to the Moon

Science · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

On 1 April 2026, at 6:35 p.m. Eastern time, NASA's Artemis II lifted off from Launch Pad 39B at Kennedy Space Center in Florida, carrying the first humans to travel to the Moon since Apollo 17 in 1972. The crew was commander Reid Wiseman, pilot Victor Glover, and mission specialist Christina Koch of NASA, together with mission specialist Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. Their Orion capsule was named Integrity, and it rode the Space Launch System rocket on a free-return loop around the Moon. The crew flew around the far side on 6 April and splashed down in the Pacific off San Diego on 10 April, after a flight of 9 days, 1 hour and 32 minutes.

The mission set a distance record. At its farthest, the crew reached 252,756 miles from Earth, beating the record held by Apollo 13 since 1970, and NASA reported a total distance flown of about 695,081 miles. Artemis II did not land on the Moon: it passed within about 4,067 miles of the lunar surface. Its purpose was to prove the combined system, rocket, capsule, heat shield, and life support, with people aboard, before any landing is attempted. The heat shield was a particular concern after the Artemis I uncrewed flight, and post-mission reporting said inspections found far fewer char-loss spots than on Artemis I.

The science followed months later. On 7 October 2026 NASA released more than 800 gigabytes of Artemis II data to its Planetary Data System, including over 11,000 images and 8.5 hours of crew audio, alongside a report NASA itself called preliminary. The crew reported five faint impact flashes during the flyby, and the observations are meant to inform camera choices and imaging plans for future surface missions, especially around the lunar south pole, where lighting is extreme.

What comes next is now reshaped. NASA announced in October 2026 that Artemis III is targeted for summer 2027 as a low-Earth-orbit test mission docking with lander test vehicles, rather than as the landing it was once planned to be, and that a crewed landing is aimed at 2028 on Artemis IV. All of these dates are targets, not promises, and NASA has said schedule pressure does not change the laws of physics.

Key takeaways

  • Artemis II (1–10 April 2026) was the first crewed lunar flight since 1972, a flyby that set a human distance record of 252,756 miles from Earth.
  • The mission tested the SLS rocket, Orion capsule, heat shield, and life support with four astronauts aboard, without landing.
  • NASA's October 2026 data release and revised plan put Artemis III in 2027 as an orbit test and a landing attempt in 2028, both as targets.

Why it matters for civil studies

Mega-programmes like Artemis show how states manage risk, cost, and international partnership when the payoff is years away and failure is public.

Think it through

Should a crewed lunar programme be judged by its schedule, its science, or its ability to survive political and budget changes?
