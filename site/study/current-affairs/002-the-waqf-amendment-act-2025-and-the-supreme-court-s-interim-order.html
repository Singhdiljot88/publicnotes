Current affairs explainers · Note 002

The Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025, and the Supreme Court's interim order

Polity and courts · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution; separation of powers, dispute redressal mechanisms and institutions; government policies and interventions; statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

A waqf is a permanent dedication of property for religious or charitable purposes under Islamic law. In India, waqfs have long been administered under statute, most recently the Waqf Act, 1995, with Central and State Waqf Boards and tribunals to deal with disputes. Parliament passed the Waqf (Amendment) Bill in April 2025, and the President assented on 5 April 2025, bringing a package of changes to how waqf property is identified, registered, and managed.

Among the changes were a requirement that a person must have practised Islam for five years before creating a waqf, a new process under which a designated government officer could inquire whether property claimed as waqf was in fact government land, and changes to the composition of the Central Waqf Council and State Waqf Boards, including provision for non-Muslim members. Supporters in government presented the changes as measures to improve transparency, prevent encroachment, and bring clearer records to waqf administration.

Petitioners challenged the Act in the Supreme Court, arguing that parts of it interfered with religious freedom and with the management of religious endowments. The Court heard the matter in April and May 2025, during which the Centre assured the Court that waqf by user and waqf by deed properties would not be denotified while the proceedings were pending, and then reserved its order on interim relief.

On 15 September 2025, a Bench led by then Chief Justice B. R. Gavai, with Justice Augustine George Masih, declined to stay the Act as a whole, holding that legislation carries a presumption of constitutionality and that a wholesale stay is granted only in the rarest of cases. It did, however, stay the operation of the five-year practising requirement until rules are framed for how it is to be verified, and it stayed the provision allowing a government officer to decide whether waqf property encroached on government land. It also capped non-Muslim membership at four in the Central Waqf Council and three in State Waqf Boards.

The order was interim, not final. Its observations were prima facie, meaning they were provisional views pending a full hearing, and the Court left petitioners free to press other grounds. The case therefore illustrates an important distinction for civil studies readers: an interim order can freeze or adjust the most contested parts of a law while leaving the rest in operation, without deciding the law's ultimate validity.

Key takeaways

  • Parliament passed the Waqf (Amendment) Act in April 2025, amending the 1995 Act on registration, management, and board composition.
  • On 15 September 2025 the Supreme Court refused a wholesale stay but stayed the five-year practising requirement and the officer-led inquiry into government land.
  • The interim order capped non-Muslim membership of the Central Council at four and of State Boards at three, pending a final hearing.

Why it matters for civil studies

Interim orders show how courts balance a legislature's authority against claims of rights, without deciding a case before it is fully heard.

Think it through

When should a court stay part of a law before a final hearing, and when should it let the law operate in full?
