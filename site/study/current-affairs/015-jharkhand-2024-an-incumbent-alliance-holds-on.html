Jharkhand 2024: an incumbent alliance holds on

Elections · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Salient features of the Representation of People's Act; appointment to various Constitutional posts, powers, functions and responsibilities of various Constitutional Bodies.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

Jharkhand voted in two phases on 13 and 20 November 2024 for all 81 seats in its Legislative Assembly, with counting in November 2024 alongside Maharashtra. The contest was between the incumbent Mahagathbandhan, led by the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha, and the National Democratic Alliance, led by the Bharatiya Janata Party. The election was watched closely because incumbents in several Hindi-belt states had struggled to retain power, and because Jharkhand's politics turns on tribal identity, welfare delivery, and land and mining questions as much as on national issues.

The Mahagathbandhan won 56 seats, a clear majority in an 81-member House where 41 seats are needed to govern. The Jharkhand Mukti Morcha itself won 34 seats, its best-ever result, while the Congress won 16, the Rashtriya Janata Dal four, and the Communist Party of India (Marxist-Leninist) Liberation two. The BJP won 21 seats, and the NDA as a whole won 24, well short of a majority despite the BJP securing the largest single-party vote share, at 33.18 per cent.

Hemant Soren, the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha leader, continued as Chief Minister. His return to office was notable because he had resigned earlier in 2024 after his arrest by the Enforcement Directorate in a land case, and had returned as Chief Minister after being granted bail. The election result therefore also functioned as a public judgment on a period in which the state's politics had been dominated by investigation, arrest, and legal proceedings, as well as by governance.

The result contrasted with Maharashtra, counted in the same week, where the incumbent alliance won by a landslide, and with Haryana, where the incumbent party also retained power. Taken together, the three results in late 2024 suggested that there was no single national swing, and that state-level leadership, welfare schemes, and tribal and regional identity continued to shape outcomes in ways that national narratives alone could not explain.

Jharkhand also illustrated a familiar feature of Indian elections: the largest vote share does not always produce the largest seat share. The BJP's vote, though the largest for a single party, was spread across the state, while the Mahagathbandhan's combined vote translated into a larger seat total. That is not an anomaly; it is how first-past-the-post works.

Key takeaways

  • The Mahagathbandhan won 56 of 81 seats in Jharkhand in 2024, with the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha winning 34 and the Congress 16.
  • The BJP won 21 seats despite securing the largest single-party vote share, at 33.18 per cent.
  • Hemant Soren continued as Chief Minister after an election fought against a background of investigation and legal proceedings.

Why it matters for civil studies

Seat share follows where votes are concentrated, not just how many votes a party wins in total.

Think it through

Should a party with the largest vote share but fewer seats be seen as the real winner?
