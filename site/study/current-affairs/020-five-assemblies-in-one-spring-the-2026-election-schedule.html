Current affairs explainers · Note 020

Five assemblies in one spring: the 2026 election schedule

Elections · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Salient features of the Representation of People's Act; appointment to various Constitutional posts, powers, functions and responsibilities of various Constitutional Bodies.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

Assembly elections are due when a House completes its five-year term, unless it is dissolved earlier. In early 2026, five Houses were approaching the end of their terms at the same time: West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Assam, and the union territory of Puducherry. All five had last voted in 2021, and all five were due to vote again in the spring of 2026, making that round one of the largest state election exercises between general elections.

On 15 March 2026, the Election Commission announced the schedule. Polling was set for 9 April in Assam, Kerala, and Puducherry, for 23 April in Tamil Nadu, and in two phases, on 23 and 29 April, in West Bengal. Counting for all five Houses was scheduled for 4 May 2026. The terms of the outgoing Houses expired on different dates: West Bengal on 7 May, Tamil Nadu on 10 May, Assam on 20 May, Kerala on 23 May, and Puducherry on 15 June, so the schedule was designed to complete the process before those dates.

The scale was substantial. The five Houses together comprised 824 seats: 294 in West Bengal, 234 in Tamil Nadu, 140 in Kerala, 126 in Assam, and 30 in Puducherry. The electorate was reported at about 17.4 crore voters. The Commission also announced Model Code of Conduct directions and the deployment of flying squads and observers, as it does for every major election round, to monitor expenditure, inducement, and violations of the code.

The political stakes differed by state. West Bengal was a contest between the ruling Trinamool Congress and the BJP, with the Left and Congress also in the field. Tamil Nadu pitted the ruling Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam alliance against the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam alliance. Kerala, which has historically alternated between the Left Democratic Front and the United Democratic Front, was watched for whether that pattern would hold. Assam and Puducherry were contests in which the ruling National Democratic Alliance sought to retain power.

This note covers the schedule, not the results, which were due on 4 May 2026 and should be read separately. Its teaching value is in the logistics: a single Commission, working to five different term-end dates, must sequence polling, counting, and government formation so that no state is left without a House when its term expires, and so that the Model Code does not run longer than necessary.

Key takeaways

  • The Election Commission announced the 2026 schedule on 15 March 2026, with polling in April and counting on 4 May 2026.
  • The five Houses totalled 824 seats, with an electorate reported at about 17.4 crore voters.
  • Terms expired between 7 May and 15 June 2026, which set the deadline for completing the process in each state.

Why it matters for civil studies

Staggering polls across five Houses is a logistical exercise as much as a political one, governed by term-end dates.

Think it through

Should elections in several states be held together to save time and money, or does that nationalise what should be state contests?
