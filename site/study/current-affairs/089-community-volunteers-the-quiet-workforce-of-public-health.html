Community volunteers: the quiet workforce of public health

Society · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; role of women, population, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

Health programmes are usually described in budgets and hospital beds, but India's tuberculosis drive in 2026 showed a different workforce: volunteers. More than 7.16 lakh Ni-kshay Mitras, individual citizens and organisations, were providing nutritional and vocational support to TB patients, according to the Health Ministry, and a government backgrounder of 13 October 2026 reported 5.7 lakh new Ni-kshay Mitras joining the initiative. Under the Nikshay Poshan Yojana, over 4,590 crore rupees had been transferred to 1.39 crore TB patients since 2018 as nutritional support. The idea is simple and medically sound: TB treatment fails when patients are hungry, stigmatised, or alone, so food and company are part of the cure.

The volunteer model expanded in October 2026 with JOSH, Joint Squad for Health, teams launched in 55 districts across 11 states and union territories. Each team brings together five MY Bharat volunteers and five National Cadet Corps cadets to raise awareness of TB symptoms, encourage early diagnosis and treatment, support patients in completing treatment, and address stigma. The first phase covers 55 districts, with expansion planned across the country. The design deliberately pairs young volunteers with a state programme, so that awareness work is done by people patients actually meet, in their own neighbourhoods and colleges, rather than by posters alone.

The same logic appears in the panchayat certifications the programme reports. The government said in October 2026 that 67,933 panchayats across 697 districts were recognised as TB Mukt in 2026, a figure that should be read within the programme's certification framework: it recognises progress against programme criteria, not a permanent absence of TB risk. A separate October backgrounder reported 46,118 gram panchayats receiving TB-free certification, a different figure that appears to reflect a different counting point, and the discrepancy is a reminder to attribute such numbers to their source and date rather than treating them as a single settled fact.

The broader lesson is about the limits of the state. No health system can station a worker beside every patient for six months of treatment, but a community can. Volunteer programmes work when they are organised, supplied, and recognised, and they fail when they are treated as free labour without training or support. The Ni-kshay Mitra and JOSH models are an attempt to formalise that middle ground: citizens as partners of the state, not substitutes for it.

Key takeaways

  • Over 7.16 lakh Ni-kshay Mitras support TB patients with nutrition and vocational help, alongside 4,590 crore rupees of nutritional transfers since 2018.
  • Youth-led JOSH teams, pairing MY Bharat volunteers with NCC cadets, launched in 55 districts in October 2026 to fight stigma and support treatment completion.
  • TB-free panchayat counts differ between government releases (67,933 and 46,118), showing why certification figures must be attributed to source and date.

Why it matters for civil studies

Volunteer and community models are a form of state capacity, and their design, training, and recognition determine whether participation strengthens or substitutes for public services.

Think it through

Should volunteers in health programmes be paid, trained, and certified like workers, or does formalising them destroy what makes volunteering work?
