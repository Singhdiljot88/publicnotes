Fully literate states: six and counting

Society · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; role of women, population, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

In 2026, Sikkim and then Uttarakhand joined the list of fully literate states in India, taking the count to six. Sikkim was declared India's fifth fully literate state, with a literacy rate of 99.82 per cent, announced by Chief Minister Prem Singh Tamang during the convocation ceremony of Sikkim University in Gangtok in the presence of President Droupadi Murmu. Uttarakhand was then declared the sixth fully literate state in 2026, reporting an adult literacy rate of 98.70 per cent. The earlier states on the list were Mizoram, Goa, Tripura, and Himachal Pradesh, and Ladakh was the first union territory to achieve the status.

The definition matters, because “fully literate” here does not mean every person can read. Under the Ministry of Education's framework for the ULLAS programme, Understanding Lifelong Learning for All in Society, a state is considered fully literate when literacy among people aged 15 and above exceeds 95 per cent. That benchmark is treated as equivalent to full literacy, recognising that reaching the last few per cent, among the elderly, the remote, and the marginalised, is the hardest part. Mizoram became the first state to be declared fully literate under this framework, in May 2025, with 98.2 per cent literacy according to Periodic Labour Force Survey data for 2023-24. Goa followed with a reported 100 per cent functional literacy, Tripura with 95.6 per cent, and Himachal Pradesh with 99.3 per cent, announced on International Literacy Day, 8 September.

ULLAS, also known as the Nav Bharat Saaksharta Karyakram, is a centrally sponsored scheme running from 2022 to 2027, aligned with the National Education Policy 2020. It targets non-literate people above 15 years of age and covers foundational literacy and numeracy, digital literacy, financial literacy, critical life skills, and continuing education. Functional literacy under the programme means more than signing one's name: it means reading, writing, and comprehending information well enough to use it in daily life. The Education Minister has said India's literacy rate rose from 74 per cent in 2011 to 80.9 per cent in 2023-24, which shows both the progress and the distance still to travel nationally.

The caveat is that a state-level average can hide the people it misses. A state at 98 per cent still has tens of thousands of non-literate adults, often concentrated among older women, tribal communities, and migrant workers. The achievement is real, but the last mile is where literacy work becomes hardest, and where the census of 2027 will provide an independent check.

Key takeaways

  • Sikkim (99.82 per cent) and Uttarakhand (98.70 per cent) were declared fully literate in 2026, becoming the fifth and sixth states on the list.
  • “Fully literate” under ULLAS means over 95 per cent literacy among people aged 15 and above, a benchmark treated as equivalent to full literacy.
  • ULLAS (2022–2027) targets adults missed by formal schooling, covering foundational, digital, and financial literacy, not just reading and writing.

Why it matters for civil studies

Literacy is a gateway capability: without it, citizens cannot fully use welfare, banking, health, or legal rights, so adult literacy is a governance issue, not only an education one.

Think it through

Should a state celebrate 95 per cent literacy as “fully literate”, or does that label risk abandoning the last 5 per cent?
