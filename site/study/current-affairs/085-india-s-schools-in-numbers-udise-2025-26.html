India's schools in numbers: UDISE+ 2025-26

Society · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; role of women, population, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

In July 2026, the Ministry of Education published the UDISE+ report for 2025-26, the Unified District Information System for Education Plus, which counted more than 1.48 million schools, over 26 crore students, and 1.02 crore teachers. Those three numbers describe the largest school system in the world by enrolment, and they are the denominator for almost every other education statistic: pupil-teacher ratios, dropout rates, infrastructure gaps, and budgets per child all divide by these totals. A system this large cannot be reformed by a single scheme; it can only be steered, district by district.

The scale also explains why data systems matter. UDISE+ collects school-level information on enrolment, teachers, and infrastructure, and it feeds the NITI Aayog school education report of May 2026, which combined UDISE+ 2024-25 with ASER 2024 and the PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 to assess access, infrastructure, equity, digital integration, and learning outcomes across all 36 states and union territories. The combination is deliberate: UDISE+ counts who is in school, ASER tests what children learn at home, and PARAKH assesses competencies nationally. No single source can answer both the access question and the learning question.

The numbers also reveal a structural fact: with 1.02 crore teachers for 26 crore students, the national pupil-teacher ratio implied by the totals is roughly 25 to 1, but national averages hide the distribution. Averages conceal single-teacher schools, subject shortages in secondary grades, and the difference between a sanctioned post and a teacher actually present. That is why UDISE+ data is most useful when disaggregated by state, district, school level, and subject, and why reforms that look good nationally can fail locally.

For civil studies readers, the point is methodological. When a policy claims to improve education, ask which denominator it uses: enrolment, attendance, or learning. UDISE+ answers the first. ASER answers the third. Confusing the two is how a country can celebrate full enrolment while a quarter of its third-graders cannot read.

Key takeaways

  • UDISE+ 2025-26, published July 2026, counted over 1.48 million schools, 26 crore students, and 1.02 crore teachers.
  • UDISE+ measures access and inputs; ASER and PARAKH measure learning, and the two kinds of data must be read together.
  • National averages hide distribution: teacher shortages, single-teacher schools, and subject gaps only appear in disaggregated data.

Why it matters for civil studies

Administrative data systems like UDISE+ are the machinery of governance, and knowing what each dataset can and cannot prove is essential to evaluating policy claims.

Think it through

If enrolment is near-universal but learning is weak, should education budgets shift from building schools to training teachers, and how would you prove the shift worked?
