When a Governor sits on a Bill: the Tamil Nadu assent case

Polity and courts · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution; separation of powers, dispute redressal mechanisms and institutions; government policies and interventions; statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

Under Article 200 of the Constitution, when a Bill passed by a state legislature is presented to the Governor, the Governor may assent to it, withhold assent, or reserve it for the consideration of the President. If the legislature passes the Bill again after it has been returned, the Governor is expected to assent, unless the Bill is reserved for the President. The question that reached the Supreme Court was what happens when none of these steps is taken promptly, and Bills simply remain pending for years.

Tamil Nadu approached the Supreme Court in 2023, challenging the Governor's handling of a set of Bills passed by the state Assembly between 2020 and 2023. The state argued that the Governor, R. N. Ravi, had kept Bills pending indefinitely, then withheld assent to ten Bills without a message, and later, after the Assembly passed them again, reserved them for the President. The Court examined whether that course was consistent with Article 200 and with its earlier ruling in the Punjab Governor case.

On 8 April 2025, a two-judge Bench of Justices J. B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan held that the withholding of assent to the ten Bills was illegal and erroneous. Using its powers under Article 142, the Bench declared the ten Bills to have been assented on the date they were presented again to the Governor, 18 November 2023. It also laid down timelines for Governors and the President to act on Bills, including three months for a Governor to act in the first instance and one month where a Bill had been re-passed.

The story did not end there. Later in 2025, a larger Constitution Bench reconsidered the question and held that courts cannot impose mandatory timelines on the President or a Governor for granting assent, because the Constitution itself does not prescribe such timelines. It rejected the idea of deemed assent as a general mechanism. Readers should therefore treat the April 2025 timelines and deemed assent as the position taken in that case, not as a settled, continuing rule after the later Bench ruling.

The episode matters because it shows how a procedural question can become a federal question. A Governor is a constitutional head, not an elected legislator, yet delay at that stage can hold up laws passed by an elected Assembly. At the same time, the later ruling shows the Court's own willingness to revisit its remedies when it judges that it has gone beyond what the constitutional text supports.

Key takeaways

  • Article 200 gives a Governor assent, withholding of assent, or reservation for the President, not indefinite delay.
  • In April 2025 the Supreme Court called the withholding of assent to ten Tamil Nadu Bills illegal and erroneous and deemed them assented.
  • A larger Bench later in 2025 rejected mandatory timelines and deemed assent as a general rule, so the final position is narrower than the April ruling alone suggests.

Why it matters for civil studies

Governor assent cases test the balance between an appointed constitutional head, an elected legislature, and judicial review in a federal system.

Think it through

Should the Constitution prescribe a fixed time limit for assent, or would that create more problems than it solves?
