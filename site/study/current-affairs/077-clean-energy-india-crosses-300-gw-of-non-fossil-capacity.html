Clean energy: India crosses 300 GW of non-fossil capacity

Environment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation; disaster and disaster management.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

On 31 July 2026, India's installed non-fossil-fuel electricity generation capacity crossed 300 gigawatts, reaching 300.50 GW, more than 54 per cent of a total installed base of roughly 552 GW, according to the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy. That is more than 60 per cent of the 500 GW non-fossil target set for 2030. Solar accounts for 164.59 GW of the total, against 2.8 GW in 2014, and wind stands at 58.14 GW, against about 21 GW in 2014. By 30 September 2026, total capacity had reached 558.8 GW, with 308 GW from non-fossil sources, about 55 per cent of the total.

The path to the milestone shows how fast the build-out has become. As of 31 March 2026, non-fossil capacity stood at 283.46 GW, comprising 274.68 GW of renewables and 8.78 GW of nuclear, with solar at 150.26 GW and wind at 56.09 GW. During the financial year 2025-26, India added a record 55.29 GW of non-fossil capacity, of which solar contributed 44.6 GW and wind 6 GW. India reached the 50 per cent non-fossil share of installed capacity in June 2025, five years ahead of its 2030 nationally determined contribution target, and now ranks third globally in installed renewable capacity, behind China and the United States.

Manufacturing has scaled alongside generation. Solar module manufacturing capacity rose from 2.3 GW in 2014 to about 172 GW by March 2026, and under the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers framework, listed solar module capacity has surpassed 200 GW. The PM Surya Ghar Yojana for rooftop solar had benefited around 59.5 lakh households through about 50.1 lakh rooftop installations by 29 September 2026. These figures describe capacity built, which is not the same as electricity delivered.

That distinction is the honest caveat. In 2025-26, non-fossil sources contributed 29.2 per cent of total electricity generation, well below their share of capacity, because solar and wind produce only when the sun shines or the wind blows, while coal remains the dominant baseload fuel. Renewable output rose from 190.96 billion units in 2014-15 to 477.79 billion units in 2025-26, but closing the gap between capacity and generation needs storage, transmission, and grid flexibility. The 300 GW milestone is real, and it is also a measure of what is built, not yet of what is delivered.

Key takeaways

  • India's non-fossil capacity reached 300.50 GW on 31 July 2026, over 54 per cent of total installed capacity and past 60 per cent of the 500 GW 2030 target.
  • Solar, at 164.59 GW, drove a record 55.29 GW of non-fossil additions in 2025-26, with manufacturing capacity also scaling sharply.
  • Capacity is not generation: non-fossil sources supplied 29.2 per cent of electricity generated in 2025-26, so storage and grids are the next constraint.

Why it matters for civil studies

Energy transition targets are usually written in capacity, but households and industry experience generation, prices, and reliability, so both numbers must be read together.

Think it through

Should the next energy target be written in gigawatts of capacity, in share of electricity generated, or in emissions avoided?
