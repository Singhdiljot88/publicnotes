Security Council reform in 2026: the case against 1945

World affairs · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — India and its neighbourhood — relations; bilateral, regional and global groupings and agreements; important international institutions, agencies and fora.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

On 22 October 2026, India's Permanent Representative to the United Nations, Ambassador Parvathaneni Harish, told the General Assembly that a Security Council which does not adequately represent the wider membership faces a deficit in legitimacy. The Council has fifteen members: five permanent members — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States — who hold veto power over substantive resolutions, and ten members elected for two-year terms. Harish argued that authority derived from victory in a war more than eighty years ago cannot substitute for representation today, noting that about 2.5 million Indians served in the Second World War and about 90,000 died in military service.

India's practical demand was procedural rather than rhetorical: that the intergovernmental negotiations on reform proceed on the basis of a written text, so that countries debate and revise a shared document instead of allowing the process to remain open-ended. A written text would not settle the dispute over permanent seats or the veto, but it would force negotiators to work through their differences on representation and power. India wants the Council expanded in both the permanent and elected categories, and works with Brazil, Germany and Japan in the Group of Four, which supports new permanent seats and greater representation for regions that lack them.

The 81st General Assembly debate in September 2026 showed both the breadth of support and the depth of disagreement. Portugal's Prime Minister Luís Montenegro called for permanent seats for Africa, Brazil and India; Mauritius's Foreign Minister Dhananjay Ramful explicitly reiterated support for India's candidature; the Marshall Islands backed Japan and its G4 partners; Finland's President Alexander Stubb proposed at least two new permanent seats for Africa, two for Asia-Pacific and one for Latin America. Russia's Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov supported India and Brazil but opposed Germany and Japan, exposing a fault line inside the G4 itself. The Uniting for Consensus group, by contrast, opposes any new permanent members and favours expansion through elected or rotating seats.

Reform remains blocked by the Charter's own amendment rule: any change to the Council's membership requires approval by two-thirds of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of UN members, including all five permanent members — each of which can veto the change that would dilute its own privilege. The Council's membership was last changed by an amendment adopted in 1963 and in force from 1965, which raised it from eleven to fifteen by adding four elected seats, without touching the five permanent seats. India's October 2026 intervention did not change those rules; it sharpened the argument that an organisation which cannot renew its principal organ will struggle to command authority in a world that has moved far beyond 1945.

Key takeaways

  • On 22 October 2026 India's UN ambassador argued the Security Council faces a legitimacy deficit because its permanent membership still reflects 1945.
  • India pressed for negotiations on a written text, and for expansion in both permanent and elected categories, with the G4 partners Brazil, Germany and Japan.
  • Support came from Portugal, Mauritius and others, but Russia backed only India and Brazil, and Charter amendment requires all five permanent members' ratification.

Why it matters for civil studies

Reform of the world's top security body is blocked by the very privilege it would reform: every permanent member can veto its own dilution.

Think it through

If a veto-holder can block reform forever, is the Council's legitimacy a legal question, a political one, or simply a fact of power?
