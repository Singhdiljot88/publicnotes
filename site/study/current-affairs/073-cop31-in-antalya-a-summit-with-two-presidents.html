Current affairs explainers · Note 073

COP31 in Antalya: a summit with two presidents

Environment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · Updated 23 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation; disaster and disaster management.

Background explainer from the November 2026 study collection. Events discussed may predate the collection; this is not a monthly news update.

Update, 9 November 2026: COP31 opened today in Antalya. The agenda was adopted without objection after a delay while delegations settled it, President Murat Kurum called the summit an “implementation COP” and said the 1.5°C target “remains our compass”, and the WMO State of the Climate Update 2026, released on opening day, put January–September 2026 at about 1.47°C above the pre-industrial average. The leaders’ summit met on 11–12 November; negotiated outcomes are still to come, so what follows remains a preview of the substance, not an outcome.

Update, 12 November 2026: Over 100 national leaders attended the two-day summit, which produced pledges and new facilities rather than negotiated outcomes. Countries endorsed the Antalya Compact for Energy Efficient Buildings, which aims for a 25% cut in the operational energy intensity of the global building stock by 2035 alongside faster electrification of buildings; Türkiye and China signed a renewable-energy cooperation agreement envisaging about $15 billion of investment, including 7 GW of new solar and wind capacity; and the Pacific Resilience Facility passed $200 million in pledges. The presidency also launched a green-industrialisation facility for least developed countries, and Brazil handed over a voluntary fossil-fuel transition roadmap that sits outside the negotiations. At the Leaders-Level Non-CO2 Greenhouse Gases Summit, philanthropies pledged over US$480 million to cut methane and other super-pollutants, and the UK announced a further US$25 million for the Super Pollutant Country Action Accelerator, taking it to seven more countries — Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, Fiji, Morocco, Pakistan, the Philippines and Uzbekistan. Negotiated outcomes were still to come, with the summit scheduled to run to 20 November.

Update, 20 November 2026: COP31 did not close on schedule. The summit was due to end on Friday 20 November, but the closing plenary was suspended at 10:29 pm local time with no final text agreed, and negotiations continued into the weekend. The presidency promised a third draft of the cover decision on Saturday morning, 21 November; a Carbon Brief survey of summit watchers put the median expected finish at late on Sunday night, 22 November. The second draft, published on Thursday, had dropped all references to transitioning away from fossil fuels and removed a proposed target of US$120 billion a year in adaptation finance by 2035, keeping only a non-binding call to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035. A few decisions were adopted before the suspension, including on Article 6 carbon-market rules and matters relating to least developed countries, and Ethiopia was confirmed as host of COP32 in Addis Ababa in 2027. There is no final negotiated outcome yet, so this note will be updated again once the talks conclude.

Update, 21 November 2026: Talks stayed deadlocked through Saturday 21 November. No third draft of the cover decision had appeared by Saturday afternoon, despite the presidency’s promise of one that morning, and negotiators were still divided over restoring language on transitioning away from fossil fuels — a loose coalition of about 130 countries insisted on it, while Saudi Arabia and its allies opposed it — and over adaptation finance and carbon capture. The EU’s climate commissioner said on Saturday it would be “a good deal or no deal”, and on Saturday evening the EU, the UK, the least developed countries, the small island states and many African nations marched to the presidency office to demand the fossil-fuel language be restored. Talks were expected to continue into Sunday 22 November. There is still no final negotiated outcome, so this note will be updated again once the talks conclude.

Update, 22 November 2026: COP31 closed on Sunday 22 November 2026 — two days into overtime, the longest climate summit on record — without an overarching deal. The EU, the UK and other countries rejected the proposed “Antalya Pact” cover decision because its final draft made no mention of transitioning away from fossil fuels, only recalling the COP28 agreement in Dubai; about 130 countries had insisted the language be restored, while Saudi Arabia and its allies opposed it. The draft’s other headline targets — raising electricity’s share of final energy use to 35% by 2035 and scaling up carbon-removal technology — were not adopted either. President Murat Kurum told the closing plenary: “I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted.” Not everything was lost: countries did adopt separate decisions on the Global Goal on Adaptation, the just transition work programme, Article 6 carbon-market rules, climate finance, loss and damage and other technical matters. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the outcome “deeply disappointing”. The unresolved issues now pass to COP32 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027.

The 31st United Nations climate conference, COP31, opened on 9 November 2026 at the Antalya Expo Center in Türkiye and was scheduled to run to 20 November 2026. What follows is a preview of the substance, written before the summit opened; it closed on 22 November 2026 with no overarching deal agreed (see the updates above). Its most unusual feature was settled a year earlier. Türkiye and Australia had both bid to host, and under UN rules failure to agree would have sent the summit to Bonn in Germany, home of the UN climate secretariat. At COP30 in Belém, Brazil, in November 2025, the two countries compromised: Türkiye hosts the summit and holds the COP presidency, while Australia's climate and energy minister, Chris Bowen, presides over the negotiations.

The split is genuinely unprecedented in thirty years of climate summits. Türkiye's environment minister, Murat Kurum, chairs COP31, hosts the World Leaders' Summit, and leads the action agenda, while Bowen holds authority over the negotiated outcomes. Australia also leads a pre-COP meeting hosted in the Pacific, in Fiji and Tuvalu, from 5 to 8 October 2026, intended to give ministers a chance to narrow differences before Antalya and to foreground the climate vulnerability of Pacific island states, which was Australia's original reason for bidding with the Pacific. Reporting in September 2026 noted tension in the arrangement, with Kurum saying the final decision rests with the COP presidency, while the agreed modalities give Australia authority over negotiations, so how the split works in practice is itself something to watch.

Several items arrive unresolved from the mid-year negotiations in Bonn in June 2026. Developing countries have pressed for the new climate finance work programme agreed at Belém to have its own agenda item at COP31, focused on Article 9.1 of the Paris Agreement, under which developed countries are to provide finance. At COP30, dozens of countries backed a roadmap for transitioning away from fossil fuels, but it was not included in the decision. The European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism entered its definitive period on 1 January 2026, requiring importers of covered goods above a 50-tonne annual threshold to be authorised declarants, which adds a trade dimension to the climate talks.

For India, the summit matters on finance, adaptation, and energy transition: the pace at which climate finance flows, whether a just-transition mechanism agreed at COP30 gets real shape, and how border carbon measures treat developing-country exports. None of that will be decided by who holds the gavel. It will be decided, as always, by whether nearly two hundred countries can agree on text.

Key takeaways

  • COP31 ran 9–20 November 2026 in Antalya, then ran two days into overtime — the longest climate summit on record — and closed on 22 November 2026 with no overarching deal, after the EU, the UK and others rejected the proposed “Antalya Pact” because it made no mention of transitioning away from fossil fuels. It ran under a split arrangement: Türkiye holds the presidency and hosts, while Australia's Chris Bowen presides over negotiations.
  • The split followed a hosting deadlock at COP30 that risked defaulting the summit to Bonn, and its working in practice was tested at the summit.
  • Unresolved items include the climate finance work programme, a fossil-fuel transition roadmap left out at Belém, and the EU's carbon border measure now in force; the fossil-fuel transition language and the adaptation finance target were left out of the final draft and remain unresolved, passing to COP32 in Addis Ababa in 2027. Separate decisions on adaptation, just transition, Article 6 and other technical matters were adopted.

Why it matters for civil studies

Climate summits are where international law, trade policy, and development finance meet, and their procedures, like a split presidency, can shape outcomes as much as their pledges.

Think it through

Does splitting a summit's presidency between two countries strengthen multilateralism, or weaken accountability for the outcome?
