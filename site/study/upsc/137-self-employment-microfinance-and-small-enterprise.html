UPSC foundations · Note 137

Self-employment, microfinance and small enterprise

Development, poverty and employment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Inclusive growth and issues arising from it; issues relating to poverty and hunger. Also touches GS I: poverty and developmental issues.

A large share of Indians are not employees at all: they run a small farm, a shop, a repair unit, a food stall or a tailoring business, or they work as own-account workers. Self-employment is therefore not a residual category but the main form of work for much of the country, and policy for jobs is, in large part, policy for small enterprise. The micro, small and medium enterprises (MSME) sector illustrates the scale: it comprises more than 6.3 crore enterprises and, by official estimates, contributes nearly 30 per cent of GDP, roughly 45 per cent of manufacturing output and about 45 per cent of exports.

Small enterprises face three chronic constraints: credit, markets and skills. Banks prefer larger, collateral-backed borrowers, so small firms have long relied on informal lenders at high cost. Self-help groups (SHGs) — small collectives, mostly of women, who save together and lend to members — became a major answer from the 1990s, linked to banks and later supported through the National Rural Livelihoods Mission, launched in 2011. Microfinance institutions, which lend small sums without collateral, expanded access further, but also drew criticism for high interest rates and aggressive recovery in some cases.

The government has layered several schemes on this base. The Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana, launched in 2015, refinances loans to small borrowers through banks and microfinance institutions, with loans classified as Shishu (up to Rs 50,000), Kishore (up to Rs 5 lakh) and Tarun (up to Rs 10 lakh). Stand-Up India, also from 2016, targets bank loans for Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe and women entrepreneurs. Digital payments and e-commerce platforms have since given small sellers wider markets, though they also expose them to new competition and platform fees.

The debate is about quality, not just credit. Most micro-enterprises stay tiny, employ few people beyond the family, and pay low incomes. Turning self-employment into productive enterprise requires reliable power, transport, market linkages, timely payments from buyers, and skills — the ecosystem around the firm, not only a loan. Microfinance can relieve a cash crunch, but it cannot by itself create a growth business, and when loans fund consumption rather than investment, debt can become a trap rather than a ladder.

Key takeaways

  • More than 6.3 crore MSMEs contribute nearly 30 per cent of GDP and about 45 per cent of exports.
  • Self-help groups and microfinance widened credit access, but high costs and over-indebtedness are real risks.
  • MUDRA (2015) grades small loans as Shishu, Kishore and Tarun, but enterprise growth needs markets and infrastructure too.

Why it matters for civil studies

Self-employment is the default form of work in India, so enterprise policy is employment policy.

Think it through

Does microcredit create entrepreneurs, or does it mainly help people survive in work that pays too little?
