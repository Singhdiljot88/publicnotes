Textiles and apparel: from fibre to fashion

Industry · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Effects of liberalisation on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth; infrastructure: Energy, Ports, Roads, Airports, Railways; investment models.

Textiles run from fibre to finished clothing, and India is present at nearly every stage: cotton farming, ginning, spinning, weaving, processing, garment stitching, and handloom and handicraft work. The chain includes large mills, powerloom clusters, handloom weavers and garment exporters, and it is one of the country's largest sources of employment, especially for women. That breadth is a strength, because value can be added at many stages, and a weakness, because a problem at one stage, such as raw material prices or dyeing pollution, spreads quickly.

Cotton is the best-known fibre, but the industry also works with jute, silk, wool and man-made fibres, and increasingly with blended fabrics. Natural fibres link textiles to agriculture and to rural livelihoods, while man-made fibres link it to petrochemicals and to global supply chains. A textile policy therefore has to speak to farmers, weavers, mills, exporters and environmental regulators at the same time, which is why the sector often appears fragmented in administration.

Two structural issues recur. First, much of India's output sits in the lower and middle segments of the value chain, while design, branding and high-performance fabrics capture more value abroad. Second, processing stages such as dyeing and finishing are water-intensive and polluting, so common effluent treatment in clusters and cleaner technology are not side issues but conditions for the industry's survival near towns and rivers. Export buyers increasingly ask for traceability, labour standards and environmental compliance, so these are now commercial requirements, not only regulatory ones.

Government support has included technology upgradation support, integrated textile parks under the PM MITRA scheme, support for handlooms and handicrafts, and skill programmes. The park idea mirrors the cluster logic: shared infrastructure for processing, testing and logistics can help small units meet buyer standards that none could meet alone. Handloom support raises a different question, about preserving craft livelihoods and cultural products in a market dominated by powerlooms and imports.

The forward question is whether India can move from volume to value: more technical textiles for health, construction and defence uses, more design and branding, and cleaner production, while protecting the employment that makes the sector socially important. That balance, between jobs today and upgrading for tomorrow, is the heart of textile policy.

Key takeaways

  • India's textile chain runs from fibre and farming through spinning, weaving, processing and garments.
  • Value is concentrated in design, branding and technical textiles, not only in volume of cloth.
  • Dyeing and finishing make water use and pollution central to the sector's future.

Why it matters for civil studies

Textiles link agriculture, manufacturing, exports, women's employment and environmental regulation in one chain.

Think it through

Should policy protect handloom jobs as they are, or help weavers move into higher-value products? What would each path cost?
