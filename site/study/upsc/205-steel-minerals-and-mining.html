UPSC foundations · Note 205

Steel, minerals and mining

Industry · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Effects of liberalisation on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth; infrastructure: Energy, Ports, Roads, Airports, Railways; investment models.

Steel is the backbone metal of construction, automobiles, railways, ships and machinery, so steel policy is also infrastructure policy. India has been the world's second-largest producer of crude steel since 2018, after China, according to the World Steel Association's reporting. Domestic demand is driven by housing, roads, railways and manufacturing, which makes steel output a rough indicator of construction and investment activity, though not a complete one.

The National Steel Policy of 2017 set an aspiration of 300 million tonnes of steelmaking capacity by 2030-31, alongside goals for quality, efficiency and value-added products. Reaching such capacity depends on iron ore and coking coal, much of the latter imported, on energy costs, and on logistics from mines and ports to plants. Green steel, made with lower-carbon energy and processes, is now part of the debate, because steelmaking is energy-intensive and faces pressure from climate policy and from buyers who want lower-carbon material.

Mining sits upstream of steel and of many other industries. The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act dates to 1957 and was amended in 2015 to move most mineral concessions to auction, replacing a system of discretionary allocation that had drawn criticism. Auctioning was meant to bring transparency and revenue to states, but it also raised questions about whether winning bids, royalties and local development funds actually reach mining-affected communities.

Those communities bear the visible costs of mining: land disturbance, dust, water use, forest clearance and displacement. The District Mineral Foundation, funded from mining royalties, was created to spend on development in affected districts, and forest and environmental clearances are meant to set conditions on mining. The persistent governance question is whether these safeguards are monitored after a mine opens, and whether affected people have a real voice in decisions about their land and water.

A balanced view treats steel and mining together. India needs steel for development and minerals for industry, but the terms matter: who gets the revenue, how the environment is restored, and whether mining districts become more prosperous or merely more excavated.

Key takeaways

  • India has been the world's second-largest crude steel producer since 2018, after China.
  • The National Steel Policy of 2017 aspired to 300 million tonnes of capacity by 2030-31.
  • The 2015 mining amendments moved most concessions to auction, raising transparency and local-benefit questions.

Why it matters for civil studies

Steel and mining link industrial growth, energy, federal revenue and environmental justice.

Think it through

Should mining revenue be spent mainly where minerals are extracted, or shared across the state? Who should decide?
