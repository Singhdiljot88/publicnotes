Evolution and the diversity of life

General science: physics, chemistry and biology foundations · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life (foundations: physics, chemistry, biology).

Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of populations over generations. Its central mechanism, natural selection, was set out by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in the mid-nineteenth century, most famously in Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859). The logic is simple and powerful. Individuals vary; some variations help survival and reproduction in a given environment; those individuals leave more offspring, so helpful variations become more common. Over many generations, small changes accumulate into large differences. Antibiotic resistance is natural selection in real time: bacteria that happen to survive a drug reproduce, and resistant strains spread.

The evidence for evolution comes from independent lines that converge. Fossils show simpler forms in older rocks and transitional forms linking major groups. Comparative anatomy reveals the same bone pattern in a human arm, a bat's wing, and a whale's flipper, adapted to different uses — strong evidence of common ancestry rather than separate design. Genetics confirms and extends this: species that look alike share more DNA, and all living things use the same genetic code, with DNA spelled in the same four bases. Embryos of different vertebrates also show striking similarities in early development.

Evolution explains the diversity of life without needing each species to be separately created in its present form. From a common ancestor, populations split when separated by geography or ecology, adapt to different conditions, and eventually become unable to interbreed — new species. This branching tree of life is why biologists classify organisms into nested groups: species within genera, genera within families, and so on up to domains. Biodiversity is therefore not a random assortment but a record of history, and extinction is the permanent loss of a unique branch.

Two misunderstandings are worth clearing up. First, evolution has no goal or direction toward perfection; it favours what works in a current environment, which is why cave fish lose eyes and island birds lose flight. Second, humans did not evolve from modern apes; humans and modern apes share a common ancestor that lived millions of years ago, and each lineage has changed since. For public policy, evolution matters practically: it explains why pests evolve resistance to pesticides, why flu vaccines must be updated, and why preserving habitats protects genetic diversity that may hold future medicines and crops.

Key takeaways

  • Natural selection (Darwin and Wallace, 1859): heritable variation plus differential reproduction changes populations over generations.
  • Fossils, comparative anatomy, and genetics independently support common ancestry.
  • Evolution has no goal; humans share a common ancestor with modern apes rather than descending from them.

Why it matters for civil studies

It explains practical policy problems — drug resistance, pesticide resistance, vaccine updates, conservation — from one mechanism.

Think it through

If evolution has no goal, why do so many species seem well designed for their environments?
