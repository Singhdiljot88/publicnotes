Space technology in daily life: weather, navigation, communication

General science in everyday life and emerging technology · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology.

Space technology affects daily life far more through services beamed down than through rockets watched going up. Weather satellites monitor clouds, storms, and ocean temperatures continuously, feeding forecasts that protect farmers, fishermen, airlines, and coastal communities. Navigation satellites let a phone fix its position anywhere on Earth, guiding ambulances, delivery vehicles, and aircraft. Communication satellites relay television, phone calls, and internet to places where laying cables is impractical. Remote-sensing satellites photograph the ground in wavelengths beyond visible light, revealing crop health, forest loss, water levels, and damage after disasters.

These services depend on different orbits. Satellites close to Earth pass overhead quickly and see detail, which suits imaging and some internet constellations. Satellites over the equator at about 36,000 kilometres orbit once a day and appear fixed, which suits continuous broadcasting and weather watch over one region. Choosing an orbit is a trade-off between detail, coverage, and cost, and launching and operating satellites requires tracking, ground stations, and rules to avoid collisions in increasingly crowded orbits — including the growing problem of space debris from defunct satellites and spent rocket stages.

India's space programme illustrates how a developing country can use space for development. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), established in 1969, built its programme around practical applications: communication satellites for television and telephony, remote-sensing satellites for agriculture and resource mapping, and navigation through the NavIC regional system. Its missions have also reached further: Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole in August 2023, making India the first country to land there, and the Mars Orbiter Mission reached Mars orbit in 2014 on its first attempt. The programme is often noted for achieving these results at comparatively low cost.

Space technology also raises governance questions. Who owns orbital slots and radio frequencies, who is liable when satellites collide, and how should the benefits of Earth observation be shared? Data from public satellites now help track deforestation, plan cities, and verify compliance with environmental rules — a form of transparency from above. At the same time, high-resolution imagery raises privacy questions, and dependence on foreign satellite services raises strategic ones. Space policy is therefore not only about rockets; it is about infrastructure, data, and sovereignty.

Key takeaways

  • Daily space services are weather forecasting, navigation, communication, and remote sensing for crops, forests, and disasters.
  • Orbit choice trades detail against coverage; geostationary satellites appear fixed above the equator.
  • ISRO (established 1969) built an applications-led programme; Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole in August 2023.

Why it matters for civil studies

It links science and technology questions to development, disaster management, and strategic policy.

Think it through

Should high-resolution satellite imagery of private land be freely available to anyone? Where would you draw the line between public benefit and privacy?
