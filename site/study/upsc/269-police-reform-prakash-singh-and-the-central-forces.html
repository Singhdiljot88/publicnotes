Police reform: Prakash Singh and the central forces

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

India's police system still rests on the Police Act, 1861, enacted under colonial rule, though many states have since passed their own police acts. The 1861 Act designed the police as an instrument of the state government, and that design creates a recurring tension: a police force must be accountable to the elected government, but not so dependent on it that transfers, postings and investigations become political rewards or punishments.

In Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006), the Supreme Court issued seven directives to kick-start reform: State Security Commissions to insulate policing from unwarranted political pressure; merit-based selection and a minimum two-year tenure for Directors General of Police; minimum two-year tenure for officers on operational duties, including Superintendents of Police and Station House Officers; separation of investigation from law-and-order functions; Police Establishment Boards for transfers and postings; Police Complaints Authorities at state and district levels; and a National Security Commission for chiefs of central police organisations. The Court directed implementation through executive orders or new legislation.

Implementation has been partial. Research by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative and others has found that most states have set up commissions and boards on paper but with limited independence, short tenures in practice or complaints authorities without binding powers. The result is a familiar pattern in Indian reform: formal compliance without functional change. Central armed police forces add another layer: forces such as the Central Reserve Police Force, Border Security Force, Indo-Tibetan Border Police, Sashastra Seema Bal, Central Industrial Security Force and Assam Rifles are controlled by the Centre and deployed to states, raising questions about coordination with state police and about accountability when they operate in a state.

Reform debates now also include technology and capacity: forensic laboratories, scientific investigation, community policing, women's help desks and better training. A police force that investigates well, treats complainants decently and is insulated from political transfer threats is more useful to internal security than a larger force with the same weaknesses.

Key takeaways

  • India's police still rest on the 1861 Police Act, supplemented by state police acts.
  • The Supreme Court's 2006 Prakash Singh directives set seven reforms, including fixed tenures and complaints authorities.
  • Implementation has been largely formal, with limited independence in practice, according to reform monitors.

Why it matters for civil studies

Police reform decides whether the state's most everyday power — the local police station — is trusted or feared.

Think it through

Should police chiefs be chosen by an independent commission, by the elected government, or by a mix of both?
