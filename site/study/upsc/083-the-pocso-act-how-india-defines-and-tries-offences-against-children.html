The POCSO Act: how India defines and tries offences against children

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, known as the POCSO Act, creates a special law for sexual offences against children. A child is defined as any person below eighteen years of age. The Act is gender-neutral in its protection: it applies to boys, girls and children of other gender identities. It defines distinct offences, including penetrative sexual assault, aggravated penetrative sexual assault, sexual assault, sexual harassment and the use of a child for pornographic purposes, with punishments graded according to the gravity of the offence.

The Act was amended in 2019 to introduce more stringent punishments, including the death penalty for certain aggravated offences against children. It also requires child-friendly procedures. Cases are to be tried by special courts designated for this purpose, the child's identity is to be protected, and the recording of evidence is meant to avoid repeated questioning and unnecessary exposure. Reporting of offences is mandatory for persons who know that an offence has been committed, and the Act provides that a person who fails to report may be punished.

The POCSO Rules, notified in 2020, strengthen these safeguards by setting out procedures for reporting, medical examination, support persons and compensation. The design reflects an understanding that a criminal trial can itself harm a child witness if it is slow, intimidating or public. Speed matters, but so does the way evidence is taken. A special court therefore has to balance two duties: protecting the child and giving the accused a fair trial, including the presumption of innocence and the right to challenge evidence.

Critics have raised questions about the Act's operation in practice, including delays in trials, the treatment of consensual relationships between older adolescents, and the interaction of the Act with personal laws on marriage. Parliament has retained eighteen as the age threshold, and the government has described that choice as a deliberate policy to prevent manipulation and exploitation of minors. For students, the Act is best studied as a complete system — definition, offence, procedure and support — rather than as a list of punishments alone.

Key takeaways

  • POCSO defines a child as a person below eighteen and is gender-neutral in the protection it offers.
  • The Act provides for special courts, protection of identity and child-friendly procedures, with POCSO Rules notified in 2020.
  • The 2019 amendment strengthened punishments, including the death penalty for certain aggravated offences.

Why it matters for civil studies

The POCSO Act shows how procedure can be as important as punishment: a law meant to protect children can fail if the trial itself causes harm.

Think it through

How should the law handle cases involving older adolescents without weakening protection for younger children who cannot consent?
