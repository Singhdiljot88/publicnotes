India's health system: public, private and in between

Health, education and human development · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Issues relating to development and management of Social Sector/Services relating to Health, Education, Human Resources. Also touches GS III: Indian Economy — growth, development and employment.

India's health system is a mix. The public system is organised in three tiers: sub-centres and primary health centres for villages and small towns, community health centres for blocks, and district hospitals and medical colleges for referrals. The National Health Mission, formed by merging the National Rural Health Mission (launched 2005) and the National Urban Health Mission under one umbrella in 2013, funds much of this network and supports a large cadre of ASHA community health workers who link villages to clinics. Health is primarily a state subject, so the quality of this network varies greatly between states.

The private sector, however, provides most outpatient care and a large share of hospital care, ranging from corporate hospitals in metros to single-doctor clinics and informal practitioners in villages. Households pay a high share of health costs out of pocket, which means a serious illness can push a family into debt — the problem economists call catastrophic health expenditure. Public spending on health has historically been low compared with other large economies, and shortages of doctors, nurses, drugs and equipment in public facilities push even poor patients toward private care.

The system also faces a double burden of disease. Infectious diseases, maternal and child conditions and undernutrition remain major problems, especially in poorer states, while non-communicable diseases — heart disease, diabetes, cancers and chronic lung disease — now cause a growing share of deaths as lifestyles, diets and life expectancy change. A health system built for infections and childbirth must now also manage lifelong conditions that need regular medicines, screening and follow-up.

Reform debates therefore focus on three questions: how much public money should go to health and to what — primary care, hospitals, or insurance; how to regulate private providers on price and quality; and how to staff rural facilities. The Ayushman Bharat programme, covered in the next note, is the largest recent attempt to answer the financing question, but the underlying structure — a thin public system alongside a large, unevenly regulated private sector — remains the defining feature of Indian health care.

Key takeaways

  • Public care runs in three tiers, funded largely through the National Health Mission (rural mission from 2005, merged in 2013).
  • Private providers deliver most outpatient care, and out-of-pocket payments can push families into debt.
  • India faces a double burden: infectious and maternal conditions plus rising non-communicable disease.

Why it matters for civil studies

Health system design decides who gets care, who pays, and whether illness means impoverishment.

Think it through

Should public money expand free primary care, or insure hospital treatment — and can it do both?
