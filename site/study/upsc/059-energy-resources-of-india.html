Energy Resources of India

Climate, rivers and natural resources of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Distribution of key natural resources across the world (including South Asia and the Indian subcontinent); important geophysical phenomena.

India's energy comes from a mix of sources, and the mix is changing. Coal has long been the largest source of commercial energy and electricity, mined mainly in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal and Madhya Pradesh, and burned in thermal power stations across the country. Oil and natural gas come partly from domestic fields, including the western offshore fields around Mumbai High and fields in Assam and Gujarat, and partly from imports, which makes India sensitive to world oil prices. Hydroelectric power comes from Himalayan and peninsular rivers, nuclear power from a small number of reactors, and renewable sources, above all solar and wind, have grown rapidly in the last decade.

Each source has a different geography and a different problem. Coal is abundant but polluting and concentrated in a few states, so transporting it to power stations elsewhere is costly. Hydropower is clean at the point of generation but requires dams that displace people and alter rivers, especially in the Himalayas, where geology adds landslide and earthquake risk. Solar power suits much of India, especially the sunny west and south, but it is variable across the day and needs storage and grid management. Wind power is concentrated in coastal and hilly corridors, notably in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Karnataka, where wind speeds are higher.

Energy policy must therefore balance three goals that often conflict: keeping power affordable and available, reducing pollution and carbon emissions, and ensuring that energy projects do not harm local communities and ecosystems. India's stated climate commitments include a large expansion of non-fossil electricity capacity, but the pace of coal's decline, the financing of renewables, the strengthening of transmission lines, and the provision of storage are all contested and practical questions. Rural energy access, cooking fuel and the reliability of supply for small businesses matter as much as large power stations in this picture.

For civil studies, energy is a systems question. A decision about a coal mine affects air quality, railways and state revenue; a decision about a solar park affects land use, water for cleaning panels and local jobs; a decision about a dam affects rivers, forests and displaced families. Good answers trace those links and ask who pays, who benefits and who decides, rather than treating energy as a list of sources and percentages that quickly go out of date.

Key takeaways

  • Coal remains India's largest commercial energy source, while oil and gas are partly imported, making prices sensitive to world markets.
  • Hydropower, nuclear, solar and wind each have distinct geographies: solar suits the sunny west and south, wind concentrates in coastal and hilly corridors.
  • Energy policy must balance affordability, emissions and local impacts, including displacement, land use and grid storage.

Why it matters for civil studies

Energy choices link geology, climate commitments, state revenue and everyday access to power and cooking fuel.

Think it through

Should India phase out coal as fast as possible, or as fast as replacement power, jobs and grid stability allow?
