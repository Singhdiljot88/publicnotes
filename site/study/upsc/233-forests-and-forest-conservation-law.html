Forests and forest conservation law

Environment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation, environmental impact assessment.

Forests provide timber and non-timber products, store carbon, regulate water, harbour wildlife and support the livelihoods of forest-dependent communities. India's forest policy has moved from treating forests mainly as a source of revenue and timber, under colonial and early post-independence management, to recognising their ecological and social roles. That shift is visible in law, but it is not complete, and the tension between use and protection runs through every forest decision.

The Forest (Conservation) Act of 1980 requires central government approval before forest land can be diverted to non-forest purposes such as mining, dams, roads or industry. It was meant to slow the rapid diversion of forests that had marked earlier decades. An amendment in 2023 changed the Act's scope, including provisions on the categories of land to which it applies, and it drew criticism from ecologists who argued that it could weaken protection for some forest areas, a debate that should be followed in its implementation and any court proceedings.

Compensatory afforestation is the mechanism for offsetting diversion: when forest land is diverted, the user must pay for afforestation elsewhere, under the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act of 2016, which created funds at national and state levels. Critics argue that a plantation is not a substitute for a natural forest, which holds biodiversity, soil and water functions that take decades to develop, and that compensatory sites are sometimes poor land or already vegetated. The debate is about whether money and planting can genuinely compensate for ecological loss.

The Forest Survey of India publishes the India State of Forest Report every two years, assessing forest and tree cover from satellite data and ground checks. Its figures are widely cited but also debated, because definitions matter: a plantation, an orchard or a dense natural forest can all count as cover, while differing in biodiversity. Community management adds another layer: joint forest management from the 1990s, and the Forest Rights Act of 2006, which recognises the rights of forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, both rest on the idea that people who live in forests are partners in protection, not obstacles to it.

A balanced forest policy must therefore answer three questions together: how much forest is diverted and for what, whether compensation genuinely restores ecological function, and whether forest communities gain secure rights and livelihoods from protection.

Key takeaways

  • The Forest (Conservation) Act of 1980 requires central approval before forest land is diverted.
  • The Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act of 2016 governs payments for afforestation to offset diversion.
  • The Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognises rights of forest-dwelling communities.

Why it matters for civil studies

Forest law combines conservation, development, federal approval and community rights.

Think it through

Can a plantation ever compensate for a natural forest? If not, what should diversion require instead?
