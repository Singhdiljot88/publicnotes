The Forest Rights Act, 2006: recognising rights that were never recorded

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, known as the Forest Rights Act, begins from a historical claim: during the consolidation of state forests, the rights of forest-dwelling communities were not adequately recorded. The Act therefore recognises and vests forest rights in forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers who were residing in forests for generations but whose rights could not be recorded. It was enacted on 29 December 2006 and came into force at the end of 2007.

The Act provides both individual and community rights. Individual rights include the right to hold and live on forest land under individual or common occupation for habitation or self-cultivation for livelihood. Community rights include nistar and other customary uses, ownership of and access to minor forest produce traditionally collected, grazing and fishing rights, and the right to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage community forest resources. Individual titles are capped at four hectares, are heritable but not alienable, and are issued jointly in the names of both spouses where applicable.

Eligibility differs for the two groups. Forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes are recognised in areas where they are scheduled. Other traditional forest dwellers must show that they have lived in and depended on forests for three generations, understood as seventy-five years, before 13 December 2005. The Gram Sabha is the authority that initiates the process of determining rights, and claims pass through sub-divisional and district committees. The Act also provides that forest dwellers are not to be evicted until their rights have been recognised through this process.

The Act's implementation has been uneven. Individual titles have been granted more readily than community forest resource rights in many areas, and disputes over evidence, forest department objections and conservation concerns continue. For civil studies, the Act is a reminder that a right can be old in practice and new in law: recognition requires a record, a forum and a procedure, not only a declaration that a community has lived in a forest.

Key takeaways

  • The Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognises rights of forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers whose rights were not recorded during forest consolidation.
  • Individual rights cover habitation and self-cultivation up to four hectares, heritable but not transferable; community rights cover minor forest produce, grazing, fishing and conservation.
  • Other traditional forest dwellers must show three generations, or seventy-five years, of residence and dependence before 13 December 2005, with the Gram Sabha initiating claims.

Why it matters for civil studies

Forest rights law turns history into procedure: a community's long residence must be proved, recorded and titled before it counts in law.

Think it through

When conservation and livelihood conflict in a forest, who should decide — the Gram Sabha, the forest department, or a court — and on what evidence?
