Public debt: how much borrowing is sustainable

Public finance · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Government Budgeting.

Public debt is the total stock of borrowings owed by the government, accumulated from past deficits. It differs from the fiscal deficit, which is the flow of new borrowing in a single year. India's central government debt is mainly internal, raised through dated securities, treasury bills and small savings, with a smaller external share, while state governments also borrow through market loans and central loans. Debt is held by banks, insurance companies, pension funds, the Reserve Bank and other investors, so government borrowing is deeply linked to the financial system.

Debt sustainability depends less on the absolute amount than on the relationship between interest rates, growth and the primary balance. If the economy grows faster than the interest rate on debt, the debt-to-GDP ratio can fall even while the government borrows, because the denominator grows faster. If interest rates exceed growth and the government runs primary deficits, the ratio rises and interest payments consume a growing share of revenue, leaving less for services and investment. This arithmetic explains why the same debt ratio can be safe in one period and risky in another, depending on growth, inflation and borrowing costs.

India's general government debt, combining Centre and states, is high by emerging-market standards, though it is largely denominated in rupees, which reduces currency risk compared with foreign-currency debt. Supporters of higher borrowing argue that it funds infrastructure and welfare that raise growth and future revenues, and that India's savings and captive buyers, such as banks required to hold government securities, provide stable demand. Critics argue that high interest payments, state debt stress and off-budget liabilities, including guarantees and borrowings by public entities, understate true obligations and crowd out private credit.

Transparency is therefore central to the debt debate. Official debt figures may exclude guarantees, arrears or special-purpose borrowings, and different definitions produce different ratios. The Finance Commission and the FRBM framework have recommended debt anchors and glide paths, but these are targets, not automatic limits, and they are revised during shocks. For civil studies, public debt is a question of intergenerational fairness: borrowing for assets that benefit future generations can be justified, while borrowing for current consumption shifts costs to people who did not vote for the spending.

Key takeaways

  • Public debt is the accumulated stock of past borrowing; the fiscal deficit is the flow of new borrowing in one year.
  • Debt sustainability depends on growth relative to interest rates and on the primary balance, not on the debt level alone.
  • India's central debt is mainly internal and rupee-denominated, which limits currency risk but not the burden of interest payments.

Why it matters for civil studies

Debt links today's budgets to tomorrow's taxpayers, so its sustainability is a democratic as well as a financial question.

Think it through

When is it fair for a government to borrow for current spending, and when should borrowing be limited to investment?
