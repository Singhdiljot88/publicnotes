The Right to Education Act, 2009: a fundamental right with school standards

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

Article 21A, added by the 86th Amendment in 2002, makes free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen a fundamental right. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 gives effect to that right. It came into force on 1 April 2010. Every child in the age group has the right to full-time elementary education in a neighbourhood school until completion of elementary education, and no child is liable to pay any fee or charge that would prevent pursuing and completing that education.

The Act sets duties for governments and standards for schools. Appropriate governments must provide neighbourhood schools, and schools must meet norms on infrastructure, pupil-teacher ratios and teacher qualifications. It prohibits physical punishment and mental harassment, screening procedures for admission, capitation fees, private tuition by teachers, and the running of schools without recognition. No child is to be held back, expelled or required to pass a board examination until completion of elementary education.

A widely discussed provision requires private unaided schools, other than minority institutions, to admit children from disadvantaged groups and weaker sections to at least twenty-five per cent of seats in the entry class, with reimbursement by the government as prescribed. The aim is to prevent schooling from becoming a closed system in which access depends on a family's ability to pay. In practice, admission, reimbursement and the definition of a neighbourhood have all produced disputes, and implementation varies across states.

The Act's limits are also instructive. It covers elementary education, not pre-school or secondary education, and its quality depends on teacher availability and training, learning outcomes and school monitoring, not only on enrolment figures. For civil studies, the Act illustrates the difference between a right declared in the Constitution and a right delivered through budgets, school norms, admission rules and grievance mechanisms.

Key takeaways

  • Article 21A, added in 2002, makes free and compulsory education for ages six to fourteen a fundamental right; the RTE Act of 2009 came into force on 1 April 2010.
  • The Act sets school norms, bans capitation fees, screening, corporal punishment and detention before completion of elementary education.
  • Private unaided schools, other than minority institutions, must admit at least twenty-five per cent disadvantaged and weaker-section children in the entry class.

Why it matters for civil studies

The RTE Act shows the distance between a constitutional right and a classroom: norms, admissions, reimbursement and teacher supply decide whether the right is real.

Think it through

Should the right to education be extended beyond elementary school, or should the priority be improving learning inside the schools the Act already covers?
