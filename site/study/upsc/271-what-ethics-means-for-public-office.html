What ethics means for public office

Ethics and integrity · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS IV — Ethics and Human Interface; aptitude and foundational values for Civil Service; public/civil service values and Ethics in Public administration; probity in Governance — concept of public service, information sharing and transparency, codes of ethics and conduct, citizen's charters, work culture, quality of service delivery, utilisation of public funds, challenges of corruption.

Ethics is the study of what is right and good, and of how people ought to act. It is not the same as the law: an action can be lawful but still unethical, and in rare cases a person may argue that an unjust law should be challenged. Nor is ethics simply personal opinion. It involves reasons that can be stated, examined, and defended in public. For someone holding public office, those reasons matter more than usual, because decisions affect people who did not choose the official and cannot easily escape the consequences.

Public office is a trust. Officials handle public money, make rules that bind others, and exercise powers such as licensing, policing, and taxation. Citizens cannot monitor every decision, so they must trust that officials will act in the public interest rather than for themselves, their families, or their friends. That is why most systems for public standards combine rules with values: the rules set minimum boundaries, while values guide judgement in the many cases where no rule gives a complete answer.

A widely used statement of those values is the Seven Principles of Public Life, often called the Nolan principles, set out in 1995 by the United Kingdom's Committee on Standards in Public Life under its first chair, Lord Nolan. They are selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, and leadership. They are not law, but they have influenced codes of conduct in many countries, and they give a useful checklist: act only for the public interest, decide on merit, explain decisions, and model the standards you expect of others.

Ethics in public office is therefore a practical skill, not an abstract subject. It means noticing when a decision affects your own interests, seeking advice before acting, recording reasons, and accepting scrutiny even when it is uncomfortable. An official who does these things consistently protects both citizens and the institution, because public trust, once lost, is far harder to rebuild than any single decision is to defend.

Key takeaways

  • Ethics concerns reasons for action that can be defended in public, not only private opinion or legal compliance.
  • Public office is a trust: citizens cannot watch every decision, so officials must act in the public interest.
  • The 1995 Nolan principles — selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership — remain a standard checklist.

Why it matters for civil studies

It frames every later ethics topic: before asking what the rules say, ask whose interests a decision serves and whether its reasons could be defended openly.

Think it through

Think of a public decision you have read about recently. Could its reasons be defended in public without appealing to who benefits privately?
