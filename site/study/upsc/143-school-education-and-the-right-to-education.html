School education and the Right to Education

Health, education and human development · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Issues relating to development and management of Social Sector/Services relating to Health, Education, Human Resources. Also touches GS III: Indian Economy — growth, development and employment.

School education in India is vast: hundreds of millions of children, millions of teachers, and schools run by central and state governments, local bodies, aided institutions and private managements. The Constitution originally placed free and compulsory education for children up to 14 in the Directive Principles, as a goal rather than an enforceable right. That changed with the 86th Constitutional Amendment, 2002, which inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14 a fundamental right, and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, which gave it legal shape.

The RTE Act came into force on 1 April 2010. It requires free and compulsory elementary education in a neighbourhood school, bars capitation fees and screening of children at admission, and lays down norms for pupil-teacher ratios, classrooms, toilets, drinking water and playgrounds. Its best-known provision requires private unaided schools to reserve 25 per cent of entry-level seats for children from economically weaker sections and disadvantaged groups, with the government reimbursing the cost. It also originally barred detention — no child could be failed up to Class 8 — though a 2019 amendment allowed states to reintroduce examinations and detention in Classes 5 and 8 with safeguards.

Enrolment is now near-universal at primary level, but the system faces three deeper problems. First, learning levels are low, as the ASER surveys show and a later note discusses. Second, quality varies sharply: teacher vacancies and absenteeism, multi-grade classrooms and weak early-grade teaching affect poor and rural children most. Third, the school system is stratified — government schools, low-fee private schools and elite private schools offer very different experiences, and the 25 per cent quota, while important, reaches only a fraction of children.

The National Education Policy, 2020, added a new structure — 5+3+3+4, replacing the 10+2 system — and a focus on foundational literacy and numeracy, mother tongue or local language as medium in early years, and vocational exposure. Whether these changes raise learning depends less on the structure than on teacher training, classroom materials and accountability at the school level. For civil studies, the RTE story is a case study in turning a constitutional promise into a justiciable right — and in the gap between a right on paper and a classroom that delivers.

Key takeaways

  • Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) made education for ages 6-14 a fundamental right; the RTE Act, 2009, took effect on 1 April 2010.
  • The Act mandates free neighbourhood schooling, infrastructure norms, and 25 per cent private-school seats for weaker sections.
  • Enrolment is near-universal, but learning quality and stratification between school types remain the core problems.

Why it matters for civil studies

The RTE Act shows how a directive principle became an enforceable right — and how far rights alone can go.

Think it through

Does reserving 25 per cent of private seats help the system, or does it let the state off the hook for its own schools?
