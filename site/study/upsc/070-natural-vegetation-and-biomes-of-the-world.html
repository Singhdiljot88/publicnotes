UPSC foundations · Note 070

Natural Vegetation and Biomes of the World

World geography · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; history of the world — events from the 18th century (industrial revolution, world wars, redrawal of boundaries, colonisation, decolonisation).

A biome is a large region defined by its climate, soil and characteristic plants and animals. The main terrestrial biomes are tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, Mediterranean scrub, temperate grassland, temperate forest, boreal or taiga forest, and tundra. Each biome reflects a combination of temperature and rainfall: rainforest needs heat and abundant rain all year, savanna needs a wet season and a dry season, grassland needs moderate rain, taiga needs a short cool summer, and tundra survives only a brief growing season above frozen ground.

Tropical rainforests, in the Amazon, Congo basin and South-East Asia, are the most biodiverse biomes, with tall evergreen trees, layered canopies and countless species, many still undescribed. Savannas, in Africa, South America and Australia, are grasslands with scattered trees, home to large grazing animals and their predators. Temperate grasslands, the prairies of North America, the pampas of South America, the steppes of Eurasia and the veld of South Africa, have deep, fertile soils and are now largely farmed for grain. Boreal forests, circling the northern continents, are dominated by conifers adapted to cold and snow, while tundra, at the far north and on high mountains, has mosses, lichens and low shrubs.

Biomes are not fixed museum exhibits. They shift with climate, fire, grazing and human use: forests are cleared for farms and cities, grasslands are ploughed or overgrazed, and wetlands are drained. Deforestation in the Amazon and Congo basins reduces biodiversity, alters rainfall and releases stored carbon, while the loss of grassland soils reduces fertility and increases erosion. Conservation therefore involves more than protecting a single species: it means protecting the climate, soil and water conditions that allow a whole community of species to persist, often across national borders.

For civil studies, biomes link environment, economy and justice. Indigenous peoples and local communities often depend directly on a biome for food, medicine and identity, and they are often the first to suffer when it is degraded. International agreements on biodiversity, forests and climate all depend on understanding biomes, because a policy that protects rainforest may not suit grassland, and a carbon policy that values trees may undervalue grasslands and wetlands that also store carbon underground. A good answer names the biome, its climate, its uses and its threats, rather than treating nature as a single green background.

Key takeaways

  • The main terrestrial biomes are tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, Mediterranean scrub, temperate grassland, temperate forest, taiga and tundra.
  • Rainforests are the most biodiverse biomes; temperate grasslands have deep fertile soils and are now largely farmed for grain.
  • Biomes shift with climate, fire, grazing and human use, so conservation must protect conditions, not only single species.

Why it matters for civil studies

Biomes connect climate, soil, biodiversity and livelihoods, and they cross borders, requiring international cooperation.

Think it through

Should conservation prioritise the most biodiverse biomes, or the ones most threatened by human use?
