UPSC foundations · Note 300

Emerging technology and the questions it raises for society

General science in everyday life and emerging technology · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology.

Emerging technologies — quantum computing, advanced robotics, nanotechnology, brain-computer interfaces, and next-generation AI — share a pattern: they promise large benefits, arrive faster than the rules for governing them, and affect people who never chose them. Quantum computers, for instance, exploit quantum bits (qubits) that can exist in combinations of states, potentially solving certain problems — such as simulating molecules or breaking some forms of encryption — far faster than classical computers. Nanotechnology works at the scale of atoms and molecules, enabling new materials and targeted drug delivery. Each technology is different, but the governance questions repeat: who benefits, who bears the risk, and who decides?

One recurring question is jobs and livelihoods. New technologies destroy some tasks and create others, but the transition is rarely smooth: a weaver replaced by a power loom, or a clerk replaced by software, cannot instantly become the engineer who maintains the new machine. Societies have handled past transitions with education, retraining, and social protection — and sometimes failed to, leaving regions and generations behind. The lesson for today's technologies is that the pace of change matters as much as its direction, and policy that only celebrates innovation while ignoring displacement repeats old mistakes.

A second question is power. Technologies that depend on vast data, expensive infrastructure, or rare expertise tend to concentrate in a few firms or countries. That concentration affects competition, national security, and the ability of smaller players to participate. Rules on data access, competition, standards, and technology transfer therefore shape whether a new technology spreads widely or deepens existing divides. The digital divide — between those with connectivity, devices, and skills and those without — is the clearest warning: a technology's benefits are not automatically shared.

A third question is values. Should a machine decide who gets a loan, a job interview, or parole? Should human embryos be edited to prevent disease, and if so, where is the line? Should autonomous weapons choose their own targets? Different societies will answer differently, but all need a process for answering: expert advice, public deliberation, and regulation that can be revised as evidence grows. The precautionary principle — acting to prevent plausible serious harm even before full proof exists — is often invoked, but it must be balanced against the harm of delaying beneficial technologies. For civil studies, the key skill is not predicting which technology wins, but analysing who gains, who loses, what is uncertain, and which institutions can legitimately decide.

Key takeaways

  • Emerging technologies arrive faster than their governing rules and affect people who never chose them.
  • Transitions create losers as well as winners; pace matters as much as direction, and retraining and protection shape outcomes.
  • Governance questions repeat: distribution of benefits, concentration of power, and values — decided through deliberation and revisable regulation.

Why it matters for civil studies

It is the capstone for technology questions: the same framework applies whether the technology is AI, quantum computing, or gene editing.

Think it through

When a new technology's risks are plausible but unproven, should regulators wait for proof or act early? Who should bear the cost of being wrong?
