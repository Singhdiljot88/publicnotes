The Reserve Bank of India: central bank, regulator and banker to government

Banking · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment (banking and financial system as a resource-mobilisation channel).

The Reserve Bank of India, established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and nationalised on 1 January 1949, is India's central bank. A central bank differs from a commercial bank: it issues currency, conducts monetary policy, regulates banks and payment systems, manages foreign exchange reserves, and acts as banker to the government. It does not normally compete for retail deposits or lend directly to the public in the way commercial banks do.

The Reserve Bank's core functions include issuing banknotes, holding the cash reserves of banks, acting as lender of last resort when solvent banks face temporary liquidity problems, and supervising banks and non-banking financial companies. It manages the government's borrowing programme and its accounts, and it operates and oversees payment systems. It also promotes financial inclusion and consumer protection in the banking system, and conducts research that informs policy. These roles can conflict: for example, managing government borrowing cheaply can sit uneasily with tight monetary policy to control inflation, which is why the Bank's autonomy and its relationship with the government are regularly debated.

Monetary policy is now conducted under the flexible inflation targeting framework adopted in 2016, with price stability as the primary objective while keeping growth in mind. The Monetary Policy Committee sets the policy repo rate, the rate at which the Reserve Bank lends to banks. Changes in that rate influence lending and deposit rates, though transmission is not instant or complete, because banks' funding costs, competition and risk perceptions also matter. The Reserve Bank also uses tools such as the cash reserve ratio, the statutory liquidity ratio, open market operations and liquidity facilities to manage money market conditions.

The Reserve Bank's independence is functional rather than absolute. Its Governor is appointed by the government, its monetary framework and inflation target are set in consultation with the government, and it operates within laws made by Parliament. Debates about independence are therefore about the degree of operational freedom in setting rates and regulating banks, not about separation from democratic accountability. For civil studies, the Bank is a good example of a technocratic institution operating inside a democracy: it needs credibility with markets and the public, and legitimacy through law and oversight.

Key takeaways

  • The Reserve Bank was established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, and nationalised on 1 January 1949.
  • It issues currency, conducts monetary policy, regulates banks and payment systems, manages reserves and acts as banker to government.
  • Monetary policy is set by the Monetary Policy Committee under the inflation targeting framework adopted in 2016.

Why it matters for civil studies

Almost every interest rate, payment and banking rule in India traces back to the central bank's decisions.

Think it through

How much independence should a central bank have in a democracy, and who should be accountable when its policy hurts growth or jobs?
