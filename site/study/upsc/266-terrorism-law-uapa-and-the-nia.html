Terrorism law: UAPA and the NIA

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

India's main anti-terrorism law is the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, originally aimed at activities threatening India's sovereignty and integrity. Amendments in 2004 added terrorist acts to the Act after earlier laws, the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the Prevention of Terrorism Act, had lapsed or been repealed. Further amendments in 2008, after the Mumbai attacks, and in 2013 strengthened provisions on terrorist financing and asset freezing.

The 2019 amendment allowed the government to designate individuals, not only organisations, as terrorists, and empowered the Director General of the National Investigation Agency to approve seizure of property linked to terrorism in cases the agency investigates. It also allowed NIA officers of the rank of Inspector and above to investigate UAPA cases. Supporters argue these powers are needed against individuals who operate without a formal organisation; critics argue that designation without a prior judicial finding, combined with strict bail provisions, risks misuse against dissent.

The National Investigation Agency was created by the National Investigation Agency Act, 2008, after the Mumbai attacks, as a central agency to investigate and prosecute scheduled offences, including UAPA offences, across states. Its 2019 amendment extended its jurisdiction to offences committed outside India against Indian citizens or affecting India's interests, and added offences such as human trafficking, counterfeit currency, cyberterrorism and prohibited arms to its schedule. NIA cases are tried in special courts, and prosecution under UAPA requires the sanction of the central government.

The balance in terrorism law is between prevention and accountability. Strict bail, long investigations and special courts can help dismantle networks, but they also raise the cost of a wrongful accusation, since a person may spend years in custody before trial. Conviction rates, trial duration and the use of designation powers are therefore legitimate measures of whether the law works, alongside the number of attacks prevented.

Key takeaways

  • UAPA, enacted in 1967, became India's main anti-terror law through amendments in 2004, 2008, 2013 and 2019.
  • The 2019 amendment allowed individual designation as terrorists and widened NIA powers.
  • The NIA, created in 2008, investigates scheduled offences across states and tries cases in special courts.

Why it matters for civil studies

Terrorism law tests the balance between preventive power and the rights of the accused in every investigation.

Think it through

Should designation as a terrorist require a court finding first, or is executive designation with later review sufficient protection?
