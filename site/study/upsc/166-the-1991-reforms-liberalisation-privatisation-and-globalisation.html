The 1991 reforms: liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation

Economy · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment; inclusive growth and issues arising from it.

In 1991 India faced a severe balance of payments crisis, with foreign exchange reserves falling to critically low levels and the country at risk of default on external obligations. The government led by Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, responded with a New Economic Policy presented in the Budget of July 1991. The reforms are commonly summarised as LPG: liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. They marked a shift from a highly licensed, import-substituting economy towards a more market-oriented one, though the state retained a large role in many sectors.

Liberalisation meant reducing government controls: industrial licensing was abolished for most industries, restrictions on capacity expansion were eased, and trade policy moved towards lower tariffs and fewer quantitative restrictions. Privatisation meant a smaller direct role for public sector enterprises in business, including disinvestment of government equity and opening sectors to private competition. Globalisation meant deeper integration with the world economy through trade, foreign investment and technology flows. India later became a founding member of the World Trade Organization in 1995, which locked in many of these openings.

The reforms also included stabilisation measures: fiscal consolidation, monetary restraint and a more realistic exchange rate, supported by assistance from the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. Supporters argue that the reforms raised growth, expanded consumer choice, built foreign exchange reserves and helped create competitive industries in services and manufacturing. Critics argue that gains were uneven, that agriculture and informal workers benefited less, and that inequality, jobless growth in some periods and vulnerability to global shocks increased. Both claims can be partly true, which is why evaluation depends on the period and indicator chosen.

For civil studies, 1991 is a case study in crisis-driven reform. It shows how external pressure, domestic political leadership and expert advice combine, how reforms are sequenced rather than delivered at once, and how later governments have continued, paused or reversed particular measures. It also shows the limits of a single turning point: many controls were dismantled gradually, and debates on the state's role in welfare, infrastructure and strategic sectors continue long after 1991.

Key takeaways

  • The 1991 New Economic Policy responded to a balance of payments crisis under Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh.
  • LPG stands for liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation: fewer licences and controls, a smaller direct business role for the state, and deeper world-market integration.
  • The reforms combined stabilisation with structural change, and their effects were uneven across sectors and groups.

Why it matters for civil studies

1991 reshaped the framework within which every later budget, trade and industrial policy has been made.

Think it through

Was 1991 mainly a response to crisis, a change of ideas about the state, or both, and how would you tell the difference?
