Water resources and groundwater

Environment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation, environmental impact assessment.

India holds a large share of the world's people but a much smaller share of its renewable freshwater, and rainfall is concentrated in a few monsoon months. That combination makes storage, groundwater and efficient use central to water policy. Rivers, reservoirs, tanks and aquifers must carry the country through dry months, while floods in the wet months must be managed without wasting the water that will be needed later.

Groundwater is the quiet workhorse. It irrigates a large share of farmland and supplies drinking water to villages and cities, often through private wells that are cheap to drill but hard to regulate. Because groundwater is largely invisible, over-extraction shows up late, as falling water tables, drying wells, and in coastal areas, saltwater entering freshwater aquifers. India is among the world's largest users of groundwater, which makes its management a national issue, not a local one.

Institutions reflect the split between surface and groundwater. The Ministry of Jal Shakti, created in 2019, brings drinking water and water resources under one roof. The Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in 2019, aims to provide tap water to every rural household. The Atal Bhujal Yojana, also launched in 2019, works on community-led groundwater management in water-stressed areas, paying attention to measurement, aquifer mapping and local planning. The Central Ground Water Authority regulates extraction in notified areas, though enforcement across millions of wells is limited.

The deeper governance problem is that water is a state subject under the Constitution, while rivers cross state boundaries, so inter-state disputes over sharing are common and are adjudicated through tribunals and, increasingly, political negotiation. Pricing is another difficulty: free or cheap electricity for pumping encourages groundwater use, while charging for water is politically sensitive even where it would encourage conservation. Demand management, such as drip irrigation, crop choice and reuse of treated wastewater, often matters more than finding new supply.

For civil studies, water rewards systems thinking. A question about a dam, a well or a tap connection is also a question about energy pricing, crop patterns, federal relations and climate variability, and an answer that treats only one of these will miss why the problem persists.

Key takeaways

  • Groundwater irrigates much farmland and supplies drinking water, but over-extraction shows up late as falling tables.
  • The Ministry of Jal Shakti was created in 2019; the Jal Jeevan Mission and Atal Bhujal Yojana also launched in 2019.
  • Water is a state subject while rivers cross states, making sharing disputes a federal issue.

Why it matters for civil studies

Water policy connects agriculture, energy pricing, federalism and climate in a single system.

Think it through

Should groundwater be treated as a private right of the landowner, or as a common resource? What follows from each view?
