Manual scavenging: prohibition, hazardous cleaning and rehabilitation

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

Manual scavenging is the manual cleaning, carrying or disposal of human excreta from insanitary latrines, open drains, pits or railway tracks. The Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013 prohibits the construction of insanitary latrines and the employment of any person as a manual scavenger. It replaced an earlier law of 1993 that had approached the issue mainly as a sanitation problem. The 2013 Act frames it as a question of dignity and rights, and pairs prohibition with rehabilitation.

The Act also deals with hazardous cleaning of sewers and septic tanks. A person may not be employed for such cleaning unless the employer provides protective gear and cleaning devices and observes safety precautions. This distinction matters: work done with proper equipment, training and safeguards is not manual scavenging as defined by the Act, while the same task done by hand, without protection, is prohibited. Local authorities — municipalities, panchayats, cantonment boards and railway authorities — are responsible for surveying their areas, identifying manual scavengers and ensuring that no insanitary latrines remain.

Rehabilitation is the second half of the Act. A person identified as a manual scavenger is to receive a photo identity card, financial assistance, a scholarship for children, a residential plot and assistance to build on it, and training for an alternative livelihood with a stipend during training, together with subsidy and loan support for self-employment. The aim is not only to stop a practice but to break dependence on it: without an alternative income, prohibition alone can push workers into the same work informally or into destitution.

Implementation remains difficult, and deaths during sewer and septic tank cleaning continue to be reported. The National Action for Mechanised Sanitation Ecosystem, known as NAMASTE, launched in 2023-24, seeks to mechanise sanitation work and support sewer and septic tank workers. The lesson for civil studies is to follow a prohibition through to its enforcement chain: survey, identification, equipment, penalty, rehabilitation and mechanisation must all work, or the ban remains a statement on paper.

Key takeaways

  • The 2013 Act prohibits insanitary latrines and employment as a manual scavenger, replacing the 1993 sanitation-focused law.
  • Hazardous sewer and septic tank cleaning without protective gear, devices and safety precautions is prohibited; properly equipped work is treated differently.
  • Rehabilitation includes identity cards, financial help, scholarships, housing assistance and livelihood training, alongside the NAMASTE mechanisation scheme.

Why it matters for civil studies

Manual scavenging law shows that a ban is only the first step: rehabilitation and mechanisation decide whether workers actually leave the work.

Think it through

When a prohibited practice continues informally, is the failure mainly in detection, in punishment, in rehabilitation, or in the lack of machines?
