Invasive alien species in India

Biodiversity · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation, environmental impact assessment (biodiversity conservation).

Not every non-native species is a problem. Many crops, from potatoes to tomatoes, came from elsewhere and are now part of Indian farming. A species becomes invasive when it is introduced — deliberately or accidentally — outside its natural range, establishes itself, spreads, and causes harm to native species, ecosystems, livelihoods or health. The harm, not the foreign origin, is the test.

In India, well-known examples include lantana, which spreads through forests and grasslands and changes fire and grazing patterns; water hyacinth, which chokes lakes and canals, blocks light and oxygen, and hampers fishing and navigation; and Prosopis juliflora, introduced in places for fuel and shade, which in some arid areas competes aggressively with native vegetation and groundwater. Parthenium, a weed, affects crops and can cause skin and respiratory problems in people who handle it. Each case is local: a plant that is useful in one setting can be a pest in another.

Control is difficult and expensive once a species is widespread. Prevention — checking imports, ballast water, nursery stock and aquarium or pet releases — is cheaper than eradication, and early detection with rapid removal works better than a campaign launched after a species has covered a district. Biological control, using a natural enemy, can help but carries its own risk if the control agent itself spreads, so it needs careful testing and regulation.

Globally, the Kunming-Montreal framework adopted in 2022 sets a target of reducing the introduction and establishment of invasive alien species by at least 50 per cent by 2030. India has no single comprehensive invasive-species law; control is spread across forest, agriculture, quarantine and customs rules. That fragmentation is itself a policy problem for students to notice. It also means that a trader, a farmer and a forest officer may each follow a different rulebook for the same species, which weakens prevention.

Key takeaways

  • An invasive species is defined by establishment, spread and harm, not merely by foreign origin.
  • Lantana, water hyacinth, Prosopis and Parthenium illustrate different kinds of harm in India.
  • Prevention and early action are cheaper and more effective than late eradication.

Why it matters for civil studies

Invasive species connect trade, agriculture, health and forest policy, and they punish slow or fragmented government response.

Think it through

Should India have a single invasive-species law, or is coordination across existing forest, farm and trade rules enough?
