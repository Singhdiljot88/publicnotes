Senior citizens and the Maintenance Act, 2007: a speedy remedy with limits

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 provides a simple, speedy and inexpensive remedy for older people who cannot maintain themselves. A senior citizen is defined as a citizen of India aged sixty years or above. A parent or grandparent who is unable to maintain himself or herself from earnings or property may claim maintenance from adult children. A childless senior citizen may claim maintenance from a relative who would inherit the senior citizen's property.

Claims are decided by Maintenance Tribunals constituted by state governments, generally at the sub-divisional level, with an Appellate Tribunal above them. The Tribunal may order a monthly allowance for maintenance, and may order interim maintenance while the case is pending. Maintenance is defined to include food, clothing, residence and medical attendance and treatment. The Act is intended to be less formal than a civil suit, and legal representation before the Tribunal is restricted, so that an older applicant is not disadvantaged by cost or delay.

The Act also protects property in a specific situation. Under Section 23, if a senior citizen transfers property after the Act came into force, on condition that the transferee will provide basic amenities and physical needs, and the transferee then fails to do so, the Tribunal may declare the transfer void. This provision has been widely discussed because it links a gift or transfer to the care that was promised in return. Abandonment of a senior citizen by a person having care of that person is also an offence under the Act.

The remedy has limits that students should note. A Tribunal's order depends on the means of the children or relatives and on the needs of the senior citizen, and enforcement can still be slow. The Act also does not replace the wider welfare system: pensions, healthcare, old-age homes and protection from abuse require separate schemes and institutions. Its value lies in giving an older person a forum that is local, quick and focused on maintenance, rather than a general solution to ageing.

Key takeaways

  • The 2007 Act defines a senior citizen as an Indian citizen aged sixty or above and allows claims against adult children, or, for childless seniors, inheriting relatives.
  • Maintenance Tribunals decide claims, including interim maintenance, covering food, clothing, residence and medical care.
  • Section 23 allows a conditional property transfer to be declared void if promised care is not provided.

Why it matters for civil studies

Senior citizens law is a good test of forum design: a local tribunal can be faster than a court, but only if its orders can actually be enforced.

Think it through

Should maintenance for older parents be a family duty enforced by a tribunal, a state pension duty, or a mix of both?
