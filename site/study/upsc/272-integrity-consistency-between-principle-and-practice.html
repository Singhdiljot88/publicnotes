Integrity: consistency between principle and practice

Ethics and integrity · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS IV — Ethics and Human Interface; aptitude and foundational values for Civil Service; public/civil service values and Ethics in Public administration; probity in Governance — concept of public service, information sharing and transparency, codes of ethics and conduct, citizen's charters, work culture, quality of service delivery, utilisation of public funds, challenges of corruption.

Integrity is often described as wholeness: what a person believes, says, and does fit together, in public and in private. In public life it has a more specific meaning. The Nolan principles define integrity as avoiding obligations to people or organisations that might inappropriately influence official work, not taking decisions to gain financial or other benefits for oneself, family, or friends, and declaring and resolving interests and relationships. A person of integrity does not merely avoid being caught; they avoid being placed in a position where their judgement could reasonably be doubted.

That is why integrity is tested most in small, ordinary choices rather than dramatic ones. Accepting a gift from a contractor, letting a relative jump a queue, or softening a report to please a superior may each seem harmless alone. Together they create a pattern: decisions begin to follow relationships rather than rules and merit. Once that pattern is visible, citizens reasonably ask whether larger decisions are also shaped by favour, and the institution's authority weakens even if no law has been broken.

Integrity also requires consistency under pressure. It is easy to be principled when a decision costs nothing. The test comes when honesty risks promotion, popularity, or a comfortable working relationship. Systems can support this by making the honest course the easy one: clear rules on gifts and hospitality, registers of interests, and procedures that require decisions to be recorded with reasons, so that a decision taken on merit can be shown to be so.

For civil studies, integrity is best understood as a habit supported by institutions, not as a personal trait that some people simply have. Recruitment, training, supervision, and protection for those who raise concerns all shape whether officials act with integrity. Judging a system, therefore, means asking not only whether its leaders speak about values, but whether its daily routines make acting on those values possible.

Key takeaways

  • Integrity means beliefs, words, and actions fit together, in public and in private.
  • In public life it specifically means avoiding obligations that could influence decisions and declaring interests.
  • Small favours matter: a pattern of them teaches citizens that relationships, not merit, decide outcomes.

Why it matters for civil studies

It explains why conduct rules focus on gifts, interests, and recorded reasons rather than only on large scandals.

Think it through

Can an official be law-abiding yet lack integrity? Give an example, and explain what makes it an integrity problem rather than a legal one.
