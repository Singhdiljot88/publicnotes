UPSC foundations · Note 061

Latitude, Longitude and Time

World geography · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; history of the world — events from the 18th century (industrial revolution, world wars, redrawal of boundaries, colonisation, decolonisation).

Latitude and longitude are the grid by which every place on Earth is located. Latitude measures distance north or south of the equator, from 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles. The main parallels are the Tropic of Cancer (23½° N), the Tropic of Capricorn (23½° S), the Arctic Circle (66½° N) and the Antarctic Circle (66½° S). Longitude measures distance east or west of the Prime Meridian, which passes through Greenwich in London and is defined as 0°. Together, a latitude and a longitude fix a place uniquely, which is why they underpin maps, navigation and satellite positioning.

Time follows from longitude. The Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, so each 15° of longitude corresponds to one hour of time difference, and each degree to four minutes. Places east of Greenwich are ahead of Greenwich Mean Time, and places west are behind it. Because local solar time would change with every degree, countries adopt standard times based on a chosen meridian. India uses 82°30′ E, giving Indian Standard Time, five hours and thirty minutes ahead of Greenwich Mean Time. Large countries such as Russia, Canada and the United States need several time zones, while small or administratively centralised countries use one.

The International Date Line, roughly along 180° longitude in the Pacific, is where the calendar date changes. Crossing it eastwards, a traveller repeats a day; crossing it westwards, a day is skipped. The line zigzags around island groups so that a single country is not split between two dates. This is a practical convention, not a physical feature: it exists because a round Earth needs an agreed place where one day ends and the next begins. Confusion about the date line is a favourite source of examination questions, and it matters in real life for flights, shipping and international business.

For civil studies, latitude and longitude are not abstract geometry. Latitude explains climate zones: the tropics are warm year-round, the temperate zones have distinct seasons, and the polar regions are cold and dark in winter. Longitude explains time zones, which affect administration, trade and communication across a country as wide as India or as connected as the modern world. A reader who can use the grid can read any map, understand why the same date can be a different day in two places, and see why time is a political as well as an astronomical choice.

Key takeaways

  • Latitude runs 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles; longitude runs east and west from the Prime Meridian (0°) at Greenwich.
  • The Earth turns 15° per hour, so each degree of longitude equals four minutes of time difference.
  • The International Date Line, near 180°, is where the calendar date changes, zigzagging to avoid splitting island countries.

Why it matters for civil studies

The lat-long grid explains climate zones, time zones and the date change, linking astronomy to everyday administration.

Think it through

Should a wide country like India keep one time zone for unity, or adopt more zones to match daylight?
