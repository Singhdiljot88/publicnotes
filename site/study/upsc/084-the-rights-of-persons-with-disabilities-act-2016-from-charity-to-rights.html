The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016: from charity to rights

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 replaced the Persons with Disabilities Act of 1995 and gave effect in Indian law to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. It came into force in April 2017. Its central shift is in language and approach: disability is not treated only as a medical condition or a matter for charity, but as the result of the interaction between a person's impairment and barriers in society, such as inaccessible buildings, transport, information and attitudes.

The Act expanded the list of recognised disabilities from seven to twenty-one. The expanded list includes blindness, low vision, hearing impairment, locomotor disability, dwarfism, intellectual disability, mental illness, autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, chronic neurological conditions, specific learning disabilities, multiple sclerosis, speech and language disability, thalassemia, haemophilia, sickle cell disease, multiple disabilities including deaf-blindness, acid attack victims and Parkinson's disease. The Central Government may notify further categories.

The Act provides for rights to equality and non-discrimination, accessibility, education, employment and social security. Persons with benchmark disabilities, defined as at least forty per cent of a specified disability, are entitled to reservation in government employment and in higher education. The Act also requires governments to ensure accessible public buildings, transport and information, and it establishes authorities and commissions at the Centre and in states to monitor implementation and hear complaints.

The gap between the Act and everyday experience remains the main challenge. Accessibility duties depend on budgets, building rules and enforcement, and many workplaces and schools still lack ramps, sign-language support or accessible learning material. The Act therefore rewards a practical way of reading law: do not stop at the right that is declared, but ask who must act, by when, with what funds, and before which authority a person can complain when nothing changes.

Key takeaways

  • The 2016 Act replaced the 1995 Act, expanded recognised disabilities from seven to twenty-one, and came into force in April 2017.
  • It defines benchmark disability as at least forty per cent of a specified disability, with reservation in government jobs and higher education.
  • Its rights depend on accessibility duties, monitoring authorities and complaint mechanisms, not on declaration alone.

Why it matters for civil studies

Disability law tests whether you read a statute as a delivery system — rights, duties, funds, deadlines and remedies — rather than as a promise.

Think it through

Which barrier matters most in practice: a building without access, a workplace without reasonable accommodation, or a complaint system that is slow to act?
