India and the United States: the civil nuclear deal that changed the relationship

International relations and India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — India and its neighbourhood — relations; bilateral, regional and global groupings and agreements involving India and/or affecting India's interests; effect of policies and politics of developed and developing countries on India's interests, Indian Diaspora.

For much of the Cold War, India and the United States were, in the phrase often used by scholars, estranged democracies: they shared democratic systems but differed on non-alignment, nuclear policy and relations with the Soviet Union and Pakistan. The civil nuclear agreement of 2008 changed that pattern. It began with a joint statement by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and President George W. Bush on 18 July 2005, under which India agreed to separate its civil and military nuclear facilities and place its civil facilities under International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards, while the United States agreed to work toward full civil nuclear cooperation.

The agreement required several steps. India produced a separation plan, concluded a safeguards agreement with the IAEA, and obtained a waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group on 6 September 2008, allowing civilian nuclear trade despite India not being a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The United States Congress passed enabling legislation, and the bilateral agreement, known as the 123 Agreement after Section 123 of the US Atomic Energy Act, was signed on 10 October 2008 and entered into force on 6 December 2008.

The deal's significance went beyond nuclear energy. It ended a long period in which India was largely excluded from civilian nuclear commerce after its 1974 nuclear test, and it signalled American acceptance of India as a responsible nuclear power outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty framework. It also opened the way for closer defence, technology and strategic cooperation, including later agreements on logistics, communications and geospatial cooperation, and the elevation of the relationship to a comprehensive global strategic partnership.

The deal also had domestic costs. It was debated intensely in India, including over the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act of 2010, which suppliers argued made them liable in ways that discouraged investment, and over whether the agreement constrained India's strategic programme. For civil studies, the nuclear deal is a case study in how a single agreement can reset a bilateral relationship, while leaving implementation questions that last for years.

Key takeaways

  • The 2005 joint statement by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and President George W. Bush set out separation of civil and military facilities under IAEA safeguards.
  • The Nuclear Suppliers Group granted India a waiver on 6 September 2008, and the 123 Agreement was signed on 10 October 2008 and entered into force on 6 December 2008.
  • The deal ended India's exclusion from civilian nuclear commerce after the 1974 test, despite India remaining outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty.

Why it matters for civil studies

The civil nuclear deal shows how an exception can become a turning point: a single agreement reset a relationship that decades of diplomacy had not.

Think it through

Was the nuclear deal primarily about energy, about strategic partnership, or about India's status in the global order?
