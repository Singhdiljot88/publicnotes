The scientific method and how science tests claims

General science: physics, chemistry and biology foundations · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life (foundations: physics, chemistry, biology).

Science is not a collection of facts but a method for testing claims about the world. The core idea is simple: a claim about nature should be stated clearly enough that evidence could show it to be wrong. A scientist begins with observations, forms a hypothesis — a testable explanation — and then designs experiments or observations that could support or undermine it. If the evidence contradicts the hypothesis, the hypothesis is revised or abandoned. This willingness to be corrected is what distinguishes science from mere opinion, however confidently held.

The philosopher Karl Popper, in The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934), called this falsifiability: a genuinely scientific claim sticks its neck out. A theory that can explain every possible outcome explains nothing, because no observation could ever count against it. Good theories make risky predictions. When Isaac Newton published his laws of motion in 1687, they predicted the paths of planets and falling bodies with precision; when later observations of Mercury's orbit did not quite fit, physicists did not ignore the anomaly — it eventually helped lead to Einstein's theory of relativity. Anomalies are not embarrassments to science; they are its engine.

Several habits protect this process. Experiments use controls, so that only one factor changes at a time. Results are measured, repeated, and checked by others, because a single striking result may be error or chance. Studies are reported with enough detail for others to repeat them, and peer review subjects claims to scrutiny before publication. None of this guarantees truth — science revises itself — but it makes error easier to find and harder to hide.

For a civil studies reader, the method matters beyond the laboratory. Public debates are full of claims dressed in scientific language: that a remedy cures a disease, that a policy will cut pollution by a stated amount, that a new technology is safe. The scientific method gives a checklist for such claims: is the claim testable, what evidence would count against it, was there a control group, has anyone independent repeated it, and is correlation being presented as causation? A claim that fails those questions may still be true, but it has not yet earned the authority of science.

Key takeaways

  • A scientific claim must be testable: evidence must be able to show it wrong (falsifiability).
  • Controls, repetition, and peer review make error easier to find and harder to hide.
  • Correlation is not causation: two things rising together does not prove one causes the other.

Why it matters for civil studies

It is the basic toolkit for judging evidence in policy debates, from health claims to environmental statistics.

Think it through

A new study claims a herbal remedy cures a common illness. What evidence would you demand before accepting the claim?
