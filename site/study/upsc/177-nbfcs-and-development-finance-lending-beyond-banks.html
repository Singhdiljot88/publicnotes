NBFCs and development finance: lending beyond banks

Banking · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment (banking and financial system as a resource-mobilisation channel).

Non-banking financial companies, or NBFCs, lend, invest and provide financial services without holding a full banking licence. They cannot generally accept demand deposits like banks, and they are regulated by the Reserve Bank of India, though the intensity of regulation varies with size and systemic importance. NBFCs matter because they reach borrowers banks serve weakly: small firms, vehicle buyers, housing borrowers, gold-loan customers and micro-entrepreneurs, often with faster decisions and local knowledge.

Development financial institutions have a different history. After independence, institutions such as the Industrial Finance Corporation of India, set up in 1948, the Industrial Credit and Investment Corporation of India and the Industrial Development Bank of India were created to provide long-term finance for industry when commercial banks focused on short-term working capital. Over time, many development institutions converted into banks or were wound down as capital markets deepened, and the National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development was set up in 2021 to renew long-term infrastructure finance. The cycle shows how the gap between short-term deposits and long-term project needs keeps returning in new form.

NBFCs face a structural risk: they often borrow short-term from banks and markets and lend long-term, so a loss of market confidence can quickly create a liquidity crisis even if their loans are not all bad. The collapse of Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services in 2018 exposed this vulnerability and led to tighter Reserve Bank supervision, including scale-based regulation that imposes bank-like requirements on the largest NBFCs. The episode also showed contagion: stress in one large NBFC tightened funding for others, for housing finance and for small firms dependent on NBFC credit.

The policy challenge is to preserve NBFCs' reach and speed without allowing regulatory arbitrage, where risk migrates to less-regulated corners of finance. That requires clear rules on capital, liquidity, governance and disclosure, and coordination among regulators for banks, markets and insurance. For civil studies, NBFCs illustrate shadow banking in Indian form: useful innovation and inclusion on one side, and systemic risk that is harder to see because it sits outside the banking balance sheet on the other.

Key takeaways

  • NBFCs lend and invest without a full banking licence and are regulated by the Reserve Bank, with tighter rules for the largest firms.
  • Development institutions such as IFCI, set up in 1948, supplied long-term industrial finance when banks focused on short-term lending.
  • The IL and FS crisis of 2018 exposed NBFC liquidity risk from borrowing short and lending long, prompting scale-based regulation.

Why it matters for civil studies

Credit outside banks expands access but can also hide risk, so its regulation is a financial stability question.

Think it through

Should fast, locally knowledgeable NBFC lending be encouraged even if it is riskier than bank lending, and who should bear that risk?
