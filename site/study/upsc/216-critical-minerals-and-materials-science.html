UPSC foundations · Note 216

Critical minerals and materials science

Science · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; achievements of Indians in science & technology; indigenisation of technology and developing new technology.

Modern technology runs on a short list of minerals that are essential and hard to substitute: lithium and cobalt for batteries, rare earth elements for magnets in motors and wind turbines, silicon and gallium for electronics, and graphite, nickel and manganese for energy storage. A mineral counts as critical when it is economically important and its supply is concentrated, vulnerable to disruption, or controlled by a few countries. Criticality is therefore a judgement about markets and geopolitics as much as about geology.

India published a list of 30 critical minerals in 2023, through the Ministry of Mines, to guide exploration, mining and recycling policy. The list reflects India's needs across clean energy, electronics, defence and transport, and it marked a shift from treating mining as a revenue activity alone to treating it as a supply-chain strategy. Exploration, however, is slow: finding a deposit, proving it, clearing forests and land, and building processing capacity can take a decade, and many critical minerals need specialised refining that India is still building.

Rare earth elements illustrate the point. They are not especially rare in the earth's crust, but they occur in low concentrations and are costly and chemically difficult to separate, which is why processing capacity is concentrated in a few countries. In India, rare earths occur notably in beach sand minerals such as monazite, and the public sector undertaking Indian Rare Earths Limited works in this area. The strategic question is not only where the ore lies but who can refine it to usable purity.

Recycling and substitution are the other half of the strategy. Used batteries, electronics and magnets contain the same metals as mines, and recovering them reduces import dependence and waste. Materials science also offers substitution: researchers look for battery chemistries that use less cobalt or lithium, and magnet designs that use fewer rare earths. Neither recycling nor substitution removes the need for mining in the near term, but both change the bargaining position of a buyer.

For civil studies, critical minerals tie together geology, trade, environment and industrial policy. A sound answer will note that listing a mineral does not create supply, that refining capacity matters as much as ore, and that mining decisions must weigh the interests of local communities and ecosystems against national supply goals.

Key takeaways

  • India published a list of 30 critical minerals in 2023 to guide exploration and supply policy.
  • Criticality reflects supply concentration and substitutability, not only geological abundance.
  • Refining capacity, recycling and substitution matter as much as finding ore.

Why it matters for civil studies

Critical minerals are a compact case study in resource security, trade and environmental trade-offs.

Think it through

Should India mine a critical mineral at home despite local environmental costs, or import it and accept supply risk?
