E-governance and the digital divide

Space and digital · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology and issues relating to intellectual property rights. Also touches GS II: e-governance — applications, models, successes, limitations and potential.

E-governance means using digital tools to deliver government services: certificates, land records, pensions, tax filing, grievance redress and benefit payments. The Digital India programme, launched in 2015, organised this work around digital infrastructure, governance on demand and digital empowerment of citizens. Its visible products include the UMANG application, which brings many government services onto one platform, and online portals for services that once required repeated visits to offices.

Common service centres are the human bridge. These village-level centres, run by local entrepreneurs, help citizens who lack a phone, connectivity or digital skills to use online services, for a small fee. They show an important design principle: a digital service is only as inclusive as its assisted channel. Where assisted access works, digital delivery can reduce travel, waiting time and petty corruption; where it fails, it can add a new gatekeeper between citizens and their entitlements.

The digital divide has several layers. Access depends on a phone, network coverage and electricity. Ability depends on literacy, language and confidence with technology. Usefulness depends on whether the service actually solves a problem, such as a land record that is wrong in the database or a pension that stops without explanation. Women, older people, persons with disabilities and remote communities face larger gaps on all three layers, so national averages for internet users hide who is left out.

Data quality is a quiet but decisive factor. Digitising a wrong land record makes the error faster and harder to challenge, not fairer. Successful e-governance therefore needs correction mechanisms: a way to report an error, a person who can fix it, and a timeline for doing so. Grievance portals and service guarantees are attempts to supply that accountability, with uneven results.

For civil studies, e-governance should be judged by outcomes for the least connected user, not by the number of services online. A service that works for a city professional but fails for a rural pensioner has not yet delivered governance on demand, whatever its portal claims.

Key takeaways

  • The Digital India programme was launched in 2015 around infrastructure, on-demand governance and empowerment.
  • Common service centres provide assisted access for citizens without phones or digital skills.
  • The digital divide has access, ability and usefulness layers, and national averages hide who is excluded.

Why it matters for civil studies

E-governance tests whether technology improves accountability or merely digitises old problems.

Think it through

Is an online-only service ever justified for an essential entitlement? What safeguards would it need?
