Public expenditure and subsidies: who benefits and at what cost

Public finance · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Government Budgeting.

Public expenditure is how the state turns revenue into services: salaries for teachers and health workers, interest on past debt, subsidies for food, fuel and fertiliser, transfers to states, and investment in roads, railways and digital infrastructure. How spending is classified matters for debate. Revenue expenditure maintains current services, capital expenditure creates assets, and transfers move resources between levels of government or to households. Each category serves a purpose, and cutting one to protect another has different long-term effects.

Subsidies are a large and contested part of spending. Food subsidy supports the public distribution system, fertiliser subsidy lowers input costs for farmers, and fuel and electricity subsidies have at various times lowered household and farm costs. Supporters argue that subsidies protect food security, farm incomes and access to energy for poor households. Critics argue that subsidies leak to non-poor beneficiaries, distort prices and cropping choices, discourage efficiency in power and fertiliser use, and consume funds that could build schools, health facilities or irrigation. The same subsidy can therefore be defended as protection and attacked as waste, depending on its targeting and side effects.

Reform efforts have focused on targeting and delivery rather than abolition alone. Direct Benefit Transfer sends cash support to bank accounts, Aadhaar-linked identification aims to remove duplicate beneficiaries, and nutrient-based subsidy for fertilisers tries to encourage balanced use. These tools can reduce leakages but can also exclude genuine beneficiaries when records are wrong or authentication fails. Evaluating a subsidy therefore requires evidence on leakage, exclusion, price effects and fiscal cost together, not any one figure in isolation.

For civil studies, expenditure analysis is about opportunity cost: every rupee spent on one programme is a rupee not spent on another, or a rupee borrowed for the future. Budgets reveal priorities more honestly than speeches, because they show what the state actually funds, in what amounts, and with what results. Asking who benefits, who pays, and what would happen without the spending turns budget tables into a democratic question.

Key takeaways

  • Public spending includes revenue spending on services, capital spending on assets, subsidies and transfers to states and households.
  • Food, fertiliser and energy subsidies aim to protect consumers and farmers but can leak, distort prices and crowd out other spending.
  • Direct Benefit Transfer and better targeting aim to cut leakages, but exclusion errors remain a risk.

Why it matters for civil studies

Spending choices reveal government priorities more reliably than announcements, because money is finite.

Think it through

Should a poorly targeted subsidy be reformed, replaced by cash transfers, or kept because its beneficiaries have no alternative?
