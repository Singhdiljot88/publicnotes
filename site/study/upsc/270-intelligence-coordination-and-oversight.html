UPSC foundations · Note 270

Intelligence, coordination and oversight

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

Intelligence failures are often failures of coordination rather than collection: one agency holds a piece of information, another holds a second piece, and no one joins them in time. The 1999 Kargil conflict and the 2008 Mumbai attacks both led to reviews that stressed this point. The Kargil Review Committee recommended better coordination and a National Security Council system, while post-Mumbai reforms created the National Investigation Agency, strengthened coastal security and expanded the Multi Agency Centre for intelligence sharing.

The main domestic intelligence agency is the Intelligence Bureau, India's oldest intelligence organisation, which collects internal intelligence and coordinates through the Multi Agency Centre, a platform under the Intelligence Bureau where central and state agencies share terrorism-related inputs. The Research and Analysis Wing handles external intelligence, while military intelligence, the National Technical Research Office and financial and signals agencies contribute technical and financial streams. The National Intelligence Grid, or NATGRID, was designed to link databases from agencies such as immigration, banking, telecom and transport for counter-terrorism analysis, though its scope and privacy safeguards have been debated.

Oversight is the weak link. Unlike several democracies, India has no dedicated parliamentary committee or statutory oversight body for intelligence agencies; the Intelligence Bureau and the Research and Analysis Wing are not created by statute in the way the NIA is, and their budgets and operations are not publicly audited in detail. Supporters of the current system argue that secrecy is essential and that executive oversight through the Prime Minister's Office and the National Security Adviser is sufficient. Critics argue that secrecy without independent oversight risks misuse, including surveillance of political opponents or journalists, and that a classified oversight mechanism could protect both security and rights.

The test for any intelligence system is not whether it collects more data, but whether it produces timely, accurate warnings that decision-makers act on, and whether it does so within the law. More databases without analysts, translators and local knowledge produce noise, not warning, and surveillance without a legal framework produces distrust that makes future cooperation harder.

Key takeaways

  • Post-Kargil and post-Mumbai reviews stressed intelligence coordination over collection alone.
  • The Intelligence Bureau leads domestic intelligence and hosts the Multi Agency Centre for sharing.
  • India lacks a dedicated statutory parliamentary oversight body for its intelligence agencies.

Why it matters for civil studies

Intelligence reform is about joining information in time and holding secret power to account without revealing its methods.

Think it through

Should India create a parliamentary intelligence oversight committee with access to classified material, or would that risk leaks that endanger operations?
