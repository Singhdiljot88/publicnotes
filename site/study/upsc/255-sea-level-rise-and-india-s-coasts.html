Sea-level rise and India's coasts

Climate and disasters · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Disaster and disaster management; conservation, environmental pollution and degradation (climate change).

Sea level rises for two main reasons: ocean water expands as it warms, and land ice — glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets — melts into the sea. The rise is not uniform: local land subsidence, ocean currents and the gravitational effects of melting ice mean some coasts experience faster relative rise than the global average. For low-lying coasts, even a few tens of centimetres matter, because they raise the baseline for storm surges and push salt water further into rivers, wells and farmland.

India's exposure is large. Major cities including Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata lie near the coast, and the Sundarbans delta, the Kutch coast and many fishing villages sit barely above sea level. Saltwater intrusion already affects drinking water and crops in parts of the Sundarbans and coastal Gujarat and Tamil Nadu, according to state and research reports, and cyclones striking a higher sea cause deeper inland flooding than the same storm would have decades ago.

Responses fall into three broad types: protection, such as seawalls and mangrove restoration; accommodation, such as raised houses, flood-proof infrastructure and changed cropping; and, in the most exposed places, planned relocation. Mangroves are often the cheapest protection, since they dampen waves, trap sediment and support fisheries, but they need space and protection from clearing and pollution to work.

The hardest decisions are about land and people. A seawall protects one stretch of coast and can worsen erosion next door; relocation protects lives but breaks fishing livelihoods and community ties. Coastal regulation, insurance and land-use planning therefore need to be decided with coastal communities, not announced to them after a storm, and consent for relocation, where it is unavoidable, must be genuine and compensated fairly. Insurance that pays quickly, without complex paperwork, can also prevent a storm from becoming a debt trap for a fishing family.

Key takeaways

  • Sea level rises mainly from thermal expansion and land-ice melt, and its local effect varies.
  • Higher seas raise storm-surge flooding and saltwater intrusion into water and farmland.
  • Mangroves, seawalls, raised building and, where needed, relocation are different tools, not substitutes.

Why it matters for civil studies

Sea-level rise turns a global average into local questions of housing, water, fishing and land rights.

Think it through

When a coastal village becomes unsafe, who decides whether to protect it, adapt in place or relocate — and who pays?
