E-governance and Digital India: services online, and the access question

Governance, rights and institutions · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Important aspects of governance, transparency and accountability, e-governance, citizens' charters; statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies; role of civil services in a democracy.

E-governance means the use of information technology to deliver government services, to share information and to allow citizens to interact with the state without repeated visits to offices. The Digital India programme, launched on 1 July 2015, set out three visions: digital infrastructure as a core utility, governance and services on demand, and digital empowerment of citizens. Its pillars include broadband connectivity, universal mobile access, public internet access, e-governance, electronic delivery of services, and information for all.

Several platforms illustrate the model. DigiLocker allows citizens to store and share verified digital documents, such as driving licences and education certificates, without carrying physical copies. UMANG provides access to services of central and state governments through a single application. The Unified Payments Interface, developed by the National Payments Corporation of India, allows instant payments between bank accounts through a mobile phone, and has become a central part of digital payments in India, although it is a payments system rather than a government service portal.

E-governance can reduce discretion and delay, because an application leaves a digital trail and a service may be delivered without an intermediary. It can also create new barriers. Citizens without smartphones, connectivity, digital literacy or reliable identity documents may find an online-only service harder to use than an office, and errors in digital records can be difficult to correct. The design question is therefore not whether a service is online, but whether an offline alternative, help desk and correction mechanism remain available.

For civil studies, e-governance should be judged by outcomes for the least connected citizen, not by the number of services listed online. A service that is fast for a city resident with broadband and impossible for a rural resident without connectivity has not become more accessible; it has changed who can access it. Inclusion, language, grievance redress and data protection are part of e-governance, not additions to it.

Key takeaways

  • Digital India, launched on 1 July 2015, aims at digital infrastructure as a utility, governance and services on demand, and digital empowerment.
  • DigiLocker stores verified digital documents and UMANG brings central and state services into a single application.
  • Online delivery can reduce discretion but can exclude citizens without connectivity, devices, literacy or reliable records.

Why it matters for civil studies

E-governance is judged not by how many services are online, but by whether the least connected citizen can still reach the state.

Think it through

Should every government service have an offline alternative, or does keeping one slow the move to a fully digital state?
