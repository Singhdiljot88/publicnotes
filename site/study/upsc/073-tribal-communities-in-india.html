Tribal Communities in India

Indian society: diversity and change · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; effects of globalisation on Indian society.

Tribal communities, officially called Scheduled Tribes, are groups listed in the Constitution's schedules for particular states and union territories, entitled to special protection and support. According to Census 2011, Scheduled Tribes numbered about 10.43 crore people, or 8.6 per cent of India's population, in 705 groups. They are concentrated in two broad belts: the hills and forests of central India, including Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Odisha, and the north-eastern states, where tribal populations form majorities in several states. Other tribal communities live in the Himalayas, the Western Ghats, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep, each with distinct languages, economies and social organisation.

Tribal economies and societies are diverse. Some communities practise shifting cultivation, or jhum, clearing a forest patch, farming it for a few years and then leaving it fallow; others practise settled farming, pastoralism, hunting and gathering, or craft and wage work. Many tribal societies are relatively egalitarian compared with caste society, with less rigid hierarchy, greater freedom for women in some respects, and collective decision-making through village councils. At the same time, tribal communities differ greatly among themselves in language, religion, dress and political organisation, and no single description fits all of them.

Tribal communities face distinctive pressures. Loss of land and forest to dams, mines, plantations and conservation projects has displaced many families; the Fifth Schedule protects scheduled areas in much of central India, and the Sixth Schedule provides autonomous district councils in parts of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, but implementation is uneven. The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996, known as PESA, extends panchayat self-government to scheduled areas, and the Forest Rights Act, 2006, recognises individual and community rights over forest land and produce. Both laws are widely regarded as important but incompletely implemented, with claims delayed, rejected or contested.

For civil studies, tribal questions are questions of rights, development and identity together. Should development mean integration into the mainstream economy, protection of distinct ways of life, or a combination chosen by the community itself? Who decides when a mine, dam or sanctuary affects tribal land? And how can education, health and livelihood programmes reach remote communities without undermining their languages and cultures? A good answer respects tribal agency, distinguishes between different communities, and treats constitutional protections as tools that need administration, funding and political will to work.

Key takeaways

  • Census 2011 counted about 10.43 crore Scheduled Tribe people (8.6 per cent of the population) in 705 groups, concentrated in central India and the north-east.
  • The Fifth Schedule covers scheduled areas and the Sixth Schedule provides autonomous councils in parts of the north-east.
  • PESA (1996) extends panchayat self-government to scheduled areas, and the Forest Rights Act (2006) recognises forest rights, but both are incompletely implemented.

Why it matters for civil studies

Tribal policy combines land and forest rights, self-government and development, and its success depends on implementation, not only on law.

Think it through

Should tribal development mean integration, protection of distinct life, or a choice made by each community?
