Make in India and the push for manufacturing

Industry · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Effects of liberalisation on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth; infrastructure: Energy, Ports, Roads, Airports, Railways; investment models.

The Make in India initiative was launched on 25 September 2014 with the aim of facilitating investment, fostering innovation, building high-quality manufacturing infrastructure and making India a hub for manufacturing, design and innovation. It was not a single scheme but an umbrella approach: opening sectors to investment, simplifying procedures, and presenting India as a place where goods could be made competitively for domestic and world markets.

Coordination sits largely with the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade. Under Make in India 2.0, the initiative covers 27 sectors, with manufacturing sector plans coordinated by that department and service sector plans coordinated by the Department of Commerce. This division matters because manufacturing policy touches land, power, logistics, skills and regulation, which are handled by different ministries and by state governments, so a factory decision is rarely the work of one office alone.

Alongside it, the government has used production-linked incentives to encourage firms to scale up production in selected sectors, public procurement preferences for domestically made goods, a national single window system for approvals, and the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan for coordinated infrastructure planning. The argument behind these tools is that incentives alone do not create factories: firms also need reliable power, transport links, clear land titles, skilled workers and predictable clearances.

Critics and supporters debate different measures of success. Supporters point to rising foreign investment interest, new plants in electronics, automobiles and pharmaceuticals, and growth in exports of manufactured goods. Critics ask whether manufacturing has created enough good jobs, whether India has moved up the value chain from assembly to design and components, and whether small firms have shared in the gains. Both questions are legitimate, because an industrial policy can succeed at attracting plants while still falling short on jobs or on technology depth.

For a civil studies reader, the useful frame is to separate intent from instrument and outcome. Make in India states an intent; the instruments are sector plans, incentives, infrastructure and regulatory reform; the outcomes must be judged sector by sector, on jobs, exports, domestic value addition and regional spread, rather than by announcements alone.

Key takeaways

  • Make in India was launched on 25 September 2014 to promote manufacturing, design and innovation in India.
  • Make in India 2.0 covers 27 sectors, split between manufacturing and services coordination.
  • Manufacturing outcomes depend on infrastructure, skills, clearances and markets, not on incentives alone.

Why it matters for civil studies

Industrial policy is a recurring examination theme: it links trade, jobs, federal coordination and infrastructure in a single case study.

Think it through

Which is the better test of Make in India: the number of factories announced, or the share of value added in India? What evidence would you use?
