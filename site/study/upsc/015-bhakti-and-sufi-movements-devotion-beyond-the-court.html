Bhakti and Sufi movements: devotion beyond the court

Medieval India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Indian culture — salient aspects of art forms, literature and architecture from ancient to modern times; history of medieval India.

Between the eighth and seventeenth centuries, two great devotional movements reshaped the religious life of India. The Bhakti movement taught that devotion to a personal god mattered more than birth, ritual, or learning, while the Sufi movement within Islam taught that love of God and closeness to a spiritual guide mattered more than outward observance alone. Both movements spoke in the languages people actually used, and both drew followers across caste and community lines, though neither abolished the social order around them.

The early Bhakti poets of the Tamil south, the Alvars who worshipped Vishnu and the Nayanars who worshipped Shiva, sang in Tamil from about the sixth century onwards. In the north, the movement flowered later with figures such as Kabir, a fifteenth-century weaver-poet who criticised both Hindu and Muslim orthodoxy, Guru Nanak, who founded the Sikh tradition and lived from 1469 to 1539, Mirabai, the Rajput princess who sang of Krishna, and Chaitanya in Bengal and Tukaram in Maharashtra. Their songs are still sung today, and many were collected in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture compiled in 1604.

The Sufi orders, called silsilas, were led by saints whose shrines became places of pilgrimage for Muslims and Hindus alike. Muinuddin Chishti, who settled at Ajmer in the late twelfth century, founded the Chishti order in India, and Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, who died in 1325, drew disciples from the court and the common people. Sufi saints often kept their distance from rulers, though rulers sought their blessing, and Sufi music, poetry, and the practice of sama, listening to devotional music, became a major part of Indian culture.

The movements matter for civil studies because they show change from below. Kings built temples and mosques, but saints built communities, languages, and literatures. They also show the limits of devotional reform: Bhakti and Sufi teachers challenged priestly and scholarly privilege, yet caste and community divisions survived, and later traditions often absorbed the saints into the very institutions they had questioned.

Key takeaways

  • Bhakti taught devotion to a personal god above birth and ritual; Sufi teaching stressed love of God and a spiritual guide.
  • Kabir, Guru Nanak (1469-1539), Mirabai, and the Chishti saints spread these ideas in people's own languages.
  • Both movements built communities and literatures, but neither abolished caste or community divisions.

Why it matters for civil studies

It shows how religious change can come from poets and saints rather than from kings and courts.

Think it through

If Kabir criticised both Hindu and Muslim orthodoxy, why did later communities claim him as their own?
