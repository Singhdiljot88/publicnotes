Migration in India

Indian society: diversity and change · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; effects of globalisation on Indian society.

Migration is the movement of people from one place to another, and it is one of the largest forces reshaping Indian society. According to Census 2011, about 45 crore people in India were migrants by the census definition, which counts a person as a migrant if their place of birth or place of last residence differs from the place of enumeration. Most migration in India is within states and over short distances, often for marriage, and much of it is rural-to-rural or rural-to-urban. Long-distance, interstate migration for work, though smaller in numbers, is highly visible because it concentrates workers in construction, manufacturing, domestic work and services in major cities and industrial regions.

Migration is driven by push and pull factors. Push factors include lack of work, low wages, landlessness, drought and debt in the place of origin; pull factors include jobs, higher wages, education and services in the destination. Seasonal and circular migration is common: workers move for harvests, construction projects or festival seasons and return home, maintaining links in both places. Remittances, money sent home by migrants, support families, pay for schooling and health care, and reduce poverty in origin villages, but they also create dependence and leave villages short of working-age adults, especially men, shifting farm and care work onto women and the elderly.

Migrants face distinctive vulnerabilities. They often lack local ration cards, voter registration, housing security and access to health care and schooling for their children in the destination, because many entitlements are tied to a fixed address. The COVID-19 lockdown of 2020 made this visible nationally, when large numbers of migrant workers walked or travelled long distances home after losing work and shelter. In response, the One Nation One Ration Card scheme was expanded to allow portability of subsidised food grains across states, and labour codes and welfare boards were debated as ways to protect migrant workers, though coverage remains incomplete.

For civil studies, migration connects development, federalism and rights. A worker's rights should not disappear when they cross a state border, yet administration is organised state by state. Good policy therefore needs portable entitlements, reliable data on seasonal and circular migrants, safe housing and transport, and protection from exploitation by contractors. It also needs to recognise that migration is not a problem to be stopped: it is how people seek better lives, and how regions share labour, skills and income. The question is how to make it safe and rewarding, not how to prevent it.

Key takeaways

  • Census 2011 counted about 45 crore migrants by place of birth or last residence; most migration is within states and short-distance, often for marriage.
  • Push factors include lack of work and debt at origin; pull factors include jobs, wages and services at destination; remittances support origin families.
  • The 2020 lockdown exposed migrants' lack of portable entitlements, and One Nation One Ration Card was expanded to allow food-grain portability across states.

Why it matters for civil studies

Migration reshapes labour, cities and villages, but entitlements tied to a fixed address often fail mobile workers.

Think it through

Should welfare entitlements follow the person across state borders, or remain tied to their home state?
