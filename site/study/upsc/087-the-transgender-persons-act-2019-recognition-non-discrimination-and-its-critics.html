The Transgender Persons Act, 2019: recognition, non-discrimination and its critics

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 followed the Supreme Court's decision in National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India in 2014, which recognised transgender persons as a third gender and directed governments to frame welfare schemes for them. The Act defines a transgender person as a person whose gender does not match the gender assigned at birth. It includes trans-men and trans-women, persons with intersex variations, genderqueer persons and persons with socio-cultural identities such as kinnar and hijra.

The Act prohibits discrimination against transgender persons in education, employment, healthcare, access to goods, facilities and opportunities available to the public, the right to movement, the right to reside, rent or occupy property, the opportunity to hold public or private office, and access to establishments in whose care or custody a transgender person is. It provides that a transgender person has the right to reside with parents and immediate family members, and that a person shall not be separated from family on the ground of being transgender, except by a court order in the person's interest.

Recognition under the Act works through a certificate of identity. A transgender person may apply to the District Magistrate for a certificate indicating gender as transgender. A revised certificate, indicating male or female, may be obtained after undergoing surgery to change gender. The Act also establishes the National Council for Transgender Persons, chaired by the Union Minister for Social Justice and Empowerment, to advise the Central Government, monitor policies and programmes, and redress grievances.

The Act has been criticised on the ground that it makes recognition depend on an official certificate, rather than on self-identification alone, and that it does not provide reservation. Supporters reply that a statutory framework, a non-discrimination rule and a national council are necessary first steps, and that welfare and employment measures can be built on them. The debate is therefore about what recognition should mean in law: a declaration of identity, a document that unlocks services, or both.

Key takeaways

  • The 2019 Act follows the Supreme Court's 2014 NALSA decision and defines transgender persons to include trans-men, trans-women, persons with intersex variations and kinnar and hijra identities.
  • It prohibits discrimination in education, employment, healthcare, housing, movement and public office, and protects residence with family.
  • Recognition is through a District Magistrate certificate, and the Act creates the National Council for Transgender Persons.

Why it matters for civil studies

Transgender law raises a precise question for civil studies: does legal recognition come from self-identification, from a state certificate, or from the services that recognition unlocks?

Think it through

Should a certificate of identity be a gateway to rights, or should self-perceived identity alone be enough for the state to act on?
