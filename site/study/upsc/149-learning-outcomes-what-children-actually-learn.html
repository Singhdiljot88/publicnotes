UPSC foundations · Note 149

Learning outcomes: what children actually learn

Health, education and human development · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Issues relating to development and management of Social Sector/Services relating to Health, Education, Human Resources. Also touches GS III: Indian Economy — growth, development and employment.

Enrolment is the easiest education statistic to improve, and India has largely achieved it at primary level. Learning is harder, and the Annual Status of Education Report (ASER), a citizen-led household survey of rural India run by the NGO Pratham, has documented the gap for two decades. Its headline measure is simple: can a child in Class 3 read a Class 2 level text, and can they do basic subtraction? The answers show how far schooling and learning have diverged.

ASER 2024, which surveyed about 6.5 lakh children across 605 rural districts, found improvement after the pandemic slump. Among government-school Class 3 children, the share able to read a Class 2 level text rose from 16.3 per cent in 2022 to 23.4 per cent in 2024, above the 2018 level of 20.9 per cent. Among government-school Class 5 children, the share rose from 38.5 per cent in 2022 to 44.8 per cent in 2024. Arithmetic improved too. The recovery is real, and ASER notes that gains of this size are unusual in its history.

The levels, however, remain low: even after the recovery, roughly three in four government-school Class 3 children cannot read a Class 2 text, and more than half of Class 5 children cannot. The causes are familiar: weak foundational teaching in the early grades, multi-grade classrooms, teacher shortages, and the practice of promoting children regardless of learning, which lets gaps compound year by year. The pandemic widened the gap, since school closures hit children without devices, connectivity or literate adults at home hardest.

Policy has responded with a foundational focus. NIPUN Bharat, launched in 2021 under the National Education Policy, 2020, aims for universal foundational literacy and numeracy by the end of Class 3, with teacher training, teaching materials and regular assessment. The ASER recovery suggests that focused attention on the early grades can work, but the scale of the remaining gap means the mission's success will be judged over years, not in one survey round. For civil studies, ASER is a reminder to ask, of any education claim, not how many children are in school, but what they can do.

Key takeaways

  • ASER 2024: government-school Class 3 reading rose from 16.3 per cent (2022) to 23.4 per cent (2024); Class 5 rose to 44.8 per cent.
  • Even after recovery, about three in four government-school Class 3 children cannot read a Class 2 text.
  • NIPUN Bharat (2021) targets universal foundational literacy and numeracy by the end of Class 3.

Why it matters for civil studies

Learning outcomes, not enrolment, are the real measure of an education system.

Think it through

Should children be held back until they master basics, or promoted with support — and which harms learning less?
