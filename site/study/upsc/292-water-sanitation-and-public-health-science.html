Water, sanitation and public health science

General science in everyday life and emerging technology · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; awareness in the fields of IT, Space, Computers, robotics, nano-technology, bio-technology.

Clean water and sanitation have saved more lives than most medical advances, and the science behind them is straightforward. Disease-causing microbes reach people mainly through contaminated water, food, hands, and flies. The nineteenth-century germ theory of disease, advanced by researchers such as Louis Pasteur, replaced the older idea that disease came from bad air, and pointed to practical defences: remove microbes from water, keep faeces away from drinking supplies, and wash hands. Cities that built sewers and water treatment saw dramatic falls in cholera, typhoid, and dysentery, before antibiotics existed.

Water treatment follows a sequence. Screening and settling remove large particles; filtration through sand or membranes removes smaller ones; disinfection — usually with chlorine, or with ultraviolet light — kills remaining microbes. Chlorine is used worldwide because it is cheap, effective, and leaves a residual that keeps protecting water in pipes. Where central treatment is unavailable, boiling water, or treating it with chlorine tablets and safe storage, achieves much of the same protection. The key insight is that water can look clear and still be dangerous: microbes are invisible, so treatment must be routine, not based on appearance.

Sanitation works by breaking the faecal-oral route: the path by which microbes from faeces reach a new person's mouth. A toilet that safely contains waste, combined with handwashing with soap after use and before eating, interrupts that path. Soap matters because it lifts grease and microbes from skin far more effectively than water alone. Studies of public health consistently find handwashing one of the cheapest and most effective health interventions available, which is why it features in school health programmes and epidemic guidance worldwide.

Rehydration shows how simple science saves lives in emergencies. Diarrhoea kills mainly by dehydrating the body, and oral rehydration solution — a precise mixture of clean water, salts, and sugar — exploits the gut's mechanism for absorbing salt and glucose together, pulling water with them. The World Health Organization promotes oral rehydration salts as a frontline treatment, and it can be given at home without medical equipment. The broader lesson is that public health advances often depend less on new inventions than on applying known science widely: clean water, toilets, soap, and rehydration reach everyone, or they fail the people who need them most.

Key takeaways

  • Germ theory (19th century) showed microbes, not bad air, cause diseases such as cholera and typhoid.
  • Water treatment combines settling, filtration, and disinfection; clear-looking water can still be unsafe.
  • Sanitation breaks the faecal-oral route; soap handwashing and oral rehydration salts are among the cheapest life-saving measures.

Why it matters for civil studies

It explains why infrastructure and behaviour — pipes, toilets, soap — are health policy, not just engineering.

Think it through

Why is handwashing with soap more effective than rinsing with water alone, and when does it matter most?
