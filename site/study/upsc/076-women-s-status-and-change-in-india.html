Women's Status and Change in India

Indian society: diversity and change · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; effects of globalisation on Indian society.

Women's status in India has changed significantly since independence, but unevenly. The Constitution guarantees equality before the law (Article 14), prohibits discrimination on grounds of sex (Article 15), and guarantees equal opportunity in public employment (Article 16). It also permits special provision for women and children, and later amendments reserved one-third of seats in panchayats and municipalities for women, a provision later extended in some states to half. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023, provides for reservation of one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, to take effect after delimitation following the next census, so its practical effect is still to come.

Education and work show both progress and persistent gaps. Girls' enrolment in school has risen sharply, and women now form a large share of university students in many fields, yet women's participation in paid work, as measured by official labour surveys, remains lower than men's and is concentrated in agriculture and informal work. Many women work long hours in unpaid domestic and care work, which is not counted as employment in standard measures. Violence against women, including domestic violence, dowry-related violence and sexual assault, remains a serious problem, addressed in part by the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and by criminal law reforms, including those following the 2012 Delhi gang-rape case.

Social norms are changing at different speeds in different places. Urban, educated women are more likely to delay marriage, work outside the home and make independent decisions, while in many rural and poorer communities, early marriage, son preference and restrictions on mobility persist. Sex-selective practices, reflected in skewed child sex ratios in some states, have been addressed by the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994, but enforcement remains difficult. Women's movements, from the Chipko movement's women participants to contemporary campaigns for safety, equal pay and political representation, have been central to pushing change.

For civil studies, women's status is a cross-cutting issue, not a separate topic. It affects education, health, labour, agriculture, politics and law, and it cannot be measured by a single indicator. A good answer looks at rights, education, work, health, safety and representation together, and asks why progress in one area, such as schooling, does not automatically produce progress in another, such as paid work or safety. It also asks whose women are being discussed: class, caste, tribe, religion and region all shape women's experiences differently.

Key takeaways

  • The Constitution guarantees equality (Articles 14–16) and permits special provision for women; the 2023 amendment provides one-third reservation for women in legislatures after delimitation following the next census.
  • Girls' school enrolment has risen sharply, but women's paid work participation remains lower than men's and much unpaid care work is uncounted.
  • The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and the sex-selection prohibition Act of 1994 are key legal responses to violence and skewed sex ratios.

Why it matters for civil studies

Gender equality cuts across education, work, health, safety and politics, and progress in one area does not guarantee progress in others.

Think it through

Why does rising girls' education not automatically lead to equal paid work and safety for women?
