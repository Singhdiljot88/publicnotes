UPSC foundations · Note 265

Border management and coastal security

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

India has land borders with Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Myanmar and Afghanistan (through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir, in India's official position), and a coastline of more than 7,500 kilometres including the island territories. Different borders need different management: the western border with Pakistan is heavily fenced and militarised in places, the northern border with China is high-altitude and disputed in places, the eastern borders with Bangladesh and Myanmar are porous and crossed by communities with shared ethnicity and trade, and the Nepal and Bhutan borders are open under treaty arrangements.

The forces are organised by border. The Border Security Force guards the borders with Pakistan and Bangladesh, the Indo-Tibetan Border Police guards the border with China, the Sashastra Seema Bal guards the borders with Nepal and Bhutan, and the Assam Rifles operates in the north-east, including along the Myanmar border. The Indian Coast Guard, the Navy and marine police share coastal security, with the Coast Guard as the lead agency for coastal policing coordination. The Border Roads Organisation builds and maintains roads in border areas, which are both development and strategic assets.

Coastal security was reorganised after the 2008 Mumbai attacks, in which attackers arrived by sea. Reforms included coastal police stations, joint coastal patrols, vessel tracking, identity cards for fishers and coordination between the Navy, Coast Guard, marine police and intelligence agencies. Smuggling, illegal fishing and unmonitored small vessels remain gaps, because a coastline with thousands of fishing boats cannot be sealed like a land fence; it must be monitored through registration, community reporting and technology.

Border management is also a human question. Fencing, checkposts and patrols affect farmers, traders and families who live astride borders. A border policy that only counts intrusions and seizures, without counting the livelihoods it disrupts, will create resentment in the very communities whose cooperation border forces need most.

Key takeaways

  • Different Indian borders — fenced, high-altitude, porous and open — need different forces and methods.
  • BSF, ITBP, SSB and Assam Rifles divide border duties, with the Coast Guard leading coastal coordination.
  • Coastal security was reorganised after the 2008 Mumbai attacks, but small-vessel monitoring remains a gap.

Why it matters for civil studies

Border policy joins defence, policing, trade, migration and the daily lives of border communities.

Think it through

Should border security be judged by how sealed a border is, or by how safely and legally border communities can live and trade?
