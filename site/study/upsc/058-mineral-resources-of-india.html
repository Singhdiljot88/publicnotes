Mineral Resources of India

Climate, rivers and natural resources of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Distribution of key natural resources across the world (including South Asia and the Indian subcontinent); important geophysical phenomena.

India is rich in several minerals but not in all. It has large reserves of iron ore, coal, bauxite, manganese, mica and limestone, and smaller reserves of copper, lead, zinc, gold and other metals. The distribution is uneven: the Chotanagpur plateau and Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh hold much of the coal and iron ore; Karnataka and Odisha hold major bauxite deposits; Rajasthan holds lead, zinc and marble; and the western offshore region, around Mumbai High, is a major source of oil and gas. This geography explains where mines, steel plants and power stations are located, and why some districts are mining districts for generations.

Minerals are classified by use and by law. Metallic minerals, such as iron ore and bauxite, are used for metals; non-metallic minerals, such as limestone and mica, are used in construction and industry; and energy minerals, such as coal, oil and natural gas, are used for fuel. Under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, major minerals are regulated by the central government and minor minerals by state governments, while the 2015 amendment introduced auction as the main method for granting mineral concessions, replacing the earlier discretionary allocation system. Auctions were intended to increase transparency and state revenue after controversies over coal and other allocations.

Mining brings both revenue and harm. It provides raw material for steel, cement, power and construction, employs large numbers of people directly and indirectly, and contributes royalties to state governments. It also clears forests, displaces villages, pollutes air and water, and leaves abandoned pits and waste dumps. Mining in forest and tribal areas raises the sharpest questions, because the people who bear the costs often see the least benefit, and rehabilitation has frequently been slow or incomplete. Environmental clearance, forest clearance and consent of local bodies are therefore not formalities but the main safeguards for affected communities.

For civil studies, minerals are a test of how a country turns natural wealth into public benefit. The questions are practical: how concessions are granted, how royalties are shared, how environmental damage is prevented and repaired, and how mining districts are developed after the ore runs out. A mineral-rich district that remains poor in health, education and infrastructure has not failed geologically; it has failed institutionally. That distinction is the core of most good answers on India's mineral economy.

Key takeaways

  • India is well endowed with iron ore, coal, bauxite, manganese, mica and limestone, concentrated in the Chotanagpur plateau and central India.
  • The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, governs major minerals, and the 2015 amendment made auction the main route for concessions.
  • Mining provides raw materials and revenue but also causes displacement, forest loss and pollution, especially in tribal areas.

Why it matters for civil studies

Mineral geography shapes industrial location, state revenue and conflict over land, forests and rehabilitation.

Think it through

Should mineral wealth belong mainly to the state where it is found, to the whole country, or to the community living above it?
