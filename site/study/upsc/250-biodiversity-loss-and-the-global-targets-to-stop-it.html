Biodiversity loss and the global targets to stop it

Biodiversity · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation, environmental impact assessment (biodiversity conservation).

Biodiversity loss has five main direct drivers, recognised by global assessments: habitat change, overexploitation, climate change, pollution and invasive alien species. They interact: a forest fragmented by a road is easier to log, more prone to fire, more open to invasive plants and less able to support wide-ranging animals. Behind them sit indirect drivers — consumption patterns, trade, subsidies, population growth and weak governance — which are harder to legislate but often more powerful.

The Convention on Biological Diversity, adopted in 1992, is the main global treaty. Its earlier Aichi Targets for 2011 to 2020 were, by broad consensus, largely missed. In December 2022, parties adopted the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework at the fifteenth Conference of the Parties in Montreal. It sets four goals for 2050 and 23 targets for 2030, including the headline 30 by 30 ambition: conserve at least 30 per cent of land, inland waters and seas, and restore at least 30 per cent of degraded ecosystems.

The framework also calls for reducing harmful subsidies by at least 500 billion US dollars a year by 2030, mobilising 200 billion dollars a year for biodiversity from all sources, including 30 billion a year in international finance for developing countries, and cutting the introduction of invasive alien species by at least half. These finance and subsidy figures are targets, not money already delivered, and progress reports have warned that finance remains the weakest area.

For India, the framework means updating its national biodiversity strategy and action plan, expanding and connecting protected areas, restoring degraded land and reporting progress in a comparable way. The test will not be whether a target is repeated in a document, but whether degraded land is actually restored, whether a subsidy that harms nature is actually removed, and whether local people gain from conservation rather than only bearing its costs.

Key takeaways

  • Habitat loss, overuse, climate change, pollution and invasive species are the main direct drivers of biodiversity loss.
  • The Kunming-Montreal framework (2022) sets 23 targets for 2030, including 30 by 30 conservation and restoration.
  • Finance and harmful-subsidy reform are the framework's most contested delivery areas.

Why it matters for civil studies

Global biodiversity targets translate into Indian land, water, budget and subsidy decisions, so they are domestic policy, not distant diplomacy.

Think it through

Is a global percentage target such as 30 by 30 useful, or does it hide whether the protected 30 per cent is the land that most needs protection?
