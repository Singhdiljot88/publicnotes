The Indian Monsoon: How It Works

Climate, rivers and natural resources of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Distribution of key natural resources across the world (including South Asia and the Indian subcontinent); important geophysical phenomena.

The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds. In summer, the Indian landmass heats up faster than the surrounding oceans, creating low pressure over the interior, especially over north-west India. Moist air from the Indian Ocean then flows towards this low pressure, bringing rain. In winter, the pattern reverses: the land cools faster than the sea, high pressure builds over the interior, and dry winds blow outwards from land to sea. This annual reversal, driven by differential heating of land and sea, is the basic mechanism of the monsoon, although the full system also involves the shifting Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, the Tibetan Plateau's heating, and jet streams in the upper atmosphere.

The south-west monsoon, India's main rainy season, runs from June to September and supplies roughly three-quarters of the country's annual rainfall. It normally arrives over Kerala around 1 June, a moment often called the burst of the monsoon because rainfall increases suddenly after the hot, dry summer. The monsoon then advances in two branches: the Arabian Sea branch strikes the Western Ghats and brings heavy rain to the west coast, while the Bay of Bengal branch moves towards the north-east and then westwards along the Ganga plain. By mid-July the monsoon usually covers almost the whole country.

Rainfall is very unevenly distributed. The west coast and the north-east receive very heavy rain, while parts of Rajasthan, Gujarat and the Deccan rain shadow receive far less. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya are among the wettest places on Earth, while the Thar Desert is among the driest parts of India. Breaks in the monsoon, when rain pauses for days, and intense bursts, when a month's rain falls in a day, are both normal features of the system, and both matter for farming: a break at sowing time or a flood at harvest time can damage a crop even in a year of normal total rainfall.

For civil studies, the monsoon is the hinge of India's economy and public policy. Agriculture, food prices, rural incomes, reservoir levels, hydropower and even inflation are all sensitive to its timing and distribution. That is why the India Meteorological Department, founded in 1875, issues monsoon forecasts and onset declarations that are watched across the country. A good civil studies answer does not treat the monsoon as weather trivia; it traces a chain from wind and pressure to sowing, harvest, markets, water storage and relief, and asks how policy can reduce dependence on a single, variable season.

Key takeaways

  • The monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal caused mainly by differential heating of land and sea, with low pressure over land in summer drawing in moist ocean air.
  • The south-west monsoon (June–September) supplies about three-quarters of India's rainfall, normally reaching Kerala around 1 June and covering the country by mid-July.
  • Rainfall is highly uneven, and breaks and bursts matter as much as the seasonal total for farming and water policy.

Why it matters for civil studies

The monsoon links atmosphere, agriculture, food prices and water storage, so its forecast is a national economic event.

Think it through

Can India reduce its vulnerability to monsoon variability, or will farming always depend on a good monsoon?
