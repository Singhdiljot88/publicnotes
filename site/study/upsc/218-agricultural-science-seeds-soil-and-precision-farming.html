UPSC foundations · Note 218

Agricultural science: seeds, soil and precision farming

Science · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life; achievements of Indians in science & technology; indigenisation of technology and developing new technology.

Agricultural science in India is organised largely through the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, founded in 1929, which coordinates research institutes and agricultural universities across the country. Its work spans crop breeding, soil science, animal husbandry, fisheries and farm machinery, and it is the institutional backbone behind the high-yielding varieties of the Green Revolution, which raised wheat and rice output sharply from the late 1960s. That history shapes a continuing expectation: public research should raise yields for small farmers, not only for commercial farms.

Seed breeding remains the core. Varieties are bred for yield, drought or flood tolerance, pest resistance and shorter duration, and they must be tested across India's varied soils and climates before release. Public breeding is especially important for crops and regions that private seed firms find less profitable, such as millets, pulses and rain-fed areas. The Soil Health Card scheme, launched in 2015, gives farmers information on their soil's nutrients so that fertiliser use can be matched to need rather than applied by habit, reducing cost and runoff.

Precision farming adds a new layer: satellite imagery, soil sensors, weather data and drones help farmers decide where to irrigate, spray or harvest, rather than treating a whole field uniformly. The promise is lower input use and higher output, but the barriers are practical: small and fragmented holdings, the cost of equipment and data, connectivity in villages, and the need for advice in local languages. Technology that works on a large farm may fail on a two-hectare plot unless it is adapted to that scale.

Climate change sharpens the agenda. Rising temperatures, shifting rainfall and more frequent extremes require varieties and practices that tolerate stress, conserve water and protect soil. Research on millets, which need less water than rice, on direct-seeded rice, and on agroforestry reflects this shift from maximising a single crop's yield to making whole farming systems resilient.

For civil studies, agricultural science is best judged by adoption, not by invention. A variety or technique matters when farmers actually use it, when it survives a bad season, and when it raises income after costs. That test keeps the focus on extension services, credit, markets and local knowledge, without which even good science stays in the laboratory.

Key takeaways

  • The Indian Council of Agricultural Research, founded in 1929, coordinates crop, soil and animal research.
  • The Soil Health Card scheme, launched in 2015, matches fertiliser use to measured soil nutrients.
  • Precision farming promises input savings, but small holdings and cost limit adoption.

Why it matters for civil studies

Agricultural science links research institutions, climate adaptation and small-farmer livelihoods.

Think it through

Should public breeding prioritise maximum yield, or resilience to drought and heat? Which helps small farmers more?
