Disinvestment and asset monetisation: selling stakes, leasing assets

Public finance · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Government Budgeting.

Disinvestment means the government sells part or all of its equity in a public sector enterprise, reducing its ownership share. It differs from privatisation in degree: minority disinvestment retains government control, while strategic disinvestment transfers management control to a private buyer. The stated aims have included raising non-tax revenue, improving enterprise efficiency through market discipline, reducing the fiscal burden of loss-making firms, and freeing resources for public investment. Critics argue that profitable firms are sold to meet deficit targets, that assets are undervalued, and that workers and strategic interests suffer.

India's disinvestment history includes the early minority sales of the 1990s, the strategic sales of the early 2000s, including to private buyers in sectors such as aluminium and hotels, and later buybacks, exchange-traded funds of public enterprises and minority offers. Proceeds have fluctuated sharply with market conditions and political willingness, and targets have often been missed. The debate is not only about price: it is about whether the state should own airlines, hotels, mines or banks at all, and whether private ownership improves service and investment or merely transfers monopoly profits to private hands.

Asset monetisation is a related but distinct idea. Instead of selling ownership, the government leases existing infrastructure, such as roads, railways, power transmission lines or warehouses, to private operators for a fixed period in return for upfront or annual payments, while retaining ownership. The National Monetisation Pipeline, announced in 2021, set out such leasing opportunities across sectors. Supporters argue that monetisation unlocks value from idle or underused assets without selling them, and that private operators may maintain them better. Critics warn about user charges rising, contracts favouring operators, and the risk that short-term receipts hide long-term loss of public revenue.

For civil studies, both policies test the state's role as owner and regulator. Selling a firm or leasing an asset changes who controls a public resource, who profits from it and who is accountable when service fails. Transparent valuation, competitive bidding, worker protection and continued regulation of monopolies matter more than the label given to the transaction. A policy judged only by receipts raised in one Budget year will miss these longer consequences.

Key takeaways

  • Disinvestment sells government equity; minority sales retain control, while strategic sales transfer management control.
  • Asset monetisation leases public assets to private operators for a period while the government retains ownership.
  • The National Monetisation Pipeline, announced in 2021, set out leasing opportunities across infrastructure sectors.

Why it matters for civil studies

Sales and leases of public assets convert long-term public wealth into one-time receipts, so their terms matter for future generations.

Think it through

Should the government sell a profitable public firm to fund a deficit, lease its assets instead, or keep both, and how would you judge the price?
