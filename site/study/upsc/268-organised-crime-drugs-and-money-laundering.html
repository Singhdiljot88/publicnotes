Organised crime, drugs and money laundering

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

Organised crime differs from ordinary crime in its structure: it is planned, continuing and profit-driven, whether it involves drug trafficking, arms smuggling, human trafficking, extortion, counterfeit currency or illegal mining and sand extraction. Its danger is not only the crime itself but the corruption and violence it buys: a drug network that bribes officials, intimidates witnesses and launders profits can hollow out local policing and politics even if its members rarely commit a visible violent act.

Drug trafficking in India is shaped by geography. India lies between two major opium-producing regions, the Golden Crescent to the west and the Golden Triangle to the east, and it also produces licit opium under government licence for medicines, which creates a diversion risk. The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985, is the main law, enforced by the Narcotics Control Bureau, state police, customs and other agencies. The Act distinguishes, in its penalties, between small and commercial quantities, and provides for treatment and rehabilitation alongside punishment, though critics argue enforcement still falls heavily on users and couriers rather than kingpins.

Money laundering converts crime proceeds into apparently legitimate assets. The Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002, enforced by the Directorate of Enforcement, criminalises laundering, allows attachment of property and reverses aspects of the burden of proof in certain proceedings. Its powers have been upheld in broad terms by the Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2022), though the judgment and later amendments remain debated on bail and on the breadth of the Enforcement Directorate's powers. Financial intelligence from banks, the Financial Intelligence Unit and international cooperation through the Financial Action Task Force are central to tracing laundered money.

The policy lesson is that organised crime is a market. As long as demand for drugs, arms or smuggled goods exists and profits exceed risks, new networks replace arrested ones. Reducing demand, protecting witnesses, tracing money and cleaning up the corruption that shields networks matter as much as seizures, whose totals can rise even while the trade grows.

Key takeaways

  • Organised crime is continuing, profit-driven crime that can corrupt institutions, not only break laws.
  • The NDPS Act, 1985, governs drug offences, with the Narcotics Control Bureau as a central enforcement agency.
  • The Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002, targets crime proceeds through attachment and prosecution by the Enforcement Directorate.

Why it matters for civil studies

Organised crime links policing to finance, health, corruption and international cooperation.

Think it through

Should drug policy prioritise punishing traffickers, treating users, or reducing demand — and can it do all three at once?
