UPSC foundations · Note 026

Education, the press, and new ideas before 1857

Modern India to 1857 · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Modern Indian history from about the middle of the eighteenth century until the present — significant events, personalities, issues.

British rule brought new institutions of learning and communication that changed how Indians thought about their own society. The Calcutta Madrasa was founded in 1781 and the Sanskrit College at Benares in 1791, but the great debate was over the language and content of higher education. The English Education Act of 1835, following Thomas Macaulay's famous Minute, committed the government to teaching English and western learning, on the argument that it would create a class of Indians who could serve as intermediaries between the rulers and the ruled.

Wood's Despatch of 1854, sent by the President of the Board of Control, Charles Wood, laid out a fuller plan: departments of public instruction in each province, universities, and a system of schools from primary to university level. The first three universities, at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, were founded in 1857, in the very year of the revolt. Missionary schools, government schools, and Indian-run schools all grew in this period, and a new educated class, often called the middle class or bhadralok in Bengal, began to write, teach, and organise associations.

The press grew alongside education. James Augustus Hickey's Bengal Gazette, founded in 1780, was India's first newspaper, and Indian-owned papers followed, including Raja Rammohan Roy's Sambad Kaumudi, founded in 1821. Newspapers, pamphlets, and journals let Indians debate sati, widow remarriage, and British policy in public, and they created a reading public that stretched across provinces. The government watched the press closely and would later restrict it, but before 1857 the Indian press was already a forum for criticism.

Education and the press did not produce a single opinion. Some educated Indians admired British institutions and wanted reform within the system, others used western ideas of liberty and nationalism to criticise British rule itself, and still others rejected western learning altogether. The importance of the period is that it created the tools, schools, books, newspapers, and associations, with which later nationalists would build a movement.

Key takeaways

  • Macaulay's Minute of 1835 committed government education to English and western learning.
  • Wood's Despatch of 1854 planned a school-to-university system; the first three universities followed in 1857.
  • The Indian press, from Sambad Kaumudi in 1821, created a public forum for debate before 1857.

Why it matters for civil studies

It explains where the later nationalist movement got its organisers, its newspapers, and its arguments.

Think it through

Did English education liberate Indian minds, bind them to British rule, or do both at once?
