The Inter-State Council: a forum for Centre-State coordination under Article 263

Constitution and political system · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution — historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure.

Article 263 allows the President to establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by it. The Council may inquire into and advise on disputes between states, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action. The Inter-State Council was established in 1990, following the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State relations, which had argued that India's federal system needed a permanent forum for consultation, not only ad hoc meetings.

The Council is chaired by the Prime Minister and includes chief ministers of all states, chief ministers of union territories with legislatures, administrators of other union territories, and six Union ministers nominated by the Prime Minister. Its composition is its point: it brings the Union and the states to the same table, rather than leaving coordination to bilateral correspondence or to litigation. Its recommendations are advisory, not binding, which means its influence depends on political willingness to act on them.

The Council works alongside other federal institutions, including the Finance Commission under Article 280, the Goods and Services Tax Council under Article 279A, and zonal councils established by statute. The distinction matters. The Finance Commission recommends the sharing of revenues, and the GST Council decides on many indirect tax matters, while the Inter-State Council is a general forum for disputes and coordination. It is not a court and it does not allocate funds.

Critics note that the Council has met infrequently over the decades, which limits its role as a standing forum. Supporters reply that even infrequent meetings can settle principles on subjects such as internal security, education or the use of central forces, and that the existence of the forum matters when a dispute arises. For civil studies, the Council illustrates a recurring federal question: whether coordination should be left to politics, structured through a council, or settled by a court.

Key takeaways

  • Article 263 allows the President to establish an Inter-State Council to advise on disputes between states and subjects of common interest.
  • The Council, established in 1990 after the Sarkaria Commission, is chaired by the Prime Minister and includes all chief ministers.
  • Its recommendations are advisory, unlike the Finance Commission's revenue recommendations or the GST Council's tax decisions.

Why it matters for civil studies

The Inter-State Council shows that federalism needs a meeting room as well as a courtroom: some disputes are better talked through than litigated.

Think it through

If the Council's recommendations are only advisory, what would make governments take them seriously — regular meetings, a published agenda, or a duty to respond?
