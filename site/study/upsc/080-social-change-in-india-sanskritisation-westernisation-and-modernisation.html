Social Change in India: Sanskritisation, Westernisation and Modernisation

Indian society: diversity and change · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of Indian Society, Diversity of India; effects of globalisation on Indian society.

Indian sociologists have developed distinctive concepts to describe social change, because change in India has rarely followed a single Western pattern. M. N. Srinivas, one of India's most influential sociologists, coined the term sanskritisation to describe a process by which a lower caste, tribe or other group adopts the customs, rituals and lifestyle of a higher, usually twice-born, caste in order to raise its status. A group might adopt vegetarianism, change its rituals, or claim a new caste name, and over generations, its social position might improve. Sanskritisation changes the position of a group within the hierarchy, but it does not challenge the hierarchy itself; it accepts the ranking and seeks a higher place within it.

Westernisation, another concept from Srinivas, describes changes resulting from British rule and later contact with Western culture: new technology, education, law, administration, dress, food and ideas about equality and individual rights. Unlike sanskritisation, westernisation was not confined to caste hierarchy; it affected all communities and introduced institutions, such as modern courts, schools and bureaucracy, that operated on principles different from traditional ones. Modernisation is a broader term, used for the process by which societies adopt scientific technology, industrial production, urban life, mass education and rational, rule-based institutions. It is often associated with, but not identical to, westernisation, since non-Western societies, including Japan and later China, have modernised in their own ways.

These concepts help explain change that looks contradictory. A community might sanskritise its rituals while also sending its children to English-medium schools and seeking government jobs, combining tradition and modernity in the same household. Caste associations might use modern organisations, elections and media to advance traditional identities. Women might adopt new dress and work while maintaining family rituals. Sociologists therefore warn against a simple story of tradition giving way to modernity: Indian society shows persistence, adaptation and invention together, with old forms taking on new functions and new forms being given old meanings.

For civil studies, these concepts are tools for analysis, not labels to apply mechanically. When a policy aims to change behaviour, whether on sanitation, education, gender or health, it works through existing social structures and meanings, not on a blank slate. A programme that ignores caste, kinship and religious practice may fail, while one that works with community leaders and local norms may succeed, though it may also reinforce inequality. Understanding sanskritisation, westernisation and modernisation helps a reader ask a sharper question: is a change raising a group's position, changing the rules of the game, or doing both at once?

Key takeaways

  • Sanskritisation (M. N. Srinivas) is a lower group adopting higher-caste customs to raise its status within the hierarchy, without challenging the hierarchy itself.
  • Westernisation describes changes from British rule and Western contact, including new law, education and administration; modernisation is broader, involving technology, industry, urban life and rule-based institutions.
  • Indian change often combines tradition and modernity in the same household, community or organisation, rather than replacing one with the other.

Why it matters for civil studies

Concepts of social change help explain why reforms work through, and are reshaped by, existing caste, kinship and religious structures.

Think it through

Is social change in India mainly about groups rising within a hierarchy, or about changing the hierarchy itself?
