UPSC foundations · Note 136

Women and work in India

Development, poverty and employment · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Inclusive growth and issues arising from it; issues relating to poverty and hunger. Also touches GS I: poverty and developmental issues.

For decades, one of the most striking facts about India's labour market was how few women were counted as working. That has changed sharply in recent data. The Periodic Labour Force Survey reported female labour force participation for women aged 15 and above rising from 23.3 per cent in 2017-18 to 41.7 per cent in 2023-24, with the female worker population ratio rising from 22.0 per cent to 40.3 per cent. The increase was driven mainly by rural women, and male participation in the same year was far higher, at about 78.8 per cent, leaving a gap of more than 35 percentage points.

The headline rise needs careful reading. Analysts, including a UNFPA analysis of the same survey, note that much of the increase is concentrated in informal, self-employed and unpaid family work, particularly in agriculture, where the share of rural women working in farming rose from 71.1 per cent in 2018-19 to 76.9 per cent in 2023-24. Such work is real — it feeds families and produces output — but it is often unpaid, insecure and without social protection, and it may partly reflect distress: when household incomes are squeezed, more family members, including women, take up available work.

The barriers to better-paid, formal work for women are well documented. Unpaid care work — childcare, elder care, cooking, fetching water and fuel — falls mostly on women and limits the hours and distance they can work. Safety on transport and at workplaces, the absence of childcare, and social norms about suitable work all narrow choices, especially in towns and cities, where female participation remains much lower than in villages. Legal protections exist, including the Maternity Benefit Act, amended in 2017 to provide 26 weeks of paid maternity leave in covered establishments, but most women work outside the establishments the law reaches.

The stakes are large. Raising women's participation in quality work is not only a fairness issue: it raises household incomes, improves children's health and schooling, and adds to national output. Policy levers include childcare, safe transport, skills training, credit for women entrepreneurs, and enforcement of workplace safety. The test of progress is not only the participation rate, but the share of women in regular salaried work, in higher-paying occupations, and in decision-making roles.

Key takeaways

  • Female labour force participation rose from 23.3 per cent (2017-18) to 41.7 per cent (2023-24), driven by rural women.
  • Much of the rise is in informal, self-employed and unpaid family work, especially in agriculture.
  • Care burdens, safety, transport and norms still limit women's access to formal, better-paid work.

Why it matters for civil studies

Women's work links growth, poverty, demography and gender equality in a single indicator.

Think it through

Should a rise in unpaid family work count as empowerment, distress, or both — and how would you tell?
