India and UN Security Council reform: the G4 case for a permanent seat

International relations and India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — India and its neighbourhood — relations; bilateral, regional and global groupings and agreements involving India and/or affecting India's interests; effect of policies and politics of developed and developing countries on India's interests, Indian Diaspora.

The United Nations Security Council has five permanent members — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States — each with a veto over substantive resolutions, and ten non-permanent members elected for two-year terms. Its permanent membership reflects the power structure of 1945, when the United Nations was founded, rather than the distribution of population, economic weight and peacekeeping contribution today. India, which is not a permanent member, has argued for decades that the Council must be reformed to reflect contemporary realities.

India's case rests on several grounds: it is the world's most populous country, a large economy, a major contributor of troops to United Nations peacekeeping operations, and a country whose decisions affect climate, health, trade and development for a large share of humanity. India has served multiple terms as a non-permanent member, most recently in 2021 to 2022, but non-permanent membership does not carry a veto and does not allow a country to shape the Council's agenda in the same way as permanent membership.

India pursues reform through the G4, a grouping of Brazil, Germany, India and Japan, each of which seeks a permanent seat for itself in an expanded Council. The G4 proposal envisages expanding the Council from fifteen to around twenty-five members, adding six new permanent seats — two for Africa, two for Asia-Pacific, one for Latin America and one for Western Europe — and additional non-permanent seats. Under the proposal, new permanent members would initially not exercise a veto, with the question to be reviewed after a period.

Reform faces a high procedural bar. Amending the UN Charter requires adoption by two-thirds of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of member states, including all five permanent members. The Uniting for Consensus group, including Italy, Pakistan, South Korea and Argentina, opposes new permanent seats and favours expanding only non-permanent membership. Reform therefore requires both a proposal that commands broad support and the consent of the very members whose exclusivity it would dilute.

Key takeaways

  • The Security Council has five veto-holding permanent members from 1945 and ten non-permanent members elected for two years.
  • India's reform case rests on population, economic weight, peacekeeping contribution and repeated non-permanent terms, most recently 2021 to 2022.
  • The G4 — Brazil, Germany, India and Japan — proposes expansion to around twenty-five members with six new permanent seats, initially without veto.

Why it matters for civil studies

UNSC reform shows how procedure can block substance: even broad support for change must pass a Charter amendment requiring the consent of existing permanent members.

Think it through

Is a permanent seat without a veto worth seeking, or does it create a second-class membership that weakens the reform case?
