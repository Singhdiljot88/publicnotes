The economic impact of early British rule

Modern India to 1857 · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Modern Indian history from about the middle of the eighteenth century until the present — significant events, personalities, issues.

The economic effects of British rule before 1857 are among the most debated questions in Indian history. One widely held view, associated with Dadabhai Naoroji's later drain theory, is that wealth flowed from India to Britain through salaries, pensions, and profits remitted home, and through the use of Indian revenue to pay for British administration and wars. The theory was fully developed after 1857, but the mechanism, revenue raised in India spent on British purposes, was already visible in the Company's accounts before the revolt.

Indian crafts suffered as British machine-made goods entered the market. Indian cotton textiles, once exported around the world, faced competition from Lancashire cloth made by machines, and from the Company's own policies, which taxed Indian cloth entering Britain while letting British cloth enter India more cheaply. Weavers lost work, and many turned to agriculture, increasing pressure on the land. Historians debate the scale and timing of this deindustrialisation, since Indian weaving survived in many regions, but the direction of change in the early nineteenth century is clear.

Agriculture was also reshaped. The demand for cash revenue pushed peasants to grow cash crops such as indigo, opium, and cotton for export, and the indigo planters of Bengal became notorious for forcing peasants to grow indigo on unfavourable terms. Opium grown in India was sold in China, and its profits helped balance Britain's trade, linking Indian peasants to a global trade whose costs they did not choose.

Not all change was loss. New ports, roads, and later railways, the first line opened between Bombay and Thane in 1853, created new markets and jobs, and Indian merchants and bankers grew rich as agents of the Company and as traders in their own right. The fair summary is that British rule integrated India into a world economy on unequal terms: it created opportunities for some Indians, especially merchants and the educated, while destroying livelihoods for many weavers and pushing peasants deeper into debt.

Key takeaways

  • The drain theory argued that Indian revenue paid for British salaries, pensions, and wars abroad.
  • Machine-made Lancashire cloth undercut Indian handloom weaving in the early nineteenth century.
  • Cash revenue pushed peasants towards export crops such as indigo, opium, and cotton.

Why it matters for civil studies

It shows how trade policy, not just conquest, reshaped who won and who lost under colonial rule.

Think it through

How would you test the drain theory — what numbers would you need, and where might they mislead?
