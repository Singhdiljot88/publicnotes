Official language and the Eighth Schedule: Hindi, English and twenty-two languages

Constitution and political system · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution — historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure.

Article 343 provides that the official language of the Union shall be Hindi in Devanagari script, with the international form of Indian numerals. It also provided that English would continue to be used for official purposes of the Union for fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution. The Official Languages Act, 1963 continued the use of English for official purposes beyond that period, and English remains in use alongside Hindi for Union purposes, including in Parliament, courts and administration.

The Eighth Schedule lists languages recognised for official purposes. It began with fourteen languages and now contains twenty-two, after additions including Sindhi, Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali, and later Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali. Inclusion in the Schedule does not make a language an official language of the Union, but it carries recognition: languages in the Schedule are used for examinations, literary awards and representation in bodies such as the Official Language Commission, and a language's inclusion is often treated as a mark of national recognition.

Language policy in India is deliberately layered. States may adopt their own official languages under Article 345, and the Constitution provides for a Commission and a Committee of Parliament on official language. The arrangement reflects a compromise reached during the making of the Constitution: Hindi was accepted as the Union's official language, but English was retained and states were left free to choose, in order to avoid imposing a single language on a multilingual country.

Language continues to be politically sensitive, especially in debates over the medium of instruction, competitive examinations and the use of Hindi in Union administration. The constitutional design does not settle which language a citizen should use in daily life. It settles which languages the state must recognise and use, and it leaves the rest to politics, education and personal choice. That distinction is essential for reading language disputes accurately.

Key takeaways

  • Article 343 makes Hindi in Devanagari script the Union's official language, while the Official Languages Act, 1963 continued the use of English.
  • The Eighth Schedule, which began with fourteen languages, now lists twenty-two recognised languages.
  • States may adopt their own official languages under Article 345, making India's language policy layered rather than uniform.

Why it matters for civil studies

Language provisions show a constitutional compromise: one official language for the Union, continued English, and freedom for states to choose their own.

Think it through

Should recognition in the Eighth Schedule bring concrete benefits, such as examination and employment rights, or is symbolic recognition enough?
