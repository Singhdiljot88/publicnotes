Five-Year Plans: how India planned its economy

Indian economy: structure and planning · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment.

From 1951 to 2017 India ran its development effort through Five-Year Plans drawn up by the Planning Commission. The First Plan (1951-56) used a simple Harrod-Domar framework and gave priority to agriculture, irrigation and power after the food shortages of the late 1940s. The Second Plan (1956-61), built around the Mahalanobis model, shifted priority to heavy industry — steel, machinery and mining — on the argument that a poor country had to build its own capital goods base rather than depend on imports.

The Third Plan (1961-66) aimed at self-reliance and food self-sufficiency but was hit by droughts in 1965 and 1966 and by wars, and the plan system broke down. There followed a three-year Plan Holiday (1966-69) of annual plans, then a return to five-year planning. The Fifth Plan (1974-79) adopted the slogan of Garibi Hatao, removal of poverty, and the Sixth Plan (1980-85) pushed employment and poverty programmes. The Eighth Plan (1992-97) was the first framed after the 1991 economic reforms and accepted a larger role for markets and private investment.

Planning was never just a technical exercise. Plans set investment targets, allocated scarce foreign exchange and public funds, and licensed private industry through the industrial licensing system. Critics argue this produced shortages, inefficiency and corruption, and that targets were often missed. Supporters reply that planning built the steel plants, dams, universities and scientific institutions that later growth rested on, and that in a capital-scarce economy some coordination was unavoidable.

The Twelfth Plan (2012-17) was the last. The government decided to discontinue Five-Year Plans beyond March 2017, replacing the system with shorter strategy documents and a new institution, NITI Aayog. The planning era thus closed after twelve plans, leaving a debate that still shapes policy: how much should the state direct investment, and how much should it leave to markets?

Key takeaways

  • The First Plan prioritised food and agriculture; the Second shifted to heavy industry under the Mahalanobis model.
  • Plans allocated capital and foreign exchange, but licensing also bred inefficiency and shortages.
  • Five-Year Plans ended with the Twelfth Plan in March 2017.

Why it matters for civil studies

Planning explains the origins of India's public sector, licensing system and regional investment patterns.

Think it through

Was the Second Plan's bet on heavy industry a mistake, or a necessary foundation for later growth?
