Parliamentary privileges: protecting free speech in the House without placing members above the law

Constitution and political system · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution — historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure.

Articles 105 and 194 confer privileges on Parliament and state legislatures. Members have freedom of speech in the legislature, and no member is liable to court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the legislature or its committees. Legislatures also have the power to punish for contempt and breach of privilege, and their proceedings cannot be questioned in court on the ground of irregularity of procedure. The purpose is to protect free debate: a member should be able to criticise the government without fear of a lawsuit for words spoken in the House.

Privileges are not unlimited and they are not fully codified. The Constitution provides that the privileges of each House shall be those of the House of Commons in the United Kingdom at the commencement of the Constitution, until Parliament defines them by law, which it has not yet done comprehensively. The Supreme Court has held that privileges must be read subject to fundamental rights, and that courts may examine whether a claimed privilege exists and whether it has been exercised in accordance with the Constitution, even though they do not sit in judgment on the wisdom of proceedings inside the House.

Privileges also include freedom from arrest in civil cases during a session and forty days before and after it, and exemption from jury service, although these protections are narrower than they once were and do not cover criminal arrest. A legislature may also regulate its own procedure and exclude strangers from sittings. The power to punish for breach of privilege has been used against members and non-members, including journalists, which has raised concerns about free speech outside the House.

The central question is balance. Privilege protects speech inside the legislature so that government can be scrutinised, but it must not become a shield for conduct outside the legislature or a tool to silence criticism. A codified law of privileges would clarify the boundary, but it would also require Parliament to limit its own powers, which may explain why codification has not happened.

Key takeaways

  • Articles 105 and 194 give members freedom of speech in the legislature and immunity from court proceedings for speeches and votes in the House.
  • Privileges remain largely uncodified, referring to the House of Commons position at commencement, and courts may examine whether a claimed privilege exists.
  • Privileges include limited freedom from civil arrest during sessions, but do not cover criminal arrest or conduct outside the legislature.

Why it matters for civil studies

Parliamentary privilege protects debate inside the House, but its uncodified scope leaves a lasting question about who sets its limits.

Think it through

Should Parliament codify its privileges by law, even if that means limiting its own power to punish for contempt?
