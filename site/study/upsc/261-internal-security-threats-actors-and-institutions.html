Internal security: threats, actors and institutions

Internal security · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Linkages between development and spread of extremism; role of external state and non-state actors in creating challenges to internal security; challenges to internal security through communication networks, role of media and social networking sites, basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security challenges and their management in border areas; linkages of organised crime with terrorism; various security forces and agencies and their mandate.

Internal security means protecting a country's people, territory and constitutional order from threats that arise inside it or are directed at it from within, as distinct from war between states. The threats are diverse: terrorism, insurgency, left-wing extremism, communal violence, organised crime, cyberattacks and drug trafficking. They overlap: a terrorist group may use organised crime for money, an insurgency may use cross-border support, and a cyberattack may target power or banking systems during a physical crisis.

Under the Constitution, police and public order are state subjects, while the Union handles central armed police forces, intelligence, and matters such as defence and external affairs. In practice, internal security is therefore a shared task. States run day-to-day policing, while the Centre supplements them with central forces, intelligence sharing, funding and specialised agencies such as the National Investigation Agency. Friction between Centre and states over who controls a force, an investigation or a border is a normal part of this system, not an exception to it.

The main institutional actors include state police forces, central armed police forces such as the Central Reserve Police Force, the Border Security Force and the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, intelligence agencies, the armed forces in aid of civil authority in exceptional cases, and specialised bodies for disasters, cyber security and financial crime. The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry at the Centre for internal security, while the National Security Council Secretariat coordinates strategic assessment.

A useful way to judge any internal-security policy is to ask four questions: does it reduce the threat, does it protect rights and the rule of law, does it address the grievances that feed recruitment, and can it be sustained financially and politically? A policy that passes only the first test — for example, one that detains widely but deepens local alienation — often fails the longer test. Security and legitimacy are not trade-offs in the long run; legitimacy is what makes security durable.

Key takeaways

  • Internal security covers terrorism, insurgency, extremism, communal violence, organised crime, cyber threats and trafficking.
  • Police and public order are state subjects, with the Centre supplementing states through forces, intelligence and specialised agencies.
  • Durable security needs threat reduction, rights protection, grievance redress and sustainability together.

Why it matters for civil studies

Internal security joins constitutional federalism, policing, intelligence, development and rights in a single field.

Think it through

When Centre and state disagree over who should control an investigation or a force deployment, whose view should prevail, and why?
