The informal economy: small firms, casual work and missing protections

Economy · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment; inclusive growth and issues arising from it.

A large share of India's economic activity takes place outside formal regulation: in unregistered firms, household enterprises, street vending, construction sites, farms and homes. This informal economy is not a separate world from the formal economy. Formal firms often rely on informal suppliers, contract workers and distributors, and households move between formal and informal income sources as work becomes available. Understanding the economy therefore requires looking beyond registered companies and payroll records.

Informal enterprises are usually small, labour-intensive and short of capital, technology and formal credit. They provide livelihoods and low-cost goods and services, especially in towns and cities, but they face insecure premises, harassment, irregular orders and limited bargaining power with buyers. Informal workers often lack written contracts, paid leave, pensions and health insurance, and women, migrants and young workers are over-represented in the most precarious jobs. Their incomes fall sharply during shocks such as lockdowns, droughts or demand slumps, because there is no paid leave or unemployment benefit to fall back on.

Policy responses pull in different directions. Formalisation can bring credit, legal protection and social security, but registration alone does not raise productivity or income, and compliance costs can push small firms back into informality. Simplified registration, such as the Udyam portal for micro, small and medium enterprises, and social security efforts such as the e-Shram portal for unorganised workers, aim to make formal recognition easier without imposing the full burden of formal regulation at once. Analysts debate whether these databases translate into real benefits or mainly improve counting.

The informal economy also complicates statistics. Output and employment are estimated through surveys rather than administrative records, so figures are revised and disputed more often than for the formal sector. For civil studies, the key point is normative as well as technical: an economy judged only by its largest firms will misread how most Indians earn a living, and policies on minimum wages, credit, skilling and social security must be designed for work as it actually is, not as formal models assume it to be.

Key takeaways

  • The informal economy includes unregistered firms and workers without formal contracts or social security, and it is deeply linked to formal firms.
  • Informal workers face volatile incomes and weak protection, with women, migrants and young workers most exposed.
  • Formalisation through easier registration and worker databases helps counting, but does not by itself raise incomes or productivity.

Why it matters for civil studies

Most Indian workers earn outside formal employment, so informal work is central, not marginal, to economic policy.

Think it through

Should policy first formalise informal firms, or first extend social protection regardless of formal status, and what are the trade-offs?
