The Northern Plains of India

Physical geography of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; geographical features and their location.

The northern plains are one of the largest alluvial plains in the world, formed by the deposition of silt by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra rivers and their tributaries over millions of years. They stretch from Punjab in the west to Assam in the east, lying between the Himalayas in the north and the peninsular plateau in the south. The plain is remarkably flat, with a very gentle slope from west to east, and it is covered by deep, fertile alluvial soil. That flatness and fertility, together with abundant river water, explain why this region has supported dense settlement and intensive agriculture for centuries.

Geographers divide the plain into smaller belts that follow the journey of a river from the mountains. Closest to the foothills is the bhabar, a narrow belt of coarse gravel and boulders where small streams disappear underground. South of it lies the terai, a marshy, forested belt where those streams re-emerge, creating damp and once malarial land that has largely been cleared for farming. Further south are the older alluvium, called bangar, which lies above the flood level, and the newer alluvium, called khadar, which is renewed by floods almost every year and is usually more fertile. Knowing these belts helps explain local differences in soil, drainage and flood risk.

The plain is also divided by river basins: the Indus plain in the west, the Ganga plain in the centre, and the Brahmaputra plain in the east. The Ganga plain is the largest and most densely populated. Its rivers deposit silt, recharge groundwater and provide water for irrigation, but they also flood. Floods renew soil and damage crops, homes and roads in the same season, so flood management is a permanent public task rather than a one-off emergency. In the west, the Indus plain depends heavily on irrigation, because rainfall declines towards the drier parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan.

For civil studies, the northern plains show how physical geography becomes economic and administrative geography. Fertile soil and water support food production, flat land supports roads, railways and towns, and rivers support both irrigation and dispute: sharing river water between states and countries is a recurring policy issue. The same flatness that makes farming and transport easy also makes drainage slow, so waterlogging, groundwater depletion and urban flooding are modern problems layered over an ancient landscape.

Key takeaways

  • The northern plains are alluvial plains built by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems between the Himalayas and the peninsular plateau.
  • From the foothills southwards, the belts are bhabar, terai, bangar (older alluvium) and khadar (newer, flood-renewed alluvium).
  • The plain's fertility supports dense population and intensive farming, but also brings floods, waterlogging and interstate water-sharing questions.

Why it matters for civil studies

The northern plains are India's food and population heartland, so their soil, water and flood patterns shape agricultural and infrastructure policy.

Think it through

How should flood policy balance the benefits of silt and groundwater recharge against the damage floods cause to homes and crops?
