Corruption: causes, costs and controls

Ethics and integrity · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS IV — Ethics and Human Interface; aptitude and foundational values for Civil Service; public/civil service values and Ethics in Public administration; probity in Governance — concept of public service, information sharing and transparency, codes of ethics and conduct, citizen's charters, work culture, quality of service delivery, utilisation of public funds, challenges of corruption.

Corruption is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain. It ranges from a small bribe to speed up a service, to large-scale diversion of public funds, to the capture of policy itself, where rules are written to benefit a private group. Petty corruption is often the most visible, but grand corruption and policy capture usually do more damage, because they redirect whole budgets and distort the rules under which everyone else must operate. Defining corruption broadly matters: if only cash bribes count, many of the most harmful abuses escape notice.

Corruption flourishes where three conditions meet: discretion without oversight, monopoly over a service citizens cannot get elsewhere, and weak accountability. Complex procedures with many approvals create opportunities to charge for speed; secrecy hides them; low pay is sometimes cited as a cause, but evidence from reformers suggests that opportunity and impunity matter more than salary alone. Social acceptance also matters: where citizens expect to pay, or believe everyone does, reporting feels futile and the practice normalises.

The costs go beyond stolen money. Corruption misallocates resources, favouring projects that generate kickbacks over those that serve needs. It raises the price of doing business, deters investment, and deepens inequality, because the poor pay a larger share of their income in bribes and have fewer alternatives. It also corrodes trust: when citizens believe the system is rigged, they disengage from politics, evade taxes, or seek private solutions, which weakens the very institutions that could fix the problem.

India's legal framework illustrates the standard three-part response. The Prevention of Corruption Act, passed in 1988 and substantially amended in 2018, criminalises bribery by public servants; the 2018 amendment also criminalised giving a bribe. The Central Vigilance Commission, created in 1964 and given statutory status by an Act of 2003, oversees vigilance in central government. The Lokpal and Lokayukts Act of 2013 created an ombudsman to inquire into corruption complaints against public functionaries. Reformers describe effective control as combining punitive action, preventive measures such as simplified procedures and e-governance, and participative vigilance by citizens. No single law is enough; deterrence fails without detection, and detection fails without protection for those who report.

Key takeaways

  • Corruption is abuse of entrusted power for private gain, from petty bribes to capture of policy itself.
  • It thrives where discretion meets monopoly and weak accountability; opportunity and impunity matter more than pay alone.
  • Control needs three legs: punishment, prevention, and citizen participation — India's framework includes the 1988 Act (amended 2018), the CVC, and the 2013 Lokpal Act.

Why it matters for civil studies

It connects ethics to institutional design: most anti-corruption questions are really questions about discretion, oversight, and incentives.

Think it through

If you could reform only one thing — simpler procedures, stronger punishment, or better protection for whistle-blowers — which would reduce corruption most, and why?
