The Central Highlands and the Chotanagpur Plateau

Physical geography of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; geographical features and their location.

The Central Highlands lie between the northern plains and the Deccan Plateau, roughly north of the Narmada river and south of the Aravalli Range. They include the Malwa plateau, the Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand uplands, and, further east, the Chotanagpur plateau, which covers much of Jharkhand and parts of Odisha, West Bengal, Bihar and Chhattisgarh. The region is an old, eroded upland of hard rocks, rolling hills and river valleys, drained by rivers such as the Chambal, Betwa, Ken and Son, which flow northwards into the Ganga system.

The Chotanagpur plateau is the best-known part of the region, and for a clear reason: it is India's richest mineral belt. Iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite, mica and copper occur here in large quantities, and the coalfields around Jharia, Raniganj and Bokaro have powered steel plants and thermal power stations for generations. Towns such as Jamshedpur, Bokaro and Rourkela grew around steel and mining, and the Damodar river valley, sometimes called the Ruhr of India by comparison with Germany's industrial region, became an early focus of planned industrial development, including dams built for flood control, irrigation and power.

Yet mineral wealth has not removed poverty in the region. Mining has brought displacement of villages, loss of forest and farmland, and pollution of air, rivers and groundwater, and many mining districts still record low incomes, poor health and weak infrastructure. The region is also home to large tribal populations, including Santal, Munda, Oraon and Ho communities, for whom land and forest are both livelihood and identity. Laws protecting tribal land and forest rights therefore sit at the centre of disputes over mining leases, dams and industrial projects.

For civil studies, the Central Highlands and Chotanagpur raise a classic development question: who gains from resources found under someone else's home? The answer involves mining royalties, rehabilitation, forest rights, environmental clearance and local employment, not only the volume of ore extracted. The region also shows why geography matters to industry: coal and iron ore occur together, rivers provide water and power, and railways link mines to steel plants and ports. Remove any one of those, and the industrial geography changes.

Key takeaways

  • The Central Highlands lie north of the Narmada, including Malwa, Bundelkhand–Baghelkhand and the Chotanagpur plateau in the east.
  • Chotanagpur holds major coal, iron ore, manganese, bauxite and mica deposits, supporting steel towns such as Jamshedpur, Bokaro and Rourkela.
  • Mineral wealth coexists with displacement, forest loss and tribal land-rights questions, so benefits and costs are unevenly shared.

Why it matters for civil studies

Resource geography explains both industrial location and persistent conflict over land, forests and rehabilitation.

Think it through

Who should benefit most from minerals extracted from tribal and forest land: the state, the company, or the local community?
