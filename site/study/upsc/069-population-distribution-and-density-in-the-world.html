Population Distribution and Density in the World

World geography · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; history of the world — events from the 18th century (industrial revolution, world wars, redrawal of boundaries, colonisation, decolonisation).

The world's population is very unevenly distributed. Dense populations cluster in South Asia, East Asia, western Europe and the north-eastern United States, while vast areas, such as the Sahara, the Amazon basin, Siberia, the Australian outback and Antarctica, are almost empty. Population density, the number of people per square kilometre, is a useful measure, but it can mislead: a country with a low average density, such as Australia, can have very crowded cities, while a densely populated country can have empty mountains and forests. Distribution matters more than the average alone.

Physical factors explain much of the pattern. People cluster where water, fertile soil, mild climate and flat land make farming and settlement easy: river valleys and deltas, coastal plains and temperate lowlands. They avoid extremes of cold, heat, aridity and altitude, and rugged terrain. Human factors then reinforce the pattern: jobs, trade, ports, capitals and industrial regions attract migrants, and once a city grows, its markets, services and transport attract still more people. This cumulative process explains why a few metropolitan regions now hold tens of millions of people each, while other regions lose population.

Migration, both within countries and between them, constantly reshapes distribution. Rural-to-urban migration has made the world majority urban for the first time in history, and the United Nations has reported that more than half the world's people now live in urban areas. International migration is driven by work, education, conflict and family, and it creates diasporas, remittance flows and cultural change in both sending and receiving places. At the same time, some regions face population decline and ageing, with shrinking workforces and closing services, a very different problem from overcrowding.

For civil studies, population distribution is the background to almost every policy: where to build schools, hospitals, roads and water systems; how to manage cities and protect farmland; and how to share resources between crowded and empty regions. It also raises questions of fairness: should investment follow people, follow need, or try to balance regions? A map of population density, read alongside maps of water, jobs and climate risk, is often the quickest way to see why a policy that works in one place fails in another.

Key takeaways

  • Dense populations cluster in South Asia, East Asia, western Europe and north-eastern North America, while deserts, polar regions and dense forests are sparse.
  • Physical factors (water, soil, climate, terrain) and human factors (jobs, trade, cities) together explain distribution, with cities growing cumulatively.
  • More than half the world's people now live in urban areas, according to United Nations estimates, reshaping service and infrastructure needs.

Why it matters for civil studies

Population distribution shapes where services, infrastructure and investment are needed, and where they are wasted if misplaced.

Think it through

Should governments try to spread population more evenly, or concentrate investment where people already live?
