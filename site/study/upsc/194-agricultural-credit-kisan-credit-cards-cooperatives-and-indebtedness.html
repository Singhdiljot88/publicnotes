Agricultural credit: Kisan Credit Cards, cooperatives and indebtedness

Agriculture · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Major crops, cropping patterns, irrigation, storage, transport and marketing of agricultural produce; direct and indirect farm subsidies and minimum support prices; Public Distribution System; land reforms in India; food processing and related industries.

Credit is essential to farming: seeds, fertiliser, labour and irrigation must be paid for months before harvest income arrives. Without affordable credit, farmers borrow from input dealers, traders or moneylenders at high interest, sell crops early at low prices, or forgo inputs and accept lower yields. The formal credit system therefore aims to provide timely, adequate and cheap credit, especially to small and marginal farmers who lack collateral and formal records.

Institutional credit flows through commercial banks, regional rural banks and cooperative banks, with the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development refinancing and supporting the system. Priority sector lending requirements direct a share of bank credit to agriculture, and the Kisan Credit Card, introduced in 1998, gives farmers a revolving working-capital limit for crop and allied activities, with interest subvention reducing the effective rate for timely repayment. Self-help groups, especially women's groups linked to banks, extend credit to households that individual banks find costly to serve, and microfinance institutions fill gaps, though at higher rates.

Indebtedness remains widespread, and its causes are debated. Crop failure, health expenses, social obligations, low and volatile prices, and borrowing for non-productive purposes all contribute, and formal credit that is delayed, inadequate or tied to particular inputs can push farmers back to informal lenders for the rest of their needs. Loan waivers, announced by several state governments, provide immediate relief but are criticised for straining state budgets, rewarding default, weakening credit discipline and often missing tenant farmers and labourers who borrow informally and hold no formal loan to waive.

Long-term solutions emphasise more than credit volume: reliable irrigation and insurance to reduce risk, land records and tenancy recognition so tenants can borrow formally, financial literacy, and grievance mechanisms when banks delay or deny loans. Credit is also linked to markets: a farmer who can store produce and wait for better prices needs less distress borrowing than one forced to sell at harvest. For civil studies, agricultural credit shows how financial inclusion, fiscal policy and agrarian structure intersect, and why a loan waiver debate is never only about money.

Key takeaways

  • The Kisan Credit Card, introduced in 1998, gives farmers a revolving limit for crop and allied needs, with interest subvention for timely repayment.
  • Institutional farm credit flows through commercial, regional rural and cooperative banks, supported by NABARD refinancing.
  • Loan waivers relieve formal borrowers but strain budgets, can weaken credit discipline, and often miss informal borrowers.

Why it matters for civil studies

Timely affordable credit decides whether a farmer invests in a crop or borrows at high cost to survive the season.

Think it through

Do loan waivers help the most indebted farmers, or mainly those already inside the formal credit system?
