The Earth's Movements and the Seasons

World geography · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; history of the world — events from the 18th century (industrial revolution, world wars, redrawal of boundaries, colonisation, decolonisation).

The Earth has two main movements: rotation on its axis, which takes about 24 hours, and revolution around the Sun, which takes about 365¼ days. Rotation causes day and night, as each part of the Earth turns towards and then away from the Sun. Revolution, combined with the tilt of the Earth's axis at about 23½° to the plane of its orbit, causes the seasons. Without the tilt, every place would have roughly the same season all year; with it, each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for part of the year and away for the rest.

When the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun, around 21 June, it receives more direct sunlight and longer days, and experiences summer, while the southern hemisphere experiences winter. This is the summer solstice in the north and the winter solstice in the south. Around 22 December the position reverses: the southern hemisphere leans towards the Sun and has summer, while the northern hemisphere has winter. Around 21 March and 23 September, neither hemisphere leans towards the Sun, day and night are roughly equal everywhere, and these are the equinoxes. The word equinox itself means equal night.

Distance from the Sun is not the main cause of the seasons. The Earth is actually slightly closer to the Sun in early January, during northern winter, than in July. What matters is the angle of sunlight and the length of day: direct rays heat a smaller area more intensely, and longer days give more hours of heating. That is why places near the equator, where the Sun is high all year, have little seasonal temperature change, while places near the poles, where the Sun may not rise at all in midwinter, have extreme seasonal contrasts, including polar day and polar night.

For civil studies, the seasons explain patterns that recur in geography, agriculture and public life. Monsoon timing, crop calendars, energy demand, tourism and even school holidays follow the seasonal cycle, though local geography modifies it: a coastal city and a continental interior at the same latitude can have very different seasonal experiences. Understanding tilt, solstices and equinoxes also prevents a common error, attributing seasons to distance, and it provides the foundation for understanding climate zones, ocean currents and the distribution of life on Earth.

Key takeaways

  • Rotation (about 24 hours) causes day and night; revolution (about 365¼ days) plus an axial tilt of about 23½° causes the seasons.
  • The solstices fall around 21 June and 22 December; the equinoxes, when day and night are roughly equal, fall around 21 March and 23 September.
  • Seasons depend mainly on the angle of sunlight and day length, not on the Earth's distance from the Sun.

Why it matters for civil studies

Axial tilt explains why hemispheres have opposite seasons and why equatorial and polar climates differ so sharply.
