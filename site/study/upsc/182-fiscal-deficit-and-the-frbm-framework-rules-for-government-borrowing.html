Fiscal deficit and the FRBM framework: rules for government borrowing

Public finance · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Government Budgeting.

The fiscal deficit is the gap between the government's total expenditure and its total receipts excluding borrowings. It measures how much the government must borrow in a year to fund its spending. The revenue deficit is the narrower gap between revenue expenditure and revenue receipts, showing whether current spending is funded by current income or by borrowing. The primary deficit is the fiscal deficit minus interest payments, showing the current year's borrowing need before the cost of past debt. These three measures answer different questions, and confusing them leads to misleading debate.

Persistent deficits matter because borrowing must be repaid with interest, which then consumes future budgets and can crowd out private investment by raising interest rates. At the same time, deficits can be justified in recessions, wars or disasters, when tax revenues fall and spending needs rise, or when borrowing funds productive assets that raise future income. The economic question is therefore not whether deficits are always bad, but whether borrowing is affordable, temporary or structural, and what it funds. A deficit that builds irrigation, schools or railways differs from one that funds permanent consumption without raising revenue.

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, was enacted to institutionalise discipline, initially targeting a fiscal deficit of 3 per cent of GDP and elimination of the revenue deficit, with deadlines later postponed and revised after the global financial crisis of 2008 and other shocks. The Act requires the government to present Medium-Term Fiscal Policy, Fiscal Policy Strategy and Macroeconomic Framework statements with the Budget, and it was amended in 2018 to introduce an escape clause allowing deviation from targets in specified circumstances, such as national security, war, calamity or a sharp economic collapse. A committee chaired by N. K. Singh reviewed the framework in 2017 and recommended debt as the main anchor alongside deficit targets.

Fiscal rules face a familiar trade-off: rigid rules can force harmful cuts in a downturn, while flexible rules can be evaded in good times. India's experience shows both risks, with targets missed during crises and then reset rather than enforced. States have their own fiscal responsibility laws, and their combined deficits matter for national debt. For civil studies, the FRBM debate is about credible commitment: how can a government bind itself today without tying the hands of a future government facing a genuine emergency?

Key takeaways

  • Fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus receipts excluding borrowings; revenue deficit covers current spending versus current receipts.
  • The FRBM Act, 2003, initially targeted a 3 per cent of GDP fiscal deficit and revenue deficit elimination, later revised with an escape clause in 2018.
  • Deficits are judged by affordability, duration and purpose, not by their existence alone.

Why it matters for civil studies

Borrowing today is taxation tomorrow, so fiscal rules are about fairness between generations as well as stability.

Think it through

Should fiscal rules target the deficit, the debt ratio, or spending growth, and which is hardest for a government to evade?
