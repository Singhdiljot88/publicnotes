The SC/ST Prevention of Atrocities Act: what counts as an atrocity and how it is tried

Social justice, women and vulnerable groups · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Role of women and women's organisation, population and associated issues, poverty and developmental issues, urbanisation; social empowerment, communalism, regionalism & secularism. Also touches GS II: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, and mechanisms/laws/institutions for their protection and betterment.

The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 was enacted because existing criminal law was considered inadequate to deal with caste-based humiliation, violence and dispossession. Article 17 of the Constitution abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form, and the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 gave effect to that provision. The 1989 Act goes further by defining a set of offences, called atrocities, when committed by a person who is not a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe against a member of those communities.

The offences listed in the Act include forcing a person to eat or drink an offensive substance, parading a person naked, sexual assault of a woman, wrongful occupation of land, social or economic boycott, and abuse by caste name in public. The 2015 amendment added new offences, including garlanding with footwear, denying access to irrigation facilities or forest rights, and dedicating a woman as a devadasi. The Act also provides for relief and rehabilitation of victims, including immediate assistance through the district administration.

Procedure is a distinctive part of the Act. Offences under it are cognisable, meaning police may arrest without a warrant, and investigation is to be conducted by an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police. State governments are to establish special courts, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to try these offences, and special public prosecutors are appointed for them. Section 18 bars anticipatory bail where a prima facie case is made out, although courts have held that anticipatory bail may be considered where no prima facie case exists.

The Act has been debated in the courts and in Parliament. In 2018 the Supreme Court introduced safeguards against arrest, and Parliament responded the same year with an amendment restoring the earlier position on arrest and anticipatory bail. Supporters argue that strong procedure is necessary because victims may face social and economic pressure not to complain. Critics argue that any criminal law must guard against misuse. Both arguments point to the same test: whether investigation, trial and relief work quickly and fairly in practice.

Key takeaways

  • The 1989 Act defines atrocities as listed offences by a non-SC/ST person against an SC or ST person, building on Article 17's abolition of untouchability.
  • It provides for special courts, special public prosecutors, investigation by a senior officer and relief and rehabilitation for victims.
  • The 2015 amendment added offences and the 2018 amendment restored the bar on anticipatory bail after a Supreme Court ruling on safeguards.

Why it matters for civil studies

Atrocities law is a study in procedure: who investigates, which court tries the case and what relief reaches the victim often matter more than the offence label.

Think it through

How can a law give victims strong protection while still ensuring that an accused person is not arrested without a prima facie case?
