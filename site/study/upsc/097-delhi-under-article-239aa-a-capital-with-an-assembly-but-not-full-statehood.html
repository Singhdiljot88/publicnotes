Delhi under Article 239AA: a capital with an assembly but not full statehood

Constitution and political system · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Indian Constitution — historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure.

Delhi is a Union Territory, but it is not governed like other Union Territories. Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment in 1991 and in force from 1 February 1992, gives Delhi special provisions. The Union Territory of Delhi is called the National Capital Territory of Delhi, its administrator is designated as the Lieutenant Governor, and it has a Legislative Assembly whose members are chosen by direct election, together with a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister.

The Assembly may make laws on State List and Concurrent List subjects, except for three entries: public order, police and land. Those subjects remain with the Union. The Council of Ministers is limited to not more than ten per cent of the total membership of the Assembly, and it aids and advises the Lieutenant Governor in matters on which the Assembly may legislate. If the Lieutenant Governor differs from the Council of Ministers on a matter, the matter is to be referred to the President for decision, and the Lieutenant Governor acts according to that decision.

The balance between the elected government and the Lieutenant Governor has been litigated. In Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India in 2018, a five-judge bench held that the Lieutenant Governor is bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers on matters within the Assembly's competence, except where the Constitution or a law requires the Lieutenant Governor to act in discretion, and that the Lieutenant Governor does not have independent decision-making power as a general rule. A later Constitution Bench decision in 2023 dealt specifically with control over services, and Parliament then legislated on the subject, which remains a contested area.

Delhi's design reflects its dual character: it is both a city with an elected government and the seat of the Union government. That duality explains why police, public order and land are reserved, and why disagreements between the two levels are frequent. For students, Delhi is a case study in incomplete federalism: an elected assembly exists, but its competence is deliberately narrower than a state's.

Key takeaways

  • Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment in 1991, makes Delhi the National Capital Territory with a directly elected Assembly and a Lieutenant Governor.
  • The Delhi Assembly cannot legislate on public order, police or land, and its Council of Ministers is capped at ten per cent of Assembly membership.
  • In 2018 the Supreme Court held the Lieutenant Governor is generally bound by ministerial advice, except where discretion is expressly required.

Why it matters for civil studies

Delhi shows that federal status is not all or nothing: a territory can have an assembly, a chief minister and still lack a state's full legislative competence.

Think it through

Should a national capital have an elected government at all, or does the presence of the Union government make that unworkable?
