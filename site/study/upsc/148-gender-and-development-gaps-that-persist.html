Gender and development: gaps that persist

Health, education and human development · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — Issues relating to development and management of Social Sector/Services relating to Health, Education, Human Resources. Also touches GS III: Indian Economy — growth, development and employment.

Development is not gender-neutral. Women and girls in India have gained on many indicators — schooling, life expectancy and political participation — yet gaps remain in survival, work, assets and safety, and these gaps shape the whole economy. The most basic indicator is the sex ratio at birth: the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019-21) put it at 929 girls per 1,000 boys, up from 919 in NFHS-4, but still below the natural level, reflecting persistent son preference and sex-selective practices despite the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act, 1994, which bans sex determination for selection.

The Beti Bachao Beti Padhao scheme, launched on 22 January 2015, combined enforcement, awareness and support for girls' education to address the declining child sex ratio. Sex ratios at birth have improved in several states, but the national figure remains skewed, and the scheme's critics argue that awareness campaigns cannot substitute for enforcement and for changing the economic reasons families prefer sons — old-age support, dowry costs and inheritance practices. The overall sex ratio of the population, by contrast, reached 1,020 females per 1,000 males in NFHS-5, because women live longer than men at older ages.

Economic gaps are large. As an earlier note showed, female labour force participation rose to 41.7 per cent in 2023-24, but remains far below men's, and much of it is informal and unpaid. Women own less land and fewer assets, face a gender pay gap, and carry most unpaid care work. Political representation has moved further: women now vote at rates equal to or above men, lead panchayats in large numbers under the one-third reservation in local bodies, and the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023, reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, to take effect after delimitation.

Gender and development is therefore not a separate topic but a lens on every other one. Health policy that ignores maternal care, education policy that ignores girls' dropout, employment policy that ignores care work, and urban policy that ignores safe transport will all underperform. The direction of change is clear — gaps are narrowing — but the pace and the quality of change, especially in paid work and asset ownership, remain the real test.

Key takeaways

  • Sex ratio at birth was 929 girls per 1,000 boys in NFHS-5, up from 919, still below natural levels despite the PCPNDT Act, 1994.
  • Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (22 January 2015) targets the child sex ratio, but economic motives for son preference persist.
  • The 106th Amendment, 2023, reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and assembly seats for women, effective after delimitation.

Why it matters for civil studies

Gender gaps in survival, work and assets shape every development outcome.

Think it through

Will reserved seats in legislatures change outcomes for women, or only their presence in politics?
