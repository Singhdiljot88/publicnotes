India and the European Union: a strategic partnership still negotiating trade

International relations and India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS II — India and its neighbourhood — relations; bilateral, regional and global groupings and agreements involving India and/or affecting India's interests; effect of policies and politics of developed and developing countries on India's interests, Indian Diaspora.

The European Union is India's largest trading partner as a bloc, and the relationship covers trade, investment, technology, climate, research and foreign policy dialogue. India and the European Union established a strategic partnership in 2004, and hold regular summits, ministerial meetings and dialogues on subjects ranging from counter-terrorism to maritime security. The European Union is also a major source of foreign investment in India and a destination for Indian exports, students and professionals.

Trade negotiations have been the most difficult part of the relationship. Talks on a broad-based trade and investment agreement began in 2007, stalled in 2013, and were relaunched in 2022, with separate negotiations on investment protection and geographical indications. Differences remain over market access for agriculture and services, government procurement, labour and environmental standards, and data protection. The European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism, which imposes charges on imports of certain carbon-intensive goods, has added a new source of friction, with India arguing that it penalises developing-country exporters.

Beyond trade, the partnership includes cooperation on climate and clean energy, digital regulation, research under programmes such as Horizon Europe, and connectivity in the Indo-Pacific. The European Union's Indo-Pacific strategy, adopted in 2021, emphasises a rules-based order, freedom of navigation and partnerships with countries including India, Japan and ASEAN members. India and the European Union also cooperate on maritime security, including in the western Indian Ocean, where piracy and trafficking affect shipping.

The relationship illustrates a wider pattern in India's foreign policy: partnerships with large economies bring technology, investment and market access, but also regulatory demands that India must weigh against domestic priorities. A trade agreement with the European Union would be judged not only by tariff cuts, but by its effects on farmers, small manufacturers, data governance, climate policy, and the ability of Indian regulators to set standards for their own market in future.

Key takeaways

  • India and the European Union established a strategic partnership in 2004 and hold regular summits and dialogues on trade, security and climate.
  • Trade talks begun in 2007 stalled in 2013 and were relaunched in 2022, alongside separate investment and geographical indications negotiations.
  • The EU carbon border adjustment mechanism and differences over agriculture, services, procurement and data remain sources of friction.

Why it matters for civil studies

India-EU relations show that a strategic partnership can be broad in dialogue and narrow in agreement, especially when trade meets regulation.

Think it through

Should India accept European labour and environmental standards in a trade deal in return for market access, or keep those standards out of trade talks?
