India's Physiographic Divisions: An Overview

Physical geography of India · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS I — Salient features of world's physical geography; geographical features and their location.

India is conventionally divided into six physiographic regions: the Himalayan mountains, the northern plains, the peninsular plateau, the Indian desert, the coastal plains, and the islands. The division is based on relief, geology and structure rather than on state boundaries, so a single state can contain several regions and a single region can span many states. Used well, it is a mental map: once a reader can place a place in its region, they can predict much about its terrain, soil, rivers and climate before looking at any detail.

The Himalayas in the north are young, high and tectonically active; the northern plains are young in a different way, built from river alluvium and almost flat. The peninsular plateau is old and stable, worn down over hundreds of millions of years. The Indian desert in the north-west is arid and sandy, the coastal plains are low strips shaped by the sea and by delta-building rivers, and the islands are either mountain peaks rising from the sea, as in the Andamans, or coral atolls, as in Lakshadweep. Each region therefore has a different geological story, and that story explains its present surface.

The regions also interact. Himalayan rivers carry silt to the northern plains and build deltas on the east coast. The Western Ghats, part of the plateau's edge, wring rain from the monsoon and leave the interior Deccan drier. The desert's dry air and the plateau's heat help drive the monsoon circulation that waters the plains. A flood in Assam, a drought in the Deccan and a cyclone on the Odisha coast may look like separate events, but they are connected through the same system of mountains, rivers, seas and winds.

For civil studies, the physiographic map is a tool for thinking, not a list to memorise. It helps explain why transport is easy on the plains and hard in the hills, why irrigation matters more in the Deccan than in the Ganga plain, why ports cluster on the west coast, and why disaster risk differs by region. Good answers in geography rarely describe a place in isolation; they place it in its region, trace its connections to neighbouring regions, and then ask what those connections mean for people, farming, industry and public policy.

Key takeaways

  • India's six physiographic divisions are the Himalayas, northern plains, peninsular plateau, Indian desert, coastal plains and islands.
  • The divisions rest on geology and relief, not state boundaries, so regions cut across states and states contain several regions.
  • The regions interact through rivers, monsoon winds and deltas, so events in one region often have causes or effects in another.

Why it matters for civil studies

A physiographic map lets a reader predict terrain, soil, water and risk, and connect local events to larger systems.
