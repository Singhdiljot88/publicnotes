Electricity and magnetism: from circuits to the grid

General science: physics, chemistry and biology foundations · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life (foundations: physics, chemistry, biology).

Electricity is the flow of electric charge, usually electrons moving through a conductor such as copper wire. Three quantities describe a circuit: voltage, which is the push driving the charge; current, which is the rate of flow; and resistance, which opposes it. Ohm's law connects them: current equals voltage divided by resistance. A high-resistance filament in a bulb converts electrical energy into heat and light; a low-resistance wire lets current flow easily. Every appliance is, in effect, a designed resistance that turns electrical energy into something useful — light, heat, motion, or computation.

Electricity and magnetism are two faces of one phenomenon. A moving charge creates a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric current — a discovery associated with Michael Faraday in the 1830s. This second effect, electromagnetic induction, is how almost all large-scale electricity is generated: a turbine spins a coil of wire inside a magnetic field, and current flows. Whether the turbine is driven by steam from coal, falling water, wind, or nuclear heat, the final step is the same spinning coil.

The power grid exists because electricity is hard to store in large quantities and must be produced as it is used. Power stations generate electricity, transformers raise its voltage for long-distance transmission — high voltage means lower current for the same power, and lower current means less energy lost as heat in the wires — and local transformers step the voltage back down for homes and offices. Alternating current, which reverses direction many times per second, is used for grids because transformers work with changing current; that practical advantage settled the early contest between alternating and direct current for bulk supply.

Safety follows from the same physics. Current through the human body can disrupt the heart's own electrical signals, which is why insulation, earthing, and circuit breakers matter. A fuse or breaker interrupts the circuit when current exceeds a safe level, protecting wires from overheating and starting fires. The grid is therefore not just a network of wires but a managed system balancing supply and demand every second, with safety devices at every stage.

Key takeaways

  • Ohm's law links voltage, current, and resistance; appliances convert electrical energy into light, heat, motion, or computation.
  • Electromagnetic induction (Faraday, 1830s) is how nearly all large-scale electricity is generated.
  • High-voltage transmission reduces energy lost as heat, which is why grids step voltage up and then down again.

Why it matters for civil studies

It explains energy policy choices: why grids, storage, and transmission matter as much as generation itself.
