Motion, force and energy in everyday physics

General science: physics, chemistry and biology foundations · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Science and Technology — developments and their applications and effects in everyday life (foundations: physics, chemistry, biology).

Everyday movement — walking, driving, throwing a ball — follows laws first stated systematically by Isaac Newton in his Principia of 1687. His first law says that an object stays at rest or in uniform motion unless a force acts on it. That is why a passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes suddenly: the body tends to keep moving while the bus slows. His second law links force, mass, and acceleration: pushing a heavy object needs more force than pushing a light one to achieve the same acceleration. His third law says that forces come in pairs — when you push on the ground, the ground pushes back, and that is what lets you walk.

Friction is the force that both helps and hinders motion. Without friction between shoes and ground, walking would be impossible; with too much of it, machines waste energy as heat. Engineers spend much of their effort managing friction: tyres are treaded to grip roads, engine parts are lubricated to reduce wear, and brakes deliberately use friction to convert motion into heat and stop a vehicle. Understanding friction explains everyday advice, such as why stopping distances grow sharply on wet roads and why heavy vehicles need longer to stop.

Energy is the capacity to do work, and it appears in different forms: kinetic energy of motion, potential energy stored by height or by a stretched spring, chemical energy in fuel and food, and electrical energy in a current. A central law of physics is that energy is conserved: it changes form but is not created or destroyed in a closed system. A falling ball converts potential energy into kinetic energy; a car's engine converts chemical energy into motion and waste heat. Much of engineering is about improving the fraction of energy that becomes useful work and reducing the fraction lost as heat, sound, or vibration.

These ideas explain common safety rules. A seat belt works by spreading the force of a sudden stop over a longer time and a larger area of the body, reducing the acceleration the body experiences. A crumple zone in a car does the same for the vehicle, absorbing energy by deforming. The physics is unforgiving: doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy, which is why speed limits and braking distances matter far more than intuition suggests.

Key takeaways

  • Newton's three laws (1687) explain inertia, force and acceleration, and action-reaction pairs.
  • Friction both enables walking and grip, and wastes energy as heat in machines.
  • Energy is conserved but changes form; doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy, which is why stopping distances matter.

Why it matters for civil studies

It underpins questions on transport safety, energy efficiency, and why engineering trade-offs are unavoidable.

Think it through

Why does a car need a much longer distance to stop at 100 km/h than at 50 km/h, even though the speed has only doubled?
