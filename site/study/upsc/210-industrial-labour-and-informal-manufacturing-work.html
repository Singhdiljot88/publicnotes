UPSC foundations · Note 210

Industrial labour and informal manufacturing work

Industry · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Effects of liberalisation on the economy, changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth; infrastructure: Energy, Ports, Roads, Airports, Railways; investment models.

Much of India's manufacturing happens outside large registered factories: in workshops, home-based units, repair shops and small clusters where workers may have no written contract, paid leave or social security. This informal manufacturing is not marginal; it supplies parts, garments, food products and services to the formal economy, and it employs workers who move between agriculture, construction and factory work as seasons and orders change.

Labour law has traditionally been a thicket of separate Acts for wages, industrial relations, social security and safety. Parliament passed four labour codes in 2019 and 2020, the Code on Wages, the Industrial Relations Code, the Code on Social Security and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, consolidating many earlier laws. Their commencement has been phased, and their practical effect depends on rules, thresholds and enforcement machinery, which is why the codes are best studied as a framework whose outcomes are still unfolding rather than as a finished settlement.

The codes raise a familiar balance. Employers argue that simpler, consolidated law and more flexible hiring will encourage formal jobs and larger plants. Unions argue that raising thresholds for layoff approval and for union recognition, and extending fixed-term employment, weakens workers' bargaining power. Both sides agree on the underlying problem, that too few workers enjoy security, but they disagree on whether flexibility or protection creates more good jobs.

Social security is the hardest gap. The Code on Social Security provides for gig and platform workers and for unorganised workers, including through welfare funds, but covering workers who change employers frequently and have no single pay record is administratively difficult. Registration drives, portable benefits and contributions from platforms are attempts to solve a record-keeping problem before a benefits problem.

For a civil studies reader, the key distinction is between the formalisation of firms and the protection of workers. A firm can register, pay tax and still employ workers informally; a worker can be counted as employed while lacking any safety net. Policy should be judged on whether it moves both together: more registered firms, and more workers with contracts, safety and social security.

Key takeaways

  • Much manufacturing work is informal, in workshops and home-based units outside large factories.
  • Four labour codes passed in 2019-2020 consolidate earlier wage, relations, safety and social security laws.
  • Formalising firms and protecting workers are different goals, and both must move together.

Why it matters for civil studies

Labour policy connects industrial growth, welfare and federal enforcement, and it rewards careful, non-slogan answers.

Think it through

Does making hiring easier create more formal jobs, or merely make informal work easier to arrange? What evidence would settle it?
