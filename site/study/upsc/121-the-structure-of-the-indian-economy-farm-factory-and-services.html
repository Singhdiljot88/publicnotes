The structure of the Indian economy: farm, factory and services

Indian economy: structure and planning · Reference edition 1 November 2026 · 2 min read

Study context: GS III — Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, development and employment.

India's economy is usually described in three sectors: agriculture and allied activities, industry, and services. The balance between them has changed sharply since independence. In 1950-51 agriculture produced more than half of national output, while services produced about a third. By 2023-24, according to the Economic Survey 2023-24, the shares of gross value added at current prices were 17.7 per cent for agriculture, 27.6 per cent for industry and 54.7 per cent for services. India therefore looks, on output, like a services-led economy.

Employment tells a different story. The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) for 2023-24 found agriculture still employing about 46 per cent of workers, up from about 44 per cent in 2017-18. So nearly half the workforce produces less than a fifth of output, which means labour productivity in farming is far below the rest of the economy. This gap between output shares and employment shares is the single most important structural fact about the Indian economy: it explains low farm incomes, disguised unemployment in villages, and the pressure to create non-farm jobs.

Within services, the picture is also uneven. Trade, hotels, transport and communication employ large numbers, while finance, real estate, professional services and information technology produce much of the value but employ relatively few highly skilled workers. Industry includes manufacturing, mining, construction and utilities; construction has been a major absorber of rural workers moving out of farming, though often in informal, insecure jobs.

Economists describe this pattern as a partial structural transformation. Unlike the classic path, where workers moved from farms to factories and then to services, India moved from farms directly to services in output terms, while factory employment stayed modest. The policy question that follows is not whether services matter — they plainly do — but how manufacturing, agro-processing and labour-intensive services can absorb workers at better wages.

Key takeaways

  • Output has shifted decisively to services, but employment has not: agriculture still employs about 46 per cent of workers.
  • The gap between agriculture's output share (17.7 per cent) and its employment share is the core structural problem.
  • India's transformation skipped the usual factory stage, leaving job creation as the central challenge.

Why it matters for civil studies

Sector shares link growth, employment, farm incomes and regional inequality in one frame.

Think it through

Should India aim to raise manufacturing employment even if services grow faster, and what would that cost?
